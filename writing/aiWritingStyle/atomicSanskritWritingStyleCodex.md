# Atomic Sanskrit Writing Style

This guide records the prose style Parag Tope expects while drafting and revising *Atomic Sanskrit*. It supplements the broader authorial profile in `claudeSummary.md`. Before editing manuscript prose, read this file and follow it unless Parag gives a more specific instruction for the passage under review.

## Governing Rule

Write for an intelligent reader who knows nothing about Sanskrit, respects a clear argument, and should never have to translate an abstract sentence into ordinary English before understanding it.

When revising Parag's prose, preserve his construction, rhythm, repetition, and argumentative force. Change only what is unclear, grammatically incorrect, factually inaccurate, inconsistent with the book's terminology, or contrary to an explicit writing rule.

## Explanation and Argument

### 1. Begin with the concrete evidence

Begin with the Sanskrit form, mantra, figure, example, or procedure. Explain what the reader is looking at and what happens within it. State the architectural conclusion afterward.

Do not begin with an abstract claim and leave the reader waiting for the evidence that gives it meaning.

### 2. Explain one complete step at a time

Do not compress several steps of reasoning into one sentence. If A causes B, and B enables C, explain all three connections. A reader should never have to reconstruct a missing step.

### 3. Teach through examples

Do not write that an architecture "preserves recoverability" and move on. Show a form such as **भू -> भवति**, explain each change, and then state what the example demonstrates.

### 4. Separate evidence, inference, and speculation

State the evidence plainly. Identify the architectural inference that follows from it. When the book speculates, call it speculation and pursue the idea confidently. Do not bury every inference under academic qualifications.

### 5. Connect the larger themes after the demonstration

The radiant, calibrant, and fractal themes summarize concrete evidence. They do not replace the explanation that allows the reader to understand that evidence.

## Sentence Construction

### 6. Use clear subjects performing clear actions

Prefer:

> The Vedas preserve these completed forms.

Avoid:

> The preserved forms establish the architecture.

The first sentence identifies who preserves what. The second makes an abstraction perform the action.

### 7. Prefer subject-first openings

Prefer:

> The first example is the opening mantra of the Ṛgveda.

Avoid:

> The opening mantra of the Ṛgveda supplies the first example.

### 8. Use natural declarative phrasing

Prefer:

> Sanskrit has a twofold purpose.

Avoid:

> Sanskrit's purpose is twofold.

Likewise, prefer "the two domains serve two different purposes" when the word *two* carries part of the intended beat.

### 9. Preserve necessary repetition

Repetition often carries logic, rhythm, or emphasis. Do not delete a repeated word merely because the sentence's technical meaning would survive without it. Do not compress a deliberate beat.

### 10. Vary paragraph length naturally

A conclusion may need one short paragraph. A difficult grammatical operation may need several paragraphs. Do not force paragraphs into a uniform length or repeated pattern.

### 11. Avoid AI-shaped lists

Do not repeatedly stack five or six examples into comma-separated lists. Select the examples that carry the argument, explain them, and vary the rhythm.

## Reader Accessibility

### 12. Explain every technical term

Assume that the reader has never studied Sanskrit. Introduce the Sanskrit term, Devanagari, transliteration, and plain meaning. Then demonstrate how it functions.

The prose must never resemble abbreviated notes written for Sanskrit specialists.

### 13. Prefer plain English

Avoid academic or administrative vocabulary when an ordinary word expresses the point more clearly. Words such as *productive*, *attested*, *operational*, *framework*, *substrate*, and *field* require scrutiny and often need replacement.

### 14. Avoid compressed signposts

Do not use sentences such as:

- "The burden shifts."
- "The architecture becomes visible."
- "The next category arrives."
- "The evidence supplies the answer."
- "The procedure operates at scale."
- "That asymmetry establishes direction."
- "Style is not clock."

State what changes, what the example demonstrates, or what the reader should examine next.

### 15. Do not make abstractions perform human actions

Mantras do not "supply" examples. Categories do not "arrive." Examples do not "ask" questions. Evidence does not "speak" or "perform work." Architecture does not act like a person unless a deliberate and necessary metaphor requires it.

### 16. Avoid legal writing

Do not qualify every sentence against hypothetical objections. Put necessary scholarly limits and source qualifications in endnotes. The body should remain confident, clear, and readable.

### 17. Avoid unnecessary negative contrasts

Do not repeatedly write "This is not merely X; it is Y." State Y directly unless the passage is correcting a specific misconception that the reader has already encountered.

## Voice and Position

### 18. Retain the polemical force

When the book argues that the pyramid concealed evidence, committed category theft, or constructed a fraudulent narrative, do not soften the claim into an assumption, confusion, oversight, or mistake.

### 19. Do not import the pyramid's chronology

Avoid *early*, *late*, *archaic*, *development*, and *innovation* when those words quietly impose a chronology that the chapter has not established. Use them only when the chronology is independently supported or when describing the pyramid's own claim.

### 20. Use the book's terminology consistently

- Use **धातुः (*dhātuḥ*)** and **atom**, not *root*.
- Do not use *stem* when the Sanskrit form can be described directly.
- Use *vaidika* and *laukika* for the two domains.
- Present Pāṇini as the person who made Sanskrit's grammar explicit through unmatched documentation. Do not call him the creator or codifier of Sanskrit.
- Preserve established distinctions such as *drift*, *deviation*, and *divergence*.
- Follow the manuscript's banned-word list and coined terminology.

## Final Revision Test

Before accepting a paragraph, ask:

1. Does the reader know what concrete example or action the paragraph discusses?
2. Is every step in the reasoning stated?
3. Does a person, text, lineage, or identifiable system perform each action?
4. Could any abstract phrase be replaced by ordinary English?
5. Did the revision preserve Parag's rhythm, repetition, and force?
6. Did the revision quietly introduce academic caution, legal phrasing, or the pyramid's chronology?
7. Could an intelligent reader with no Sanskrit training understand the paragraph on the first reading?
