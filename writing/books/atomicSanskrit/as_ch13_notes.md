## 1. Working Thesis

This chapter argues that Sanskrit's preservation across the long span of the civilization is not an accident of cultural conservatism but the result of an engineered, multi-layered redundancy system designed against two distinct threats: the natural entropy that erodes any unanchored linguistic system, and the deliberate adversarial action of those who would destroy the system or capture its institutional layers. The Vedas are the central artifact of this engineering. They function as an immutable civilizational calibration matrix — a mathematically locked acoustic corpus encoding every relevant geometric permutation of the grammar, against which spoken usage can be continuously checked. The metrical structure (छन्दस्) functions as a cryptographic hash that makes any drift instantly detectable. Crucially, the apparatus is *deliberately media-independent*: there is no central archive to destroy, no institutional gatekeeper to capture, no single physical artifact on which the architecture depends. The chapter also confronts the standard claim that observable differences between Vedic and Classical Sanskrit prove organic evolution, and argues instead that those differences prove the opposite: a successful deployment of an engineered fail-safe against entropy. Finally, the chapter recasts Pāṇini not as the inventor of Sanskrit but as the most accomplished of a series of reverse-engineers (the Prātiśākhyas preceded him) who systematically articulated the structural laws of an architecture they had inherited.

## 2. Detailed Outline

### 2.1 Opening: redundancy as a feature, not a flaw

Modern engineering recognizes that any deployed system intended for long-duration operation in a noisy environment must be designed with redundancy. Critical structures fail when they have only one path; resilient systems are built with multiple redundant paths that cross-correct. Sanskrit's preservation system is not a sentimental cultural artifact — it is engineered redundancy, executed at multiple layers simultaneously, each layer cross-correcting the others.

### 2.2 The preservation apparatus

**The narrow apparatus.** Six layers of linguistic engineering, each operating on a different aspect of the system:

- **वेदाः (Vedāḥ)** — the calibration matrix. An immutable acoustic reference embedding the entire grammatical physics of the language. The Vedas are not primarily religious texts; they are primarily *engineered preservation artifacts* whose content happens to be sacred.

- **प्रातिशाख्य (Prātiśākhya) texts** — recension-specific rule codifications that explicitly extract the sandhi, accent, and phonetic rules implicit in particular Vedic śākhās. The original term was *pārṣada* — *belonging to a parisad*, a community of reciters — which makes the technical posture explicit: each text is the codified product of a specific recitational community working on its specific dataset.

  The five surviving major texts are the **Ṛk-Prātiśākhya** (attributed to Śaunaka, for the Rigveda), the **Taittirīya Prātiśākhya** (for the Taittirīya recension of the Krishna Yajurveda; phonetically the most advanced and the only one that works directly from continuous text rather than isolated words), the **Vājasaneyī Prātiśākhya** (attributed to a pre-Pāṇinian Kātyāyana, for the Śukla Yajurveda; closest to Pāṇini in technical terminology), the **Atharvaveda Prātiśākhya** (also called *Caturādhyāyikā* and in some manuscripts *Kautsa-vyākaraṇa*), and the **Sāmavedaprātiśākhya** (also called *Ṛk-tantra*, ascribed to Śākaṭāyana or Audavraji).

  Each Prātiśākhya works the same operational pipeline: take the pada-pāṭha as the *prakṛti* (the basic, analyzed form), and derive the saṃhitā-pāṭha and the various *vikṛti*-pāṭhas as transformations of it. The technique is *substitution-based*: rules specify which form replaces which under which conditions. Cardona has noted that this substitution methodology is *older than Pāṇini's*. Pāṇini did not invent the analytic technique that makes the Aṣṭādhyāyī function. He inherited it from this tradition and refined it.

  The recension-geographic correspondence is direct evidence that the engineering had downstream effects on regional speech. Because each recension dominates a particular region, the Prātiśākhya for that recension shapes regional pronunciation. The clearest surviving instance: in Bengal, where the Śukla Yajurveda is the dominant tradition, the non-distinction between *va* and *ba* (codified in the Vājasaneyī tradition) persists in everyday Bengali pronunciation to this day. The Prātiśākhyas are not merely preservation devices for Vedic recitation; they have left measurable, observable signatures in the spoken languages of the regions where their associated śākhās were dominant.

  Together, the Prātiśākhyas constitute the first explicit reverse-engineering of the Vedic dataset, predating Pāṇini's unified treatment by generations.

- **व्याकरणम् (Vyākaraṇam)** — Pāṇinian grammar. Unification across recensions into a single generalized rule system.

- **धातुपाठ (Dhātupāṭha)** — preserves the inventory of semantic atoms.

- **वर्णमाला (Varṇamālā)** — preserves the sound-field at the level of phonetic structure.

- **छन्दस् (Chhandas)** — meter. Preserves syllabic count, rhythm, and metrical pattern integrity. Functions as the cryptographic hash. Any altered valency breaks the meter and is instantly detected.

These six layers are the focus of this chapter because they are where linguistic engineering is most explicitly visible.

**The expanded apparatus: the vedāṅga frame.** The Indian tradition itself wraps a broader preservation apparatus around the Vedas. The six वेदाङ्गानि (vedāṅgāni) — *limbs of the Veda* — are:

- **शिक्षा (śikṣā)** — phonetics. Overlaps with the *varṇamālā* layer in the narrow apparatus.
- **व्याकरणम् (vyākaraṇam)** — grammar. Same as the narrow apparatus.
- **छन्दस् (chandas)** — meter. Same as the narrow apparatus.
- **निरुक्तम् (niruktam)** — etymology and semantic explanation.
- **कल्पः (kalpaḥ)** — ritual procedure: the operational context within which Vedic recitation occurs.
- **ज्योतिषम् (jyotiṣam)** — calendrical and astral timing: the temporal coordinates within which ritual recitation is properly performed.

Three of the six vedāṅgas (śikṣā, vyākaraṇam, chandas) overlap directly with the narrow apparatus. The remaining three extend the preservation system beyond the language proper — to semantic interpretation (niruktam), to operational ritual context (kalpaḥ), and to temporal coordination (jyotiṣam).

The narrow-versus-expanded distinction matters for two reasons. First, it situates the narrow apparatus within the tradition's own taxonomy. The engineering claim is not a retrospective imposition; it aligns with how the civilization itself organized its preservation work. Second, it provides a structural bridge to the Epilogue's larger civilizational claim: the engineering posture observable in the linguistic substrate is not confined to language alone. The vedāṅga frame is the tradition's own evidence that preservation was understood as a multi-domain enterprise. The Epilogue picks this up; the *Second Shānti* book picks it up further.

This chapter confines itself to the narrow apparatus because linguistic engineering is the most demonstrable case. The broader vedāṅga architecture is named here as the bridge.

### 2.3 The Vedas as calibration matrix

The architects deliberately encoded every relevant grammatical permutation into a fixed acoustic corpus. The Vedas function as an incorruptible reference file against which spoken usage can be continuously checked. While spoken Sanskrit experiences the natural friction of human use, the civilization possessed a parallel, locked dataset that did not.

The calibration-matrix concept does work beyond this chapter. The same engineering posture — a stable, anti-entropic reference against which other systems are aligned — applies, structurally, to Sanskrit's role in contact with other languages. Where Chapter 13 names the Vedas as the calibration matrix *internal* to Sanskrit, Chapter 16 deploys the term **calibrant language** for Sanskrit's role in contact with the natural languages of Central and West Asia. The two terms are deliberately paired: *calibration matrix* is the engineering artifact internal to the language; *calibrant* is the language itself functioning as a stable reference against which other linguistic systems align. The cross-chapter unity is intentional and load-bearing for the book's larger argument.

### 2.4 Meter as thermodynamic constraint

Sanskrit *padāni* are fully saturated molecular units (Chapter 12). Meaning is carried by saturation, not by syntactic word order. This saturation creates syntactic fluidity: words can be reordered freely without semantic loss. Free word order in turn allows arrangement by acoustic weight and timing — enabling the precise metrical architecture of Vedic verse.

Once locked into meter, any altered valency breaks the metrical pattern. The break is instantly perceptible to the distributed oral network. **विसर्गः (visargaḥ)** regulates breath flow within the metrical structure, providing an additional physical constraint.

### 2.5 The two threats: entropy and centralized capture

The chapter has so far described how the apparatus resists one threat: the natural decay any deployed linguistic system suffers as it passes through countless mouths and minds across countless generations. *Apabhraṃśa* — falling away from the standard — is a permanent feature of unanchored speech, and the calibration matrix together with the surrounding apparatus is calibrated to detect and correct it.

But the apparatus is designed against a second threat as well, and this second threat is what gives the system its most distinctive engineering signature.

The second threat is *centralized capture*. A preservation system that depends on a central archive can be destroyed. A system that depends on institutional gatekeepers can be infiltrated, redirected, or shut down. A system whose authority is consolidated in a small number of hands can be captured by anyone who controls those hands. The Vedic apparatus was engineered against this threat too, and the engineering response is what makes the architecture distinctive from any other ancient preservation effort.

The Vedic apparatus is *deliberately media-independent*. There is no central archive whose destruction would erase the corpus. There is no institutional headquarters whose capture would compromise the system. There is no single physical artifact on which the architecture depends. Preservation runs on countless individually trained reciters distributed across geography, lineage, and generation — reciters drilled from childhood in error-detecting recitation techniques whose function is to make corruption locally detectable without reference to any central authority.

The two threats require different engineering responses, and the apparatus delivers both:

- **Against entropy:** layered redundancy, cross-correcting layers, the metrical hash, the eleven *pāṭhas* that re-encode the text under different combinatorial constraints (Chapter 14).
- **Against centralized capture:** distribution, media-independence, the absence of a central authority. The corpus exists in the network, not in any single node.

The architecture has been tested under both threats. Entropy has worked on it continuously for as long as the civilization has existed. Centralized capture has been attempted multiple times — most recently, and most thoroughly, during the colonial period — and the architecture has held under both pressures because the engineering anticipates both. (See section 4.4 for the colonial test case.)

### 2.6 The Vedic-to-Classical transition reframed

Standard academic claim: observable differences between Vedic and Classical Sanskrit prove the language evolved. The reframing: those differences prove the engineering succeeded. The Vedas held; the spoken register experienced predictable friction; the calibration matrix preserved the architecture across both registers. The transition is not evidence of biological evolution. It is evidence of a deployed fail-safe operating exactly as designed.

### 2.7 Vedic as register, not evolutionary stage — defending the reframing

This is the chapter's most contested claim and must be defended carefully. The defense rests on the mechanics of functional architecture, not on competing claims about historical dating. We do not need to prove that the Ṛgveda and the Mahābhāṣya were composed at the same time. We only need to demonstrate that the structural differences between the Vedic and Classical registers are driven by operational purpose, not by linguistic decay.

**The standard academic position.** Vedic is an earlier evolutionary stage of Sanskrit. Features such as the subjunctive mood, the fuller udātta/anudātta/svarita accent system, and certain infinitive forms are typically interpreted as Indo-European archaisms preserved in Vedic and simplified or lost in Classical.

**Internal Indic evidence for the register interpretation.**

The strongest internal evidence is the apparatus of Pāṇini's grammar itself. The Aṣṭādhyāyī distinguishes two operational domains — *chandasi* (in the Vedic register) and *bhāṣāyām* (in the standardized spoken register) — and treats them as parallel rule sets within a single grammatical apparatus, not as historical stages. The diachronic interpretation that reads *chandasi* forms as "older" is an interpretive overlay that later scholarship has imposed on what is, in the source text itself, a synchronic distinction. If Pāṇini had treated Vedic as a previous evolutionary form, the structure of his grammatical machinery would not be set up the way it is.

Further internal evidence:
- Diglossia between high and low registers is the rule in literate civilizations, not the exception (Latin/Romance, Classical Arabic/colloquial, Classical Chinese/vernacular, Sanskrit/Prākrit). The phenomenon is named and well-established in modern linguistics. Applying diglossia to the Vedic/Classical distinction does not require any new theoretical apparatus; it requires only that we read Sanskrit by the standards already used to read other literate civilizations.
- Using comparative Indo-European evidence to argue for organic evolution is circular: it presupposes the genealogical framework the engineering thesis is asking us to set aside.

**What the strongest register claim cannot defend on its own.**

- Vedic shows internal layering across Ṛgvedic, late Vedic, and Brāhmaṇa registers. A purely frozen liturgical register would not show this.
- Some Vedic features (subjunctive, injunctive, fuller accent) have at first glance the appearance of genuine archaisms, with internal Indic evidence (Kiparsky on accent patterns) as well as comparative evidence.

**Specific cases the engineering framework handles well.**

The two cases most often cited against the register interpretation — pitch accent and morphological redundancy — both turn out to be evidence *for* the framework once examined operationally rather than diachronically. The strategy is not to deny that these features differ between Vedic and Classical, but to reframe the differences in terms of operational purpose.

*Pitch accent.* The Vedic pitch accent system (udātta, anudātta, svarita) is not phonetic ornament. It is structurally integral to the recitation system. The pāṭha grids — krama, jaṭā, ghana, and the rest (Chapter 14) — depend on accent as one of the dimensions along which corruption can be detected. A syllable with the wrong pitch is as detectable as a syllable with the wrong vowel. Pitch accent is therefore part of the engineering of the corpus's preservation. Its absence in the Classical register is not phonetic erosion. It is functional appropriateness: the Classical register's operational requirement is discursive composition, not the preservation of a fixed acoustic corpus, and discursive composition does not need the error-detection apparatus that recitation depends on. The standard argument that Classical "lost" pitch accent assumes that pitch accent is a default feature whose absence requires explanation. The engineering framework reverses this assumption: pitch accent is functional in the register that requires it, and absent from the register that does not.

*Morphological redundancy.* Vedic Sanskrit contains multiple variants for the same grammatical function. The instrumental plural ends in either -ebhiḥ or -aiḥ. The nominative plural ends in either -āsaḥ or -āḥ. The standard argument treats this multiplicity as evidence that Vedic was a "primitive, unsettled" language whose forms had not yet stabilized, with Classical as the "crystallized" end-state of that stabilization. The engineering framework reframes this. The Vedic register is composed in fixed meters that require precise syllabic counts: gāyatrī, triṣṭubh, anuṣṭubh, and the rest. A poet working within meter needs alternative forms of the same grammatical content with different syllabic weights, so that verse can be made to scan correctly. The variant endings are engineered for metrical fit. The Classical register, by contrast, is composed in prose for discursive purposes. Metrical fit is not its operational requirement, and the alternative forms collapse to a single standardized form because the operational pressure that supported them is no longer present in this register. The standardization of Classical is not evolutionary crystallization. It is the appropriate streamlining of a register that does not need metrical flexibility.

In both cases, the move is the same. Where orthodox philology says, *"Vedic lost this feature over time,"* the engineering framework replies, *"the system did not require this feature in the operational environment in which it became standard."* The differences are owned, not denied; but their explanation runs through operational purpose rather than through decay.

**The defensible synthesis.** Vedic and Classical are two operational registers within a single engineered system. The Vedic register preserves features that the Classical register does not require — some of those features are register-specific conventions (metrical, ritual, formulaic), some are genuinely older forms anchored to the corpus and protected by the calibration matrix, and the difference between the two registers reflects the difference in operational requirements between recitation-of-fixed-corpus and discursive composition. Differences between the registers reflect (a) deliberate register conventions, (b) preserved archaisms anchored by the corpus, and (c) friction in the unanchored spoken register — not organic evolution from one register to the other.

**Reframing the standard argument.** In an engineered, anti-entropic system, the default state is preservation. The persistence of Vedic features across the long span is not evidence the language evolved. It is evidence the calibration matrix worked. And where features do differ between registers, the productive question is not "what changed?" but "what does each register require?"

### 2.8 Pāṇini and the reverse-engineering tradition

Pāṇini did not invent Sanskrit. The work of reverse-engineering the Vedic dataset was already underway in the recension-specific Prātiśākhya tradition. Each Prātiśākhya extracted explicit rules from one Vedic śākhā. Pāṇini's contribution was unification: across recensions, into a single generalized system, articulating the structural laws of the entire architecture in their most general form. Kātyāyana, Patañjali, and the broader grammatical tradition extended this reverse-engineering work. The grammarians are not the architects. They are the most accomplished documenters of an architecture they had inherited.

### 2.9 Mutually correcting layers

The layers do not simply coexist. They actively cross-check one another. Drift in one layer is detected by another. *Apabhraṃśa* is recognized as a permanent threat. The system is built to continually correct it.

### 3.1 Primary Sanskrit sources

- **Ṛk-Prātiśākhya (Śaunaka)** — primary Prātiśākhya text; ancient and authoritative. Also called *Śikṣāśāstra* and *Pārṣada*. Includes a final chapter on Vedic chandas reportedly more elaborate than Piṅgala's *Chandas-sūtra*. Recognizes seven distinct varieties of *svarita* accent. Hartmut Scharfe noted that the Prātiśākhya texts have undergone revision and post-Pāṇinian interpolation, so individual sūtras cannot be cleanly assigned to a single stratum of the tradition. Identify specific sūtras that demonstrate explicit rule-extraction from Vedic recension.

- **Vājasaneyī Prātiśākhya** (attributed to a pre-Pāṇinian Kātyāyana, distinct from the later Kātyāyana of the vārttikas) — closest to Pāṇini in technical terminology. Many Pāṇinian terms — *udātta, svarita, āmredita, lopa, tiṅ, āñ, luk, lup* — already appear here. At least one paribhāṣā is essentially shared verbatim: *tasminniti nirdiṣṭe pūrvasya* at VP I.134 corresponds to Aṣṭādhyāyī I.1.67. This is the single strongest piece of evidence that Pāṇini inherited terminology and meta-rules from a specific Prātiśākhya lineage rather than developing them independently.

- **Taittirīya Prātiśākhya** — phonetically the most advanced of the surviving Prātiśākhyas; the only one that works directly from continuous text rather than isolated-word recitation. Per Siddheswar Varma, it and the Ṛk-Prātiśākhya appear to be the oldest. The Taittirīya's distinctive methodology may itself be evidence of developmental refinement *within* the Prātiśākhya tradition (see Open Questions 5.7).

- **Atharvaveda Prātiśākhya** (*Caturādhyāyikā*, also *Kautsa-vyākaraṇa* in some manuscripts) — for the Atharvaveda. Sometimes ascribed to Śaunaka.

- **Sāmavedaprātiśākhya** (*Ṛk-tantra*, sometimes *Ṛk-tantra-vyākaraṇa*; ascribed to Śākaṭāyana or Audavraji) — for the Sāmaveda.

- **General methodological note for the Prātiśākhya group:** Cardona observed that the *substitution-based morphophonemic technique* used throughout the Prātiśākhyas is older than Pāṇini's substitution method. The analytic apparatus that makes the Aṣṭādhyāyī function — substituting one form for another according to precisely specified contexts — is itself a development within the Prātiśākhya tradition that Pāṇini inherited and refined. This is significant for the chapter's argument: Pāṇini's analytic toolkit was not invented by him; it was already mature in the tradition that produced the Prātiśākhyas.

- **Sarvasammataśikṣā** — relevant to the authority hierarchy. States that where Śikṣā and Prātiśākhya conflict, Prātiśākhya prevails ("the deer is weaker than the lion"). The tradition itself recognized Prātiśākhya as authoritative for the recensions it covered.

- **Patañjali, Mahābhāṣya** — for *siddhe śabdārthasambandhe*; for the *gauḥ* example; for the recognition of *apabhraṃśa*. (Already cited in Chapters 3, 4. Verify exact references.)

- **Pāṇini, Aṣṭādhyāyī** — for the *chandasi* rules. Identify a specific cluster of Vedic-specific rules to cite as illustration.

## 5. Open Questions

### 5.1 The Vedic accent question

Kiparsky's argument that Pāṇini's accent system represents an older stratum than the Prātiśākhyas preserve is significant. If true, it means Pāṇini is anchored to a *deeper* layer of the Vedic corpus than the recension-specific Prātiśākhyas were. This strengthens the engineering thesis. But it is contested. Read Kiparsky's original argument and the responses; cite carefully if used.

### 5.2 The eleven *pāṭhas* — engineering function

How exactly do *jaṭā*, *ghana*, *krama*, etc. each function as error-detection mechanisms? Need a concrete example showing how a corruption in *saṃhitā-pāṭha* would be exposed by *krama-pāṭha*. This belongs primarily in Chapter 14 but the technique should be flagged here.

### 5.3 The geographical distribution of Vedic recensions

Do the Prātiśākhyas of geographically distant regions preserve identical Vedic content with only register-specific differences? If yes, this is empirical evidence of the calibration matrix's success. Sources to consult: Witzel on Vedic śākhās.

### 5.4 The Avestan-Vedic relationship

How to handle the obvious objection that Avestan looks like a sister language to Vedic with shared features suggesting common ancestry? The engineering thesis can answer (Vedic-bearing communities had contact with Iranian-bearing communities; shared features result from interaction, not common descent), but the answer needs to be made explicit. Connects to the Life After PIE section in Chapter 16.

### 5.5 The synchronic-vs-diachronic reading of Pāṇini's apparatus

The chapter argues that *chandasi* and *bhāṣāyām* are parallel operational domains, not historical stages, and that the diachronic reading is an interpretive overlay. This is defensible but is itself an interpretation. Pāṇini does not say in his grammar "these are two simultaneously available registers." We are reading the apparatus a specific way — though the synchronic reading is the most natural reading of the apparatus once you accept the engineering framework. The chapter should acknowledge this honestly rather than claim the synchronic reading is forced by the text.

### 5.6 The agency question

The pitch-accent and morphological-redundancy reframes can sound, in their stronger phrasing, as if some named architect made a decision to redesign the language for its new operational environment. Avoid this. The defensible version is that the system as a whole exhibits two operational modes calibrated for their respective functions. Whether that calibration was the work of identifiable architects, anonymous tradition, or gradual stabilization constrained by the engineering framework is a separate question and probably belongs to the architects-vs-documenters discussion in the Preface and Introduction.

### 5.7 Developmental refinement within the Prātiśākhya tradition

The Taittirīya Prātiśākhya is the only Prātiśākhya that works directly from continuous text rather than from isolated-word recitation, and is generally regarded as the most phonetically advanced of the surviving texts. This raises a productive question: is the Taittirīya's distinctive methodology evidence of *internal developmental refinement* within the Prātiśākhya tradition? If so, the picture is even stronger than the chapter currently presents. The reverse-engineering posture would not just predate Pāṇini; it would have its own internal history of methodological maturation, with the Taittirīya representing a more advanced stage. This would mean that by the time Pāṇini arrives, he is working in a tradition that has already iterated through multiple methodological generations of explicit rule-extraction. Worth investigating; cite Hartmut Scharfe and Siddheswar Varma if their assessments support this picture.

## 7. Parking Lot

- The phrase "incorruptible reference file" might be too computational for the chapter's tone. Alternatives: "anchored reference," "uncorrupted master copy," "fixed calibration corpus."
- A useful analogy to NIST atomic clocks: a single master reference against which thousands of working clocks are calibrated. May or may not work for the audience.
- *Daśagrantha* brahmins recite the Aṣṭādhyāyī alongside the Vedas as a sacred text. The tradition itself recognized that Pāṇini's grammar belongs to the same preservation layer as the Vedas. Where to place this — Chapter 13 or Chapter 14?
- Consider whether to introduce the cryptographic-hash terminology earlier in the book (Chapter 6 or 11) so the reader is prepared for it here. Or whether it should arrive only here, fully developed.
- The Vedic-Classical question may deserve its own short chapter rather than being a section in Chapter 13. Reconsider once the chapter is drafted and we can see whether the section overpowers the rest.
- **Strategic posture for the register-vs-stage section:** do not deny that Vedic and Classical forms differ. Own the differences. Reframe them. Every place the orthodox view says "Vedic lost this feature over time," the chapter says "the system did not require this feature in the operational environment in which Classical became standard." This posture should be consistent across all the specific cases the chapter takes on.
- **Concrete anchor — the Bengali *va*/*ba* observation.** When drafting the section on Prātiśākhyas and their downstream effects on regional speech, this is the most empirically vivid example. The Vājasaneyī Prātiśākhya codifies the non-distinction between *va* and *ba* in the Śukla Yajurveda tradition. The Śukla Yajurveda is the dominant Vedic recension in Bengal. To this day, Bengali everyday speech preserves that non-distinction — speakers will pronounce both letters as something close to *b*, and write/read with the substitution as a normal feature of the language. This is direct, observable, present-day evidence that the Prātiśākhyas did not merely preserve Vedic recitation; they shaped the spoken languages of the regions where their recensions were dominant. A precision-engineered linguistic technology, deployed at the recension level, has produced measurable downstream effects that persist into modern speech. Use this example when the chapter needs a concrete anchor for the abstract claim about engineering having downstream linguistic effects.
- The cross-chapter pairing of *calibration matrix* (this chapter) and *calibrant language* (Chapter 16) is intentional. The two terms together carry the engineering claim from the language's internal preservation system to its external propagation behavior. If the terminology gets refactored later, refactor both terms together; do not let the pairing drift.
