# Preface — Beyond the Red Lotus

After writing *Tatya Tope's Operation Red Lotus*, I wanted to understand what our ancestors had fought to protect. Freedom would allow Indians to establish **स्वराज्य (*svarājya*)**, self-rule. But what would that mean? A less oppressive version of British or Islamic rule, with Indians occupying the same positions of authority? Or a fundamentally different relationship between government and society?

Most explanations of public order begin with government and the constraints it imposes. Institutions make rules, enforce them, and punish disobedience. Citizens may choose their government and limit its powers, but the state still stands above the society it governs. This book calls that arrangement the **pyramid**. Fear and punishment are its currency of order.

I believe most people are capable of cooperation and restraint. They want to care for those around them and pursue their lives without harming others. Some will deceive, exploit, or attack. Must their conduct determine the architecture under which everyone else lives? **The pyramid treats everyone as an exception waiting to happen.** A system built on suspicion also teaches people to suspect one another.

Could society strengthen people's capacity for restraint and address harmful conduct when it occurs? I had been hearing an answer all my life, without recognizing its significance. Sanskrit kept an exact language available across generations without giving a ruler ownership of its standard. Hindu polity had been destroyed and its policies erased, but something of its architecture remained alive in our heads, our hearts, and our mouths.

*Atomic Sanskrit* is about the architecture of Sanātan. Sanskrit is the language system through which we can examine it. You do not need to know Sanskrit to follow the argument. Translations, familiar examples, and diagrams explain what the language does and how people keep it unchanged.

## A Correction I Could Check

When I was eight or nine, I mispronounced the end of a verse from the *Bhagavad Gītā*:

> राजा वचनमब्रवीत् ।\
> *rājā vacanam abravīt |*\
> The king spoke these words.[NOTE: bhagavad-gita-1-2-citation]

The word **वचनम् (*vacanam*)** ends in *m*. The next word, **अब्रवीत् (*abravīt*)**, begins with *a*. When recited together, those sounds form **म (*ma*)**. I swallowed the *a* and said *vacanam-bravīt*, leaving seven syllables where the verse required eight.

My mother heard the mistake. She explained that I needed the complete syllable *ma*, not a bare *m*. Restoring the vowel repaired both the joining of the words and the verse's count. She called the joining **सन्धि (*sandhi*)** and showed me other combinations. To a child who loved numbers, the rules looked like arithmetic.

Her correction did not depend on personal preference, regional accent, or our family's habits. Another trained listener could check the same word boundary and the same eight-syllable pattern. I could learn to check them myself and teach someone else. Knowledge of the correction could pass beyond our household without anyone needing my mother's permission to use it.

The Vedas extend this discipline across society. Their exact sounds and forms remain available for comparison. I call such an unchanged reference a **calibrant**. **Calibration** means comparing against it and correcting departures. Teachers and learners can both be corrected because neither owns the standard.

## The Language and the System That Protects It

Engineering Sanskrit's sounds, word-building parts, and grammar was one achievement. Engineering a system that could keep the language unchanged for thousands of years was the greater one. It had to resist two enemies: unintended change, or **entropy**, and deliberate attacks on the people and knowledge that kept it exact. This book calls the latter **asuric** attacks: attempts to conceal knowledge, obstruct its circulation, or bring it under an apex's control.

The Vedas remain exact while people use the same Sanskrit to write new poems, explain discoveries, and describe changing circumstances. Pāṇini documented that language with extraordinary precision. This book challenges the claim that he created its stability by imposing rules on drifting speech.

The Hindu temple gives us a familiar way to understand voluntary correction. A person approaches the **मूर्ति (*mūrti*)** for **दर्शन (*darśana*)**, beholding. Stories about choices, conduct, and consequences give meaning to the form before the seeker. The seeker must exercise **विवेक (*viveka*)**, discernment, and decide how to act. The calibrant remains available; alignment requires the person's own judgment.

Sanskrit lets us examine this responsibility in a precise system: someone learns, someone teaches, and both can check an error. Order grows through what people understand and do together. **Authority imposes constraint. A calibrant enables restraint.**

Four words describe the architecture:

- **Distributed:** teachers and lineages across India, north and south, east and west, share knowledge and the ability to correct errors. No region owns the whole.
- **Radiant:** Sanskrit's sounds, words, and methods travel outward and help others create.
- **Calibrant:** an unchanged standard remains available for checking and correction.
- **Fractal:** the same organizing principles recur at different scales. A compound word can become part of a larger compound, a process called **recursion**. More broadly, the book follows a recurring discipline from sound to word to sentence and preservation: people can identify the parts, understand their combination, and recognize a departure.

## Why the Second Shanti

Hindus often conclude a recitation with **ॐ शान्तिः शान्तिः शान्तिः (*oṃ śāntiḥ śāntiḥ śāntiḥ*)**. The three invocations of quietude are directed toward three distinct domains.

The first concerns oneself. Yoga, meditation, and other practices still teach people to understand their minds and respond to anger, fear, or desire without being ruled by them. The second concerns how people live with one another and other living beings. The third concerns natural and cosmic forces beyond direct human control that affect life.

I interpret the three invocations as a fractal map of the universe. The final volume will explain that interpretation in detail. All volumes of *Second Shanti* concern the second domain: how living beings create order together.

Much of the knowledge of this social order was lost under the Abrahamic pyramids that ruled India over the last millennium. Present-day India does not embody the earlier order in full. The series will develop a polity from **first principles**, beginning with living beings and their responsibilities, rather than assembling a government from historical fragments. Government belongs within that order as its protector, not as the owner of every standard.

Sanskrit gives us an existing system to examine before extending these principles to society and government. The Vedas, recitation, teaching, and correction still connect people across places and generations.

European philology tells another story: an unrecorded, imaginary ancestor called Proto-Indo-European, or **PIE**, followed by incoming “Aryans” who supposedly brought Sanskrit to India. This book argues that the similarities instead record Sanskrit's outward radiance. Migration alone cannot establish who created a language.[NOTE: migration-trap-movement-not-authorship]

The chapters ahead examine Sanskrit's construction and the system that keeps it exact. They also challenge the classifications that conceal that system. The full edition and its reference companion document the evidence in greater detail; this edition keeps the main argument and points to that fuller discussion.
