## 9.1 The Garland Becomes a Grid

Chapter 8 established the subcontinental superset. This chapter follows the Vedic sieve: how Sanskrit selects sounds from that larger inventory as sonances, then promotes reusable sonances to sonomers and assigns each one a grid address.

The chapter's epigraph provides the image that guides this section. It compares the formation of Speech to cleaning grain through a sieve. The wise begin with the sounds the mouth can produce. They refine those sounds with the mind and select the sound-particles that Speech can use reliably. The verb **अक्रत (*akrata*)** states what they did: they *formed* **वाक् (*Vāk*)**, Speech. It is a finite plural verb generated from the dhātu ⟪कृ⟫ (*kṛ*). The वर्णमाला (*varṇamālā*) arranges the selected sonomers in an ordered grid.

The second half adds radiance. Friends recognize friendship in that formed Speech. In separated form, the final pāda says **भद्रा एषां लक्ष्मीः (*bhadrā eṣāṃ lakṣmīḥ*)** is placed **अधि वाचि (*adhi vāci*)** — in Speech: auspicious radiance, the beauty through which order expresses itself. This is the **दिव्यता (*divyatā*)** layer. The verse moves from engineering to radiance.

![Figure 9.1 — The Vedic sieve: sound-grains pass through selection and fall as Devanagari sonomers. The sieve selects; the वर्णमाला (*varṇamālā*) will weave.](figures/mapping_mouth/vedic_sieve_sonomer_garland.svg){#fig:ch9-vedic-sieve-sonomer-garland width=100%}

Pyramids and hilltop cities announce power through height, mass, and command. Hindu sacred architecture often follows another instinct. Its aim is **दिव्यता (*divyatā*)** — radiance, presence, light — more than **भव्यता (*bhavyatā*)**, sheer grandness. The *garbhagṛha* is a perfect example: a small, concentrated chamber where darkness, lamp, threshold, axis, and मूर्ति (mūrti) create presence. The engineering becomes poetic force.

The curated heap then becomes ordered form. Sanskrit's own name for the selected sound-inventory is **वर्णमाला (*varṇamālā*)** — the garland of *varṇas*.

![Figure 9.2 — The वर्णमाला (*varṇamālā*) as the sonomer garland: selected sounds strung as a measured sonomer inventory.](figures/mapping_mouth/varnamala_sonomer_garland.svg){#fig:ch9-sonomer-garland width=100%}

The figure is my modern rendering. The arrangement by place and effort is ancient, and the name वर्णमाला (*varṇamālā*) already calls the inventory a garland. I have not found an earlier rendering that draws the garland as an articulatory mouth-map, with each bead set at its own grid address. I did not invent the order.

I saw the visual form while meditating on the architecture itself, in a state of sustained hyperfocus where the sounds had become positions, weights, colors, and paths — where engineering collided with poetic beauty. That seeing was Vāk’s gift, as is the ability to write this book.

When the sounds are visualized through Sanskrit’s own vocabulary, they arrange themselves as what the name already says: a garland of chosen beads, woven for Vāk herself to wear. I offer it to her, strung. The poetry of the माला (*mālā*) integrates the engineering into beauty.

The word is poetic and precise. A garland differs from a heap. Each bead is chosen, shaped, placed, and strung in an order that can be memorized. The वर्णमाला (*varṇamālā*) does the same with the sonomers selected by the sieve. It is the quintessence of **दिव्यता (*divyatā*)**: engineering made radiant.

The word garland is meaningful because it preserves Sanskrit's own way of viewing this sound-inventory. These are measured sound-particles before they become "letters": **sonomers**. A sonomer is what Sanskrit calls a वर्ण (*varṇa*): a selected, measured, repeatable unit of sound.

The Sanskrit analytical continuum goes one step further. It examines whether an individual वर्ण (*varṇa*) can contribute to meaning instead of serving only as material from which a word is built. It calls this capacity **वर्णशक्ति (*varṇa-śakti*)**, the capacity of a sonomer to carry meaning through its sound. The grid first establishes each sonomer as a stable physical unit. Chapter 10 then examines वर्णशक्ति (*varṇa-śakti*) inside assembled semantic atoms.

> **Sonomers: The Pre-Pāṇinian Sound-Units**
>
> Vedic phonetic disciplines already treat Speech as distinguishable sound-units measured through स्वर (*svara*), मात्रा (*mātrā*), force, continuity, and recitational joining. The ordered inventory later called the वर्णमाला (*varṇamālā*) arranges those units.
>
> The Hindu continuum remembers the Māheśvara-sūtras as the sounds of Śiva's drum, received by Pāṇini. Those sūtras arrange an inventory already in use into a compact grammatical index. Pāṇini did not create the वर्णाः (*varṇāḥ*); his analysis depends on the sonomeric architecture that the sūtras index.[NOTE: pre-panini-pratisakhya-classification]

The figures in this book use grids and hexagons. A wiser age would hear वर्णमाला (*varṇamālā*) and understand that the "beads" are selected sounds. Because the architecture has been hidden for too long, a second visual language becomes necessary: grids for addresses, hexagons for stable units, and matrices for repeated structure.

Both representations articulate the same engineered sound-inventory. Sanskrit's own language presents it as a garland. Engineering language presents it as a grid or matrix.

## 9.2 The Four Divisions

Sanskrit combines its sonomers into an **अक्षरम् (*akṣaram*)**, a stable sound-unit centered on one vowel. An अक्षर (*akṣara*) may consist of a vowel alone, or consonants may bond around that vowel. The वर्णमाला (*varṇamālā*) organizes its sonomers into four main divisions, each with a different role in forming an अक्षर (*akṣara*):

1. **स्वराः (*svarāḥ*)** — the vowels. Each forms the resonant center of the अक्षर (*akṣara*), the nucleus.
2. **स्पर्शाः (*sparśāḥ*)** — the contact sounds, the stops and nasals of the five-by-five matrix. Stops build hard contact; nasals couple the oral and nasal cavities.
3. **अन्तःस्थाः (*antaḥsthāḥ*)** — the between-standing sounds य र ल व. Each moves between vowel and consonant behavior.
4. **ऊष्माणः (*ūṣmāṇaḥ*)** — the heat or friction sounds श ष स ह. Each sustains friction and breath.

The प्रातिशाख्य (*Prātiśākhya*) discipline also recognizes boundary sounds called ***अयोगवाहाः (*ayogavāhāḥ*)***. The most familiar are **अनुस्वार (*anusvāra*)** and **विसर्ग (*visarga*)**.[NOTE: ayogavaha-category-pratisakhya] The विसर्ग (*visarga*) releases breath after a vowel. The अनुस्वार (*anusvāra*) directs nasal resonance toward the contact sound that follows.[NOTE: visarga-anusvara-articulation]

Sanskrit describes every sound through the body that produces it. Five parameters record the physical choices involved.

**स्थान (*sthāna*)** fixes the place — where along the vocal tract the constriction forms. **प्रयत्न (*prayatna*)** sets the effort — the manner of the constriction, full contact or partial. **घोष (*ghoṣa*)** switches the voice — whether the vocal cords vibrate during the sound. **प्राण (*prāṇa*)** controls the breath-pressure from the lungs, toggling between light and heavy release. **अनुनासिक (*anunāsika*)** opens the nasal passage — whether resonance couples into the nose.

Modern speech science extends Sanskrit's anatomical analysis across the sounds of other languages. Its labels differ, but its structure remains nearly identical: place, manner, voicing, breath, and nasal resonance.[NOTE: place-of-articulation-sanskrit-terms]

## 9.3 Every Sound Has an Address

Chapter 8 first located the sounds in broad mouth-zones. Tamil, Toda, and Kurukh together cover 22 of the 23 Sanskrit base cells. Korku, Mundari, and Ho cover 20 of 23.[NOTE: inventory-atlas-coverage-surveys] The remaining gaps lie among retroflex nasals and sibilants inside mouth-zones those languages already use.

The mouth can make contact at many points along a continuous vocal tract. Sanskrit does not retain every possible point as an independent sound. It selects particular combinations of place and effort.

A sound's articulatory coordinates describe where and how the body produces it. Sanskrit gives each selected consonant a stable grid address at the intersection of those coordinates. Together, these addresses form Sanskrit's ***व्यञ्जन (*vyañjana*) address grid***. A fixed address allows a sound to be taught, repeated, and corrected.

Appendix Part 3 §3.8 gives the comparative matrix from which this Sanskrit-only view is extracted.](figures/audiography/sanskrit_extracted_sonomer_grid.svg){#fig:ch9-sanskrit-extracted-sonomer-grid width=100%}

Figure 9.3 removes the surrounding comparison languages and retains only the Sanskrit inventory.[NOTE: varnamala-comparative-sound-inventories] The selected sounds form a deliberate pattern across the vocal tract.

One axis of the address grid records the place of articulation: velar, palatal, retroflex, dental, and labial. Sanskrit labels them **कण्ठ्य (*kaṇṭhya*)**, **तालव्य (*tālavya*)**, **मूर्धन्य (*mūrdhanya*)**, **दन्त्य (*dantya*)**, and **ओष्ठ्य (*oṣṭhya*)**.

![Figure 9.4 — The Mouth and Its Five Places of Articulation. The order follows the shape of the mouth. The series begins at the back and ends at the lips. Retroflex sounds occupy a particularly strong position across the languages of the Indian subcontinent.[NOTE: retroflex-global-distribution] That row later becomes a major piece of evidence against the racial Arya thesis.

Sanskrit turns these places into the horizontal axis of the grid.

## 9.4 How Five Places Become Twenty-Five Sounds

The horizontal axis identifies where the mouth makes contact. The vertical axis records what the body does at that place. Sanskrit applies the same physical controls at each of the five mouth-places.

Consider the first column. **क (*ka*), ख (*kha*), ग (*ga*), घ (*gha*),** and **ङ (*ṅa*)** all use the same mouth-place. Breath distinguishes **क** from **ख**. Vocal-cord vibration distinguishes **क** from **ग**. Heavy breath combined with vocal-cord vibration produces **घ**. Opening the nasal passage produces **ङ**.

The same design repeats at the palatal, retroflex, dental, and labial places. Voice and breath produce four oral contact sounds at each place. Nasal release produces the fifth. Five mouth-places multiplied by five physical settings produce twenty-five ***स्पर्श (*sparśa*)*** sonomers.

![Figure 9.5 — Two Features, Twenty-Five Sounds. Voice and breath run down the left rail; place of articulation runs across the top. Velar (कवर्ग), palatal (चवर्ग), retroflex (टवर्ग), dental (तवर्ग), and labial (पवर्ग) cross voiceless/voiced and light-breath/heavy-breath rows, with a fifth nasal row closing the grid.](figures/mapping_mouth/superset_varga_matrix_feature_rails.svg){#fig:ch9-varga-matrix-feature-rails width=100%}

Students often learn this as a school table. The table records the resulting sounds. The control-panel view identifies the physical settings that produce them.

![Figure 9.6 — Control-panel view of the स्पर्श (*sparśa*) matrix: five places of articulation crossed with breath, vocal-cord vibration, and nasal release.](figures/mapping_mouth/control_panel.svg){#fig:ch9-control-panel width=100%}

The complete matrix combines four physical controls:

1. **Mouth-place** identifies where contact occurs.
2. **Vocal-cord vibration** chooses voiced or voiceless.
3. **Breath pressure** chooses light or heavy release.
4. **Nasal coupling** opens the nasal passage.

The matrix therefore records instructions for the body. A student does not have to memorize twenty-five unrelated sounds. Each sonomer can be produced from its mouth-place, breath setting, vocal-cord setting, and nasal setting. The same address helps a teacher identify and correct an imprecise sound.

A compact set of anatomical controls therefore generates twenty-five distinct and repeatable sonomers.

Those five places also sit far enough apart to stay distinct in the ear. Measured backward from the lips, their anatomical coordinates fall at roughly 0, 3, 7, 9, and 12 centimeters along an adult vocal tract. No two adjacent places are closer than about two centimeters.[NOTE: varnamala-grid-geometry] The tract above the vocal cords works as a filter. Its shape at the moment of contact determines which frequencies the released sound emphasizes. Shifting the contact point reshapes that filter. The distance between the five places therefore contributes directly to the difference the listener hears. Opening the nasal passage adds an acoustic signature that the four oral settings do not produce.[NOTE: formants-source-filter-theory]

Contemporary instruments can image the vocal tract and record the resulting frequencies. They find the five selected places already separated and the nasal row already distinct from the four oral rows. The instruments do not explain how the architecture was created. They confirm the acoustic separation already built into it.[NOTE: architecture-not-analysis-pratisakhya]

## 9.5 Breath as an Axis

Of these physical controls, breath deserves separate attention. Chapter 8 temporarily set aside the ten महाप्राण (*mahāprāṇa*) stops so that it could compare the regional base inventory first. They now return as the heavy-breath half of the contact grid.

The ten heavy-breath stops are:

> ख छ ठ थ फ
> घ झ ढ ध भ

They extend the contact grid vertically. The base mouth-places already existed across the region. Sanskrit adds breath pressure as an independent distinction.

American and British English speakers usually pronounce **pin** closer to **फिन् (*phin*)** than **पिन् (*pin*)** or **फ़िन् (*fin*)**. In **spin**, the puff disappears and the **p** moves closer to **प्**. English therefore uses both releases, but the surrounding sounds decide which one appears. Sanskrit makes breath an independent axis: **प (*pa*)** and **फ (*pha*)** receive different grid addresses and function as independent word-making sonomers.

महाप्राण (*Mahāprāṇa*) demonstrates the design requirement that governs the whole grid: *distinguishability*. Breath creates new contrasts on an independent axis instead of crowding the place axis. The system keeps five clearly separated places of articulation while gaining ten additional stops.

Sanskrit regulates breath beyond the twenty-five contact sonomers as well. Reciters learn to control प्राण (*prāṇa*), give it audible form, and change its release at specified sound-boundaries.[NOTE: mishra-breath-pedagogy]

Beyond the stop matrix, Sanskrit also specifies sounds produced at a boundary between other sounds. The most familiar are **अनुस्वार (*anusvāra*)** and **विसर्ग (*visarga*)**. The अनुस्वार (*anusvāra*) indicates nasal resonance directed toward the following sound. In careful analysis, the next contact determines the precise nasal behavior. The विसर्ग (*visarga*) releases breath after a vowel, and the following sound often shapes that release.[NOTE: sandhi-anusvara-assimilation]

These sounds do not join an अक्षर (*akṣara*) in the same way as an ordinary consonant. The old category name **अयोगवाह (*ayogavāha*)** captures that difference: that which bears without joining in the ordinary way.

A word such as **सिन्धुः (*sindhuḥ*)** ends with a विसर्ग (*visarga*). When that Sanskrit word reaches another language, the receiving form may preserve, replace, or lose the final breath-release. Any proposed connection must account for what happened to it.[NOTE: visarga-cognate-shadow]

Sanskrit therefore does not leave boundary sounds to chance. It identifies them, trains their pronunciation, and specifies the conditions that shape them.

## 9.6 Nuclei, Contacts, and Timing

Sanskrit does not build with isolated sonomers alone. It bonds them around a vowel and forms an **अक्षरम् (*akṣaram*)**, a stable sound-unit that can be recited, counted, recombined, and represented in writing.

The name अक्षर (*akṣara*) means the imperishable or non-decaying. The name describes the role of the unit: it keeps its identity while Sanskrit combines it into larger forms.[NOTE: aksara-imperishable-name]

An अक्षर (*akṣara*) is vowel-centered. One vowel nucleus centers the unit. Consonants can open around it, close around it, or cluster at its edges, but the vowel forms the acoustic center. Thus **पच् (*pac*)** is one अक्षर (*akṣara*), while **पचति (*pacati*)** has three.

The वर्णमाला (*varṇamālā*) is spatial and temporal. The spatial grid tells the body where and how to make contact. The timing grid tells the reciter how long the sound lasts. The unit of duration is the **मात्रा (*mātrā*)**.

![Figure 9.7 — Mātrā Duration. Bar length compares the half-मात्रा (*mātrā*) consonant with the one-, two-, and three-मात्रा (*mātrā*) vowel forms.](figures/mapping_mouth/mapping_mouth_matra_duration_bars_stacked.svg){#fig:ch9-matra-duration-bars width=100%}

Sanskrit measures the duration of every sound through the मात्रा (*mātrā*), and Vedic recitation preserves that timing exactly.[NOTE: hrasva-dirgha-pluta-matra]

## 9.7 The Svara Address Grid

The व्यञ्जन (*vyañjana*) address grid organizes consonants by place and manner. Vowels require different coordinates. The ***स्वर (*svara*) address grid*** organizes them by vowel family, duration, pitch, and nasality.

Most students are first introduced to the Sanskrit vowels as a row of fourteen written forms:

![Figure 9.8 — Fourteen Written Svara Forms. The top row numbers the familiar teaching sequence, the middle row gives its Devanagari forms, and the bottom row gives their IAST transliteration.](figures/mapping_mouth/mapping_mouth_fourteen_written_svara_forms.svg){#fig:ch9-fourteen-written-svara-forms width=100%}

That row helps students learn the script and build a बारहखड़ी (*bārahkhaḍī*). The sound architecture, however, begins with nine ***स्वर-वर्णाः (*svara-varṇāḥ*)***, or vowel families:

> **अ इ उ ऋ ऌ ए ऐ ओ औ**

Duration creates a regular relation within the first four families. **अ/आ, इ/ई, उ/ऊ,** and **ऋ/ॠ** are their one-मात्रा (*mātrā*) and two-मात्रा (*mātrā*) members.

The **ऌ** family has a one-मात्रा (*mātrā*) form and allows प्लुत (*pluta*), but Sanskrit does not use a two-मात्रा (*mātrā*) member in Vedic passages or लौकिक (*laukika*) words. The written **ॡ** completes some teaching rows without functioning as a reusable vowel. The remaining four families, **ए, ऐ, ओ,** and **औ**, use two मात्राः (*mātrāḥ*) in the general Sanskrit inventory and can also be prolonged as प्लुत (*pluta*).

The figure marks forms that Sanskrit does not permit for general reuse. **Restricted** means that Sanskrit uses the form only under stated conditions. **Lineage-Bounded** means that named Vedic lineages preserve it in specific passages. **Excluded** means that the form receives no reusable vowel address. An unmarked form belongs to the general inventory.[NOTE: svara-restricted-and-lineage-bounded-use]

![Figure 9.9 — Svara Duration by Permission. The one-मात्रा (*mātrā*), two-मात्रा (*mātrā*), and three-मात्रा (*mātrā*) columns run against the nine स्वर (*svara*) families. A bare check marks the general inventory; a check in a circle, a form restricted to stated environments; a check in a dashed box, a form the recitation lineages preserve exactly but लौकिक (*laukika*) composition cannot select; a cross, a form Sanskrit does not select.](figures/mapping_mouth/mapping_mouth_svara_permission_rails_single.svg){#fig:ch9-svara-permission-rails width=100%}

The numeral **३** tells the reciter to sustain the vowel for three मात्राः (*mātrāḥ*). Thus **ओ३** is **ओ** extended to three counts. Sanskrit uses these प्लुत (*pluta*) forms only under stated conditions, so the figure marks them **Restricted**. The extra duration does not create another vowel family.

The half-**ए** and half-**ओ** require a different label. The Mahābhāṣya records them in the Sātyamugri and Rāṇāyanīya lineages of the Sāmaveda. Those lineages preserve the sounds in the passages that require them, but Sanskrit does not give them reusable vowel addresses for new लौकिक (*laukika*) composition. The figure therefore marks them **Lineage-Bounded**.[NOTE: vedic-half-e-half-o]

## 9.8 Duration, Pitch, and Nasality

The nine families describe vowel identity. Sanskrit then analyzes how long the vowel lasts, how its pitch moves, and whether the sound passes through the nose.

The Vedas preserve a mathematical and deterministic pitch system: **उदात्त (*udātta*)**, **अनुदात्त (*anudātta*)**, and **स्वरित (*svarita*)**.[NOTE: vedic-svara-system] The pitch follows systematic rules involving word formation, affixes, verbal forms, sentence position, and syntax. Duration tells the listener how long a sound lasts. Pitch adds another audible layer that helps the listener interpret the passage.

A vowel can carry any of the three pitch relations, and it can be oral or ***अनुनासिक (*anunāsika*)***. Each permitted duration therefore has six analytical forms:

> **3 pitch relations × 2 nasal states = 6**

The **अ, इ, उ,** and **ऋ** families each permit three durations. Each duration can carry three pitch relations and two nasal states, giving eighteen sound-forms per family.

The **ऌ** family lacks the regular two-मात्रा (*mātrā*) member. **ए, ऐ, ओ,** and **औ** lack a generally reusable one-मात्रा (*mātrā*) member. Each of these five families therefore permits two durations and has twelve sound-forms:

> **4 × 18 + 5 × 12 = 132**

*Atomic Sanskrit* derives this total by bringing the inherited components into one matrix. The number does not mean that Sanskrit has 132 written vowels or that every combination occurs in a Vedic passage. It counts the sound-forms that the architecture can distinguish when vowel identity, duration, pitch, and nasality are considered separately. One vowel family can therefore carry several kinds of audible information without requiring a different written symbol for every combination.[NOTE: svara-nine-families-132]

Figure 9.10 lays out the complete calculation. Each square represents one combination of vowel family, duration, and pitch. Its two halves represent oral and nasal pronunciation. A check marks a form that Sanskrit permits. A cross marks an Excluded position.

![Figure 9.10 — The Svara Form Matrix. Nine vowel families cross three durations, three pitch relations, and two nasal states. The matrix contains 162 possible half-cells; Sanskrit selects 132 and excludes thirty. The three-मात्रा (*mātrā*) प्लुत (*pluta*) group remains Restricted, while the lineage-bounded half-ए and half-ओ remain outside the regular matrix.](figures/mapping_mouth/svara_form_matrix.svg){#fig:ch9-svara-form-matrix width=100%}

Pitch does not create the excluded duration positions in the table. It can accompany any duration that a family already permits. A short vowel can bear स्वरित (*svarita*); a स्वरित (*svarita*) does not need two मात्राः (*mātrāḥ*). Similarly, प्लुत (*pluta*) extends duration without becoming another vowel family. Ṛgveda 10.129.5 makes the distinction audible through **आसी३त् (*āsī3t*)**, where the numeral marks the three-मात्रा (*mātrā*) duration selected by the passage.[NOTE: vedic-pluta-rv-10-129-5]

### The Vedas as the Pronunciation Calibrant

A society does not require every child to become a Vedic student for the Vedas to serve as its sound calibrant. The recitation lineages preserve the calibrant exactly. Yajñas, weddings, household rites, and other occasions bring that calibrant into the hearing of the wider society.

Children present at these occasions hear Vedic recitation long before they study Sanskrit grammar. Some may later learn the Vedas. Many may not. Whether or not they enter a Vedic lineage, they hear Sanskrit's sounds produced in a living setting. They hear dental and retroflex contact, light and heavy breath, short and long vowels, nasal resonance, and the rise and fall of Vedic pitch.[NOTE: vedic-social-sound-calibrant]

Children learn the sounds of a language by hearing the speech around them and attempting to reproduce it. The ear first becomes familiar with recurring distinctions. When the child imitates those distinctions, the tongue, breath, throat, and lips begin learning the movements that produce them.

Exact recitation requires lineage training, repetition, and correction. Wider social exposure serves another purpose: it keeps the pronunciation calibrant audible beyond the recitation lineage. A child who later studies लौकिक (*laukika*) Sanskrit has already encountered many of its sounds.

The effect remains available to an adult listener. Someone familiar with Sanskrit pronunciation does not merely hear a sequence of sounds during Vedic recitation. The mind can follow what the tongue, breath, throat, palate, and lips must do to produce them. The recitation reconnects each audible sound with the action of the body that creates it.

The Vedas therefore operate as a distributed calibrant. The lineages preserve the exact sound; the wider society continues to hear it. Chapters 11 and 12 show how the words and sentences preserved by the same Vedic calibrant also calibrate लौकिक (*laukika*) grammar.

## 9.9 The Sound Volume

The consonant grid and the vowel row can now be combined into a three-dimensional sound model.

Sanskrit is highly selective about sound. The human mouth can produce far more consonantal sounds than Sanskrit recognizes. Every consonantal sound that Sanskrit's engineering selects is a **sonance**.

Some sonances receive independent addresses in the grid. Speakers can use them freely when they build new words. These addressed sonances are **sonomers**. Sanskrit recognizes other sonances but permits them only under a stated condition or within a named Vedic lineage. These are **off-grid sonances**.

> **Sonances = sonomers + off-grid sonances**

The consonant plane crosses five places of articulation with seven rows, creating thirty-five possible grid addresses. Sanskrit assigns independent addresses to thirty-three sonances: twenty-five स्पर्श (*sparśa*), four अन्तःस्थ (*antaḥstha*), and four ऊष्मन् (*ūṣman*). Those thirty-three addressed sonances are sonomers. Two addresses remain unassigned. Their positions identify sounds that the mouth can produce but Sanskrit does not permit speakers to choose independently when they build words.

The familiar fourteen-form teaching row becomes the third dimension:

> 5 × 7 × 14 = 490 possible consonant-vowel addresses

The two unassigned consonant addresses would each extend through those fourteen written positions. Removing them leaves 462 consonant-vowel addresses. It does not claim that all fourteen forms occur equally often or have the same role.

## 9.10 What Earns a Grid Address

### The Two Excluded Positions

Figure 9.11 contains two grid addresses that Sanskrit leaves unassigned in the independent वर्णमाला (*varṇamālā*). Both positions correspond to sounds that a human mouth can make. The empty addresses therefore test whether Sanskrit selected its sonomers deliberately. If physical possibility were sufficient, both positions would be filled.

The first unassigned address lies at the back of the mouth in the अन्तःस्थ (*antaḥstha*) row. Modern phonetics writes the sound as **[ɰ]**, the voiced velar approximant. To approach it, begin with the voiced friction of **ग़ [ɣ]**. Loosen the contact until the friction disappears while keeping the voice running. The resulting sound comes from the throat region without the lip movement of English *w*. Sanskrit can accommodate the physical location but does not assign the sound an independent sonomer address.

The second unassigned address lies at the lips in the ऊष्मन् (*ūṣman*) row. Start with the **[f]** that begins the English words *phone*, *front*, and *fast*. English **[f]** brings the lower lip toward the upper teeth. Move the upper teeth away while continuing the friction between the two lips, and the sound approaches **[ɸ]**, the voiceless bilabial fricative. Sanskrit does not assign this sound an independent sonomer address either.

### The PASS Test for a Sonomer

For a sound to be promoted to a sonomer and assigned a grid address in the वर्णमाला (*varṇamālā*), it must satisfy these four requirements:

1. The mouth must produce it reliably.
2. It must support the complete vowel teaching series as stable, pronounceable अक्षराणि (*akṣarāṇi*).
3. It must distinguish forms independently rather than arise only under a stated condition.
4. It must complete a recurring operation within Sanskrit's architecture.

Both candidates are pronounceable and can be combined mechanically with the vowel teaching series. Pronounceability is only the first requirement. The other tests examine whether speakers can distinguish the resulting forms during repeated use and whether the candidate completes a recurring Sanskrit operation.

These tests apply a principle that recurs throughout Sanskrit's architecture: the ***Principle of Architectural Selection and Scope (PASS)***. It compares four things:

1. **Contribution:** What does the proposed sound, form, or operation add?
2. **Load:** What duplication, collision, or instability comes with it?
3. **Bounding support:** Does a fixed passage, stated junction, pitch pattern, or another part of the architecture contain that load?
4. **Scope:** Where can the resource be used without creating more confusion than benefit?

The result determines where Sanskrit uses the sound or form. It may enter the reusable architecture, appear only under a stated condition, remain inside a Vedic scope or a named Vedic lineage, or stay outside the independent grid.[NOTE: pass-selection-scope-principle]

The four-part analysis compares contribution with load, identifies any support that can contain the load, and establishes the appropriate scope. The two sound pairs show why physical possibility alone does not earn an independent grid address.](figures/calibration/pass_selection_and_scope.svg){#fig:ch9-pass-selection-and-scope width=100%}

### Snap to Grid at a Sound-Boundary

Some सन्धि (sandhi) operations perform ***snap-to-grid*** at a sound-boundary. They resolve the junction to an established Sanskrit address instead of leaving an intermediate sound whose identity depends entirely on its surroundings.

Consider **तत् + अपि → तदपि (*tat + api → tadapi*)**. The first word ends with the unvoiced dental **त्**. The vowel that follows begins with voice. Sanskrit resolves the junction with **द्**, the voiced sonomer at the same dental place:

> **त् → द्**

The mouth can move naturally from the final consonant into the vowel, but the resulting consonant does not remain somewhere between **त्** and **द्** as a context-dependent variant. It lands on the established address of **द्**. This snap-to-grid operation accommodates movement at a sound-boundary while keeping the finished expression inside the reusable grid.

सन्धि (Sandhi) as a whole is broader. Other operations merge vowels, preserve hiatus, remove a sound, or produce a restricted boundary sound. The snap-to-grid description applies when the junction resolves to an established address.

### Why [ɰ] Stays Outside

Begin with **[ɰ]**. The four occupied grid addresses in the अन्तःस्थ (*antaḥstha*) row arise from four established relationships:

> **इ + अ → य — *i + a → ya***\
> **उ + अ → व — *u + a → va***\
> **ऋ + अ → र — *ṛ + a → ra***\
> **ऌ + अ → ल — *ḷ + a → la***

Approximate IPA equivalents make the movement easier to see:

> **[i] + [a] → [ja]**\
> **[u] + [a] → [ʋa]**\
> **[r̩] + [a] → [ra]**\
> **[l̩] + [a] → [la]**

In each pair, the first vowel moves from the center of a syllable into the consonantal gesture of the same articulatory family. A speaker can pronounce **इ + अ (*i + a*)** separately as **[i.a]**, but an eligible Sanskrit junction draws the sounds together as **य [ja]**. Ṛgveda 10.71.4 provides a familiar example: **अशृणोति + एनाम् (*aśṛṇoti + enām*)** becomes **अशृणोत्येनाम् (*aśṛṇoty enām*)**.

Pāṇini later documented all four relationships in ***इको यणचि (*iko yaṇ aci*)***. Other rules preserve the separation where the transmitted form requires it, so the architecture controls both joining and hiatus.[NOTE: snap-to-grid-pragrihya-exception]

The reason becomes clearer when **[ɰ]** is compared with the four अन्तःस्थ (*antaḥstha*) sonomers already in the grid. Modern phonetics associates **[ɰ]** with the close back unrounded vowel **[ɯ]**. Extending the four Sanskrit relationships would therefore require a fifth vowel and a fifth joining pattern:

> **[ɯ] + [a] → [ɰa]**

Sanskrit has no **[ɯ]** vowel from which that movement could begin. Its कण्ठ्य (*kaṇṭhya*) vowel is **अ (*a*)**, and Sanskrit treats **अ** in a distinctive way. In ordinary pronunciation, short **अ** is **संवृत (*saṃvṛta*)**, contracted, while **आ (*ā*)** is **विवृत (*vivṛta*)**, open. The inventory contains no separate long संवृत (*saṃvṛta*) vowel.

Adding duration reveals two vowel forms that Sanskrit leaves outside its reusable grid. In ordinary Marathi pronunciation of **पोळपाट लाटणं**, the written **आ** in **पाट** and **लाट** lasts roughly one मात्रा (*mātrā*), producing the short open **[a]**. A speaker can also sustain contracted **अ** for two मात्राः (*mātrāḥ*) — written here only as **अऽ** — and produce **[ɐː]**. Both sounds are pronounceable and occur in living Indian speech. Sanskrit nevertheless excludes them as independent reusable vowels.

When two eligible instances of **अ** meet, they join as **आ**:

> **अ + अ → आ — *a + a → ā***

The Pāṇinian explanatory lineage documents how Sanskrit coordinates the two vowels. During a grammatical operation, short **अ** is treated as विवृत (*vivṛta*) so that it can combine with **आ** as a corresponding vowel. The final rule of the अष्टाध्यायी (*Aṣṭādhyāyī*), **अ अ**, restores the संवृत (*saṃvṛta*) short vowel in finished pronunciation.[NOTE: sound-volume-two-open-coordinates]

This treatment is already active in Vedic Sanskrit; Pāṇini documents it. Sanskrit uses the open **आ** for the two-मात्रा (*mātrā*) result, while the theoretical **[ɯ] → [ɰ]** relationship never arises. The sound **[ɰ]** is pronounceable, but it neither completes the Sanskrit vowel operation nor creates an independent contrast that would justify another reusable grid address.

### Why [ɸ] Stays at the Boundary

The second empty address requires a different explanation. **फ [pʰ]** and **[ɸ]** can sound close because both release breath at the lips, although the mouth produces them differently. **फ** closes the lips for **प [p]** and releases महाप्राण (*mahāprāṇa*) when they open. **[ɸ]** keeps the lips slightly apart and sustains friction between them. The complete stop series requires **फ**, which can combine with the vowels and build atoms and words.

The Vedic transmission also preserves the [ɸ]-like articulation. At the opening of Ṛgveda 1.1.2, **अग्निः पूर्वेभिर् (*agniḥ pūrvebhir*)**, the breath of the विसर्ग (*visarga*) moves into the following **प** and may be shaped at the lips into the sound written **ᳶ**: **अग्निᳶ पूर्वेभिर्**. The Vedic phonetic disciplines call this ***उपध्मानीय (*upadhmānīya*)***, and Pāṇini later documented its occurrence in अष्टाध्यायी (*Aṣṭādhyāyī*) 8.3.37.

Sanskrit therefore preserves [ɸ]-like articulation when a stated junction produces it. The reusable grid assigns the nearby independent role to **फ** rather than establishing another consonant beside it.

उपध्मानीय (*Upadhmānīya*) is therefore a Restricted off-grid sonance. Sanskrit recognizes the sound and produces it when विसर्ग (*visarga*) occurs before **प** or **फ**, but it receives no independent grid address. *Jihvāmūlīya* follows the same arrangement near the back of the mouth: विसर्ग (*visarga*) before **क** or **ख** produces it.

The checked inventories of Korku, Mundari, Ho, Tamil, Telugu, and Kannada contain neither **[ɰ]** nor **[ɸ]** as an independent consonant. Chapter 8 surveyed these central and southern languages in greater detail. Their absence reinforces the pattern in the Sanskrit grid: these two positions do not correspond to recurring independent sounds across the region from which Sanskrit selects its inventory.

### What the Vowel Row Excludes

The vowel row contains similar gaps. Each gap separates a sound that can be pronounced from a sound-form that Sanskrit selects for general reuse.

Short **ए** and **ओ** are physically possible. Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Korku, Mundari, and Ho use short *e* or *o*, and the Mahābhāṣya records bounded half-*e* and half-*o* in two Sāmavedic lineages. Sanskrit therefore knew the sounds.

In the general inventory, Sanskrit uses **इ** or **उ** whenever a joining rule requires a short substitute for **ए/ऐ** or **ओ/औ**. Adding short **ए/ओ** would give every speaker two more distinctions to learn and preserve without enabling another distinction needed in general Sanskrit words or rules.

The Sāmavedic forms serve a specific purpose inside inherited passages. Their named lineages preserve the passages exactly, so the sounds remain stable within that boundary. They therefore receive **Lineage-Bounded** scope rather than reusable लौकिक (*laukika*) addresses.

The **ऌ** family presents a different case. Sanskrit uses short **ऌ** and permits प्लुत (*pluta*) where a passage requires prolonged duration, but it does not use a two-मात्रा (*mātrā*) member. The teaching symbol **ॡ** fills the formal position in a complete written row. A position in a teaching row does not by itself give the symbol a reusable vowel address in Vedic or लौकिक (*laukika*) composition.

The **अ/आ** family shows the most distinctive selection. Short **अ** is described as ***संवृत (*saṃvṛta*)***, contracted, while **आ** is ***विवृत (*vivṛta*)***, open. Sanskrit nevertheless treats them as members of one operational family. Ṛgveda 10.129.1 demonstrates that vowel joining: the separated **न । असत् (*na | asat*)** of the पदपाठ (*padapāṭha*) becomes **नासद् (*nāsad*)** in the connected recitation.

> **अ + अ → आ — *a + a → ā***

Sanskrit selects one-मात्रा (*mātrā*) संवृत (*saṃvṛta*) **अ** and two-मात्रा (*mātrā*) विवृत (*vivṛta*) **आ**. That selection excludes two inverse possibilities: a two-मात्रा (*mātrā*) संवृत (*saṃvṛta*) अवर्ण (*avarṇa*) that sustains the contracted quality of **अ**, and a one-मात्रा (*mātrā*) विवृत (*vivṛta*) अवर्ण (*avarṇa*) that preserves the open quality associated with **आ**. The second possibility should not be called "short **आ**," because **आ** names the selected two-मात्रा (*mātrā*) member. Both are physically possible vowel qualities; neither receives an independent reusable Sanskrit address.

Figure 9.13 places these decisions beside one another. Sanskrit selects a particular pairing of vowel quality and duration for **अ/आ, ए,** and **ओ**. It excludes the inverse **अ/आ** pair and does not give one-मात्रा (*mātrā*) **ए/ओ** forms general reuse.

The analytical disciplines preserve the difference between spoken **अ** and **आ** while treating them as one family during rules that join the two vowels. The commentarial lineage explains that short **अ** is treated as विवृत (*vivṛta*) during the joining and restored to संवृत (*saṃvṛta*) in finished pronunciation.[NOTE: svara-avarna-operation]

1. **Included:** The form belongs to Sanskrit's reusable architecture.
2. **Restricted:** The form appears only under stated conditions.
3. **Vaidika:** The form belongs to a stated Vedic scope.
4. **Lineage-Bounded:** The form belongs to a named Vedic lineage.
5. **Excluded:** The sound or duration receives no independent grid address.

Ordinary two-मात्रा (*mātrā*) **ए/ओ** are Included. Half-**ए/ओ** are Lineage-Bounded. *Pluta* is Restricted. A sustained contracted **अ**, a one-मात्रा (*mātrā*) विवृत (*vivṛta*) अवर्ण (*avarṇa*), and an ordinary two-मात्रा (*mātrā*) **ऌ** are Excluded.

### Addressed and Off-Grid Sonances Across Two Domains

Ṛgvedic **ळ [ɭ]** provides a clear comparison. Sanskrit unquestionably uses the sound: the first mantra begins **अग्निमीळे (*agnim īḷe*)**. The *Ṛgveda-Prātiśākhya* explains that intervocalic **ड** becomes **ळ**, and that the corresponding **ढ** becomes **ळ्ह**.[NOTE: agnimile-rigveda-opening] Vedic transmission preserves the resulting sound exactly, while the वर्णमाला (*varṇamālā*) grid does not assign it an independent sonomer address.

Other Indian languages use **ळ** as an independent sound that distinguishes one word from another. The human anatomy is available in both cases. Sanskrit gives **ळ/ळ्ह** a different role in the Ṛgveda. The lineage preserves them as off-grid sonances in the words that require them, but speakers cannot choose them independently when they create new Sanskrit words. Their words, positions, and pronunciation remain fixed inside the received passages.

Promoting **ळ** to an independent sonomer would also require it to bond across the reusable vowel row. **ळृ** exposes the difficulty. As a Marathi speaker, I can pronounce **ळ** and **ऋ**. Yet I cannot join them cleanly without breaking the combination or inserting another sound. The written form is possible, but the resulting sound does not fit the reusable grid with the same clarity as **कृ, तृ,** or **हृ**.

This difficulty does not stand alone. **ळ** already sits close to **ड**, serves a lineage-bounded role, and adds no independent operation needed by the लौकिक (*laukika*) domain. Extending it across the vowel row would add more load than generative benefit.

When speakers create new words in the लौकिक (*laukika*) domain, they can independently choose only the sonances that have grid addresses: the sonomers. A Restricted off-grid sonance can still appear when the sounds around it produce it through a stated rule. The वैदिक (*vaidika*) domain preserves a wider range. A mantra can use an off-grid sonance for its meter, sound pattern, or expression. Once the mantra was seen and entered exact transmission, its words and sounds no longer moved. The off-grid sonance could therefore remain in its fixed position without becoming confused with a neighboring sonomer.

The pyramid recasts this designed difference as linguistic drift from *"Vedic"* to *"Classical."* These examples instead show वैदिक (*vaidika*) and लौकिक (*laukika*) using the same Sanskrit architecture for two different purposes.

The sound architecture contradicts the story that primitive Vedic speech gradually became refined Classical Sanskrit. The वैदिक (*vaidika*) domain preserves a wider range of sonances because the words and sounds of a received passage never change. The लौकिक (*laukika*) domain restricts independent word formation to addressed sonances because speakers can continue creating new words without limit. The wider Vedic range and the tighter लौकिक (*laukika*) selection are two engineered permissions within one architecture.

Appendix Part 8 follows the same division through case forms, verbal forms, उपसर्ग (*upasarga*) placement, pitch, meter, and composition.

Sanskrit therefore engineers the grid through both selection and exclusion.

## 9.11 Why Sanskrit Leaves a Margin

The human mouth can produce many sounds beyond the Sanskrit sonomer grid. Tamil uses an alveolar contact between the dental and retroflex places of articulation, and the retroflex central approximant **ழ் (*ḻ*)**. Sindhi uses implosives. Korku, Mundari, and Ho preserve checked or glottalized articulations in parts of their sound-systems. Contact has also brought labiodental **[f]** into many modern Indian vocabularies. Languages elsewhere use still more possibilities, including clicks and sounds produced deeper in the throat.[NOTE: tamil-alveolar-trill][NOTE: tamil-zha-retroflex-approximant][NOTE: sindhi-implosives-inventory][NOTE: ho-mundari-checked-consonants][NOTE: urdu-persian-arabic-loan-phonemes]

Every additional independent consonant would combine with the vowel row and create another series of अक्षराणि (*akṣarāṇi*). Speakers would have to distinguish that entire series in recitation, use it consistently in new combinations, and preserve it across transmission. Adding one grid address therefore creates obligations throughout the architecture.

Sanskrit assigns some articulations to stated environments, as it does with उपध्मानीय (*upadhmānīya*) and Ṛgvedic **ळ**. Other articulations remain available to the surrounding natural languages, whose sound inventories can change with use and contact. Borrowed words may bring a new sound into everyday speech without retroactively adding that sound to the Sanskrit grid.

The वर्णमाला (*varṇamālā*) leaves unneeded positions empty instead of filling every location the mouth can produce. That restraint keeps every selected grid address audible, teachable, and reusable.

## 9.12 Varṇa Is Not Letter

Readers often call the members of the वर्णमाला (*varṇamālā*) letters because they are first introduced on a page. A written letter, however, is a visible mark or glyph. A वर्ण (*varṇa*) is an action of the body with a defined स्थान (*sthāna*), प्रयत्न (*prayatna*), voice, breath, nasal setting, and duration in मात्रा (*mātrā*). The measured sound exists before any script represents it with a glyph.

A **sonomer** is that measured sound-particle. An **audiograph** is the visible mark that records it. Sanskrit is therefore sonomeric before it is audiographic: the sound architecture exists before a script gives its particles written form.

> **The Aramaic-from-Brāhmī Thesis**
>
> The figures in this chapter are not pictures of Devanāgarī. They describe the sound architecture that Indic scripts represent. A visible symbol may travel. A stroke may be borrowed. A script may absorb graphic influence. But similarity between written shapes cannot explain the sonomeric grid beneath the script. A proposed source must contain the architecture it supposedly produced.
>
> Appendix Part 3 §3.5 applies this test to Brāhmī and Aramaic. An earlier symbol can suggest contact; it cannot explain a sound-grid it does not contain. Shape does not define structure, and chronology does not prove causation.

The वर्णमाला (*varṇamālā*) orders those sounds by the way the mouth produces them. It moves from throat to lip, distinguishes full contact from open tone and turbulent breath, and records short and long duration. Alphabetical order such as *a, b, c* does not display a comparable physical map.

The sound-grid therefore comes first. A script gives that grid a visible interface, just as written digits give the place-value system a visible form. The marks can change without changing the architecture they represent. The infinity glyph `∞` likewise makes the unbounded easier to write; the symbol did not invent the idea of infinity.

When the pyramid files the architecture under its interface and calls it an alphabet, वर्ण (*varṇa*) becomes “letter,” अक्षर (*akṣara*) becomes “syllable-sign,” and the sonomeric grid becomes an ABC. The written marks remain visible while the physical order beneath disappears from view. The interface eclipses the architecture.

## 9.13 The Grid Orders the Garland

The chapter has now assembled the complete sound architecture. The वर्णमाला (*varṇamālā*) selects and orders the sonomers. The अक्षर (*akṣara*) bonds them into stable sound-units. The मात्रा (*mātrā*) specifies their duration. Together, these parts prepare Sanskrit to build semantic atoms.

### The Grid Precedes the Index

The वर्णमाला (*varṇamālā*) comes before the rule-system that indexes it. The **माहेश्वरसूत्राणि (*Māheśvara-sūtrāṇi*)** rearrange an inventory already standing into a compact index for grammar.[NOTE: pre-panini-pratisakhya-classification] The pyramid later reverses that order by presenting Pāṇini as the origin of the architecture he decoded and indexed.

### Preparing the Atom

The consonant's half-मात्रा (*mātrā*) becomes important at the next scale.[NOTE: vyanjana-duration-shiksha] Chapter 10 uses a compact notation for these measured scaffolds. **C** marks a consonant, which takes half a मात्रा (*mātrā*). **V1** marks a one-मात्रा (*mātrā*) vowel, while **V2** marks a two-मात्रा (*mātrā*) vowel. The sequence records the scaffold from left to right.

Consider the धातुः (*dhātuḥ*) **⟪गम्⟫ (*gam*)**. It contains **ग् (*g*) + अ (*a*) + म् (*m*)**. The opening consonant **ग्** takes half a मात्रा (*mātrā*), the vowel **अ** takes one मात्रा (*mātrā*), and the closing consonant **म्** takes another half-मात्रा (*mātrā*). Its scaffold is therefore **C–V1–C**, and its complete duration is 1/2 + 1 + 1/2.

**⟪भू⟫ (*bhū*)** contains **भ् (*bh*) + ऊ (*ū*)**. The consonant takes half a मात्रा (*mātrā*), while the long vowel takes two मात्राः (*mātrāḥ*). Its scaffold is **C–V2**, and its complete duration is 1/2 + 2. **⟪दृश्⟫ (*dṛś*)** contains **द् (*d*) + ऋ (*ṛ*) + श् (*ś*)**. Its two consonants each take half a मात्रा (*mātrā*), while **ऋ** takes one मात्रा (*mātrā*). Its scaffold is **C–V1–C**, and its complete duration is 1/2 + 1 + 1/2. Before the atom contributes meaning, its sonomers already give it a measured duration.

### The First Complete Specification

The वर्णमाला (*varṇamālā*) is Sanskrit's first complete specification. It is compact, ordered, mapped to the body, measured in time, teachable, and stable. Chapter 10 introduces a six-part test through the **सूत्रलक्षणम् (*sūtra-lakṣaṇam*)**. The same six qualities already appear in the sound inventory.

1. **Compact.** The selected set is small enough to learn and teach.
2. **Without waste.** Every class and position has a role.
3. **Unambiguous.** Each sonomer has a place, manner, breath, voice, nasal, and timing profile.
4. **Essence-bearing.** The classes themselves encode operational meaning: vowel, contact, nasal, between-standing, friction, breath-release.
5. **Many-facing.** The same selected set serves recitation, grammar, poetry, mantra, शास्त्र (śāstra), and ordinary speech.
6. **Stable.** The same architecture remains available across transmission, notation, teaching, and rule-making.

The वर्णमाला (*varṇamālā*) is the sonomeric sūtra. Pāṇini's Māheśvara-sūtras make that fact explicit for grammar, but the selected sound-inventory already follows the same discipline. It is small, ordered, recoverable, and powerful.

The chapter's opening mantra described Speech being sifted like grain. Sound is abundant; the sieve selects usable sonomers. The वर्णमाला (*varṇamālā*) arranges those sonomers into an architecture that the body can produce, the ear can distinguish, and the lineage can preserve.

The next mantra describes how Speech was transmitted:

> यज्ञेन वाचः पदवीयमायन्तामन्वविन्दन्नृषिषु प्रविष्टाम् ।\
> तामाभृत्या व्यदधुः पुरुत्रा तां सप्त रेभा अभि सं नवन्ते ॥
>
> *yajñena vācaḥ padavīyam āyan tām anv avindann ṛṣiṣu praviṣṭām |*\
> *tām ābhṛtyā vy adadhuḥ purutrā tāṃ sapta rebhā abhi saṃ navante ||*
>
> Through yajña they followed the path of Speech; they found her entered into the ṛṣis. Bringing her forth, they distributed her widely; the seven singers resounded toward her.[NOTE: rigveda-10-71-3-path-vak]

The verse moves from the path of Speech to its discovery in the ṛṣis and its distribution throughout society. The वर्णमाला (*varṇamālā*) enables that distribution. It gives every selected sound a position and teaches the body how to reproduce it. Transmission lineages preserve the exact sounds, while teaching and public recitation carry them across society.

This completes the first major scale in the fractal sequence. Oṃ compresses the whole instrument into one syllable. The वर्णमाला (*varṇamālā*) expands the instrument into an ordered inventory. The धातुः (*dhātuḥ*) then builds a semantic atom from that inventory and repeats the same discipline at a higher scale.
