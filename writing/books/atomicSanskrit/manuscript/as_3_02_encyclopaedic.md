# Appendix Part 2 — The Encyclopaedic Confirmation

Appendix Part 1 traced the colonial network that carried Sanskrit knowledge to Europe, baked PIE, and returned the reconstruction to India. This appendix follows those categories into independent India and into Deccan College's Sanskrit dictionary.

Sanskrit's preservation architecture assigns different causes to the differences found across its corpus. A designed Vedic form belongs to the Vedic domain. A scribal difference belongs first to the history of a manuscript. अपभ्रंशः (*apabhraṃśa*) identifies a deviation from calibrated Sanskrit. A new word demonstrates the language's generative capacity, while a new use of an established word extends its range of meanings. A historical dictionary can collapse all five into one story of linguistic change.

The Deccan College project has assembled an immense and valuable record. Its imported method misclassifies much of what that record contains.

The Church, Company, and Crown lost their combined political form after independence, but the universities, research institutions, academic chairs, journals, and systems of prestige built under their rule remained. The Indian state inherited those institutions and continued funding projects that used the same philological categories. The *church of progress* preserved the old claim to universal knowledge by replacing theology with certified scholarship.

Academic institutions trained generations of **certified intellectuals** within India and determined which accounts of Sanskrit received prestige and circulation. Their Indian identity gave the colonial categories new public authority. The *Encyclopaedic Dictionary of Sanskrit on Historical Principles* became the leading Indian continuation of that project.

## 2.1 The Institutional Network

The Deccan College dictionary provides one detailed case because its title states the method plainly. The same imported chronology shapes other institutions that classify Indian knowledge. Progressivism, the fourth Abrahamic religion described in Chapter 4, supplies that chronology to critical editions, language surveys, archaeology, and history.

**The critical-edition projects.** The Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute produced the Critical Edition of the *Mahābhārata*. The Oriental Institute at the Maharaja Sayajirao University of Baroda separately produced the Critical Edition of the *Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*.[NOTE: indian-epic-critical-editions] Both projects applied textual criticism developed around manuscript traditions to **स्मृति (*smṛti*)**. A remembered narrative can retain its core while expanding through regional retellings, performance, and use across generations. A method searching for one earliest recoverable text can classify those additions as interpolations before asking what function they serve.

**The Linguistic Survey of India.** The survey classified the languages of the Indian subcontinent through the genealogical families of European philology. It used grammar, morphology, phonology, and vocabulary, but its family tree still placed *"Indo-Aryan"*, *"Dravidian"*, and *"Munda"* in mutually exclusive lines of descent.[NOTE: grierson-lsi-classification] Chapters 8 and 9 show what those divisions obscure: a shared subcontinental sound inventory, widespread retroflex capacity, and closely related choices of articulation. The survey's family labels do not predict where those features occur. Geography predicts their distribution more accurately.

**The Archaeological Survey of India and the history departments.** The ASI and university history departments assign the इतिहास (*Itihāsa*) and the *Vedas* places on a BCE timeline. When their evidence does not yield a date, the event can be demoted to *"mythology"*. The method forces **कालचक्र (*kālacakra*)**, the wheel of time, onto a linear ruler. The Kurukṣetra War must occupy one certified point before the account can be treated as history. The same habit judges the rotational and distributed symmetry of a स्वस्तिक (*swastika*) through the linear geometry of a pyramid.

Each institution preserves useful evidence. The imported method then classifies what it has collected. Epic variants can record how स्मृति (*smṛti*) continued to grow across society. The linguistic survey can document a subcontinental sound architecture instead of forcing every language into a phantom family tree. Archaeology can establish sequences and relationships even when an event carries no secure BCE date.

This appendix concentrates on Deccan College because its documentation is extensive and its institutional choice is explicit. The dictionary shows in detail how a chronology imported under colonial rule continued through an institution funded and administered by independent India.

The contemporary account praises Pāṇini's formal achievement while calling it *codification* (Chapter 1 §1.3). That word places the engineering at Pāṇini and hides the architecture already present in the Vedas. It also treats the प्रातिशाख्य (*Prātiśākhya*), शिक्षा (*Śikṣā*), and पाठ (*pāṭha*) disciplines as later accumulation rather than parts of an engineered preservation system. The Deccan College dictionary carries that denial into post-independence India.

## 2.2 The Choice of 1948

In 1948 — less than a year after independence — **Professor S.M. Katre** at Deccan College conceived the *Encyclopaedic Dictionary of Sanskrit on Historical Principles*. The British institutions that had directed the previous century of institutional Indology no longer governed India. Indian scholars could re-examine the racial Arya thesis, the family-tree division of Indian languages, and the Indo-European reconstruction project using Indian institutions and Indian funding.

Appendix Part 1 traced Deccan College from the Sanskrit पाठशाला (*Pāṭhaśālā*) founded in 1821 with funds redirected from the Peshwa's दक्षिणा (*Dakṣiṇā*) endowment. The institution reopened in 1939 as the Deccan College Post-Graduate and Research Institute. By 1948, no colonial government compelled it to retain the categories under which it had been formed.

The project had access to the analytical disciplines preserved by the Hindu continuum: Pāṇini's grammar, Yāska's **निरुक्त (*Nirukta*)**, the Vedic vocabulary of the **निघण्टु (*Nighaṇṭu*)**, and the **वैदिक (*vaidika*) / लौकिक (*laukika*)** distinction. Pāṇini's documentation also states where particular operations apply. These categories could have defined the project from the beginning.

Instead, Deccan College adopted the *historical principles* used by the *Oxford English Dictionary*, a method James Murray had developed in the 1880s to document the history of English, and transplanted it onto Sanskrit. The college retained the comparative philological framework imposed by Müller and William Dwight Whitney. It also kept Katre's title, *Professor of Indo-European Philology*. The project therefore began by treating Sanskrit as though it had changed through time in the same way as English.

## 2.3 The Project and Its Method

**Volume 1 appeared in 1976.** The project reports thirty-five volumes, 6,056 pages, 1,500 corpus texts, and ten million Scriptorium slips assembled between 1948 and 1973.[NOTE: deccan-dictionary-project-method] In the chronology it adopts, the corpus extends from the *Vedas* around 1400 BCE to *Hāsyarṇava* in 1850 CE.

The method follows the *historical principles* established for the *Oxford English Dictionary* under James Murray in the 1880s: collect recorded uses, date the texts, arrange meanings chronologically, and treat uses assigned earlier dates as stages from which later usage developed.

The OED method was built to document change in English. English's *hlāfweard* gradually became *lord*, and the method tracks that change. When Deccan College applies the same method to Sanskrit, it imports the expected conclusion. *On Historical Principles* assumes that the differences between the Vedic corpus and the Sanskrit that Pāṇini documented belong to the same process that changed *hlāfweard* into *lord*.

The deeper conflict concerns the bond between word and meaning. Chapter 5 §5.3 defines the two positions as **सिद्ध (*siddha*) / कार्य (*kārya*)**: the bond either remains established or is continually produced. Patañjali's महाभाष्य (*Mahābhāṣya*) opens the grammatical inquiry with सिद्ध (*siddha*):

> **सिद्धे शब्दार्थसम्बन्धे**
>
> *siddhe śabdārthasambandhe*
>
> *"Given that the bond between word and meaning is established."*

*Historical principles* begins with the opposite axiom: कार्य (*kārya*), in which communities renegotiate the bond between word and meaning over time. When this method is applied to Sanskrit, it quietly replaces the सिद्ध (*siddha*) bond stated at the opening of the foundational grammar with its own premise. The method requires the bond to change. Sanskrit declares it established.

The Deccan College pages describe the dictionary as *"the only tool for tracing the development of Sanskrit language through ages"*. They promise to document *"the detailed linguistic changes that have occurred in various words and their derivations"*, arrange meanings chronologically, and assign meaning numbers according to changing nuances.[NOTE: deccan-dictionary-project-method] The words *development*, *change*, and *chronology* state the natural-language account before the first entry is examined.

*Historical principles* also needs dates. The Hindu continuum preserves its own chronology for remembered events, lineages, reigns, and civilizational time. Yet the specific dates Western philology assigns to the *Vedas*, the *Aṣṭādhyāyī*, the महाभाष्य (*Mahābhāṣya*), and the वेदाङ्ग (*Vedāṅga*) infrastructure do not come from those texts or from the **परम्परा (*paramparā*)** that transmits them. The dictionary therefore imports the dates required by its story of development.

The *progressive dogma* supplies the dates from outside. In the project's chronology, the corpus begins with the *Ṛgveda* around 1400 BCE, places Pāṇini around 500 BCE, and places Patañjali in the second century BCE. The dictionary records the assigned *"date of the text"* beside each vocable on its slips. The dates then appear inside the collection as if the evidence itself had supplied them. When those same dates are used to demonstrate the language's development, the method uses its own chronology to prove the history that the chronology assumes.

This book uses *thousands of years* when the Indic sources establish duration but provide no calendar year. It uses internal references to establish relative sequence: one work cites another, or a commentator responds to an earlier analysis. It does not convert an assigned date into evidence merely because the date has become familiar.

## 2.4 The Double Standard

The church of progress recognizes engineered preservation when it examines Hebrew and Quranic texts. When it examines Sanskrit, however, it credits the language's stability to Pāṇini's supposed codification and refuses to acknowledge the distributed calibration supplied by the Vedas.

The Masoretic apparatus preserves a fixed consonantal text through vowel signs, cantillation marks, marginal notes, and statistical checks.[NOTE: masoretic-engineered-preservation] Scholars examine its variants as evidence about the transmission of a bounded text. They do not claim that the apparatus kept every form of Hebrew unchanged in ordinary speech.

The Quranic system likewise preserves a bounded text through *tajwīd*, canonical *qirā'āt*, chains of transmission, and memorization.[NOTE: quranic-engineered-preservation] Chapter 2 showed the limit clearly: religious and political authority kept Quranic and Formal Arabic stable while spoken Arabics continued changing.

Tamil exposes the problem with the codification claim. Sanskrit and Tamil both preserved grammatical knowledge without placing a central authority above the language, as Chapter 2 §2.2 and Chapter 17 §17.10 explain. The ***Tolkāppiyam*** documented Tamil in great detail, yet Tamil continued to change. Grammatical documentation alone therefore cannot explain Sanskrit's invariance.

Sanskrit presents a larger architecture built on the Vedic corpus. The प्रातिशाख्य (*Prātiśākhya*) and शिक्षा (*Śikṣā*) disciplines specify sounds. छन्दस् (*Chandas*) preserves metrical structure. व्याकरणम् (*Vyākaraṇam*) documents grammar. Eleven पाठाः (*pāṭhāḥ*) re-encode the Vedic words in different recitation forms. Teacher-student lineages preserve the system across society. Together, the Vedas and these disciplines calibrate the sounds and grammar available to लौकिक (*laukika*) Sanskrit. They preserve more than one bounded text.

The pyramid calls Sanskrit's preservation disciplines later historical accumulation and treats variation around the calibrant as evidence that the language itself changed. It recognizes deliberate preservation when Hebrew and Quranic systems protect bounded texts, yet denies the same category to the larger distributed architecture through which the Vedas calibrate Sanskrit.

## 2.5 Five Kinds of Evidence

The project collects variant forms, manuscript differences, regional spellings, new technical words, and extensions of established meanings. Its historical method tends to arrange them as stages in one language changing through time. Sanskrit's architecture requires five distinctions.

**Designed Vedic forms.** The वैदिक (*vaidika*) domain includes forms and operations that serve fixed composition, meter, pitch, and recitation. Some remain restricted to a Vedic scope. Others differ from the forms ordinarily used in the लौकिक (*laukika*) domain. These differences arise because the two domains serve different purposes. A chronological method misclassifies them when it turns the Vedic domain into an earlier and less developed stage of the worldly language.

**Manuscript and orthographic differences.** A scribe can omit a sign, use a regional spelling, or preserve a different written convention. Such evidence belongs first to the history of that manuscript or writing practice. It becomes evidence of spoken drift only when the source shows that speakers actually used the form.

**Deviation from calibrated Sanskrit.** Patañjali describes many departures around a smaller set of established शब्दाः (*śabdāḥ*) in the **पस्पशाह्निक (*Paspaśāhnika*)**, the opening section of the महाभाष्य (*Mahābhāṣya*):

> **भूयांसोऽपभ्रंशाः, अल्पीयांसः शब्दाः ।**
>
> *bhūyāṃso 'pabhraṃśāḥ, alpīyāṃsaḥ śabdāḥ.*
>
> *Many are the corruptions; few are the words.*

The familiar **गौः (*gauḥ*)** example places ***gāvī, goṇī, gotā,*** and ***gopotalikā*** around one established word. A dictionary can record comparable departures wherever the corpus preserves them. Such a departure becomes recognizable as deviation because a calibrated Sanskrit form remains available for comparison. Manuscript differences and designed Vedic forms belong in their own categories.

**Generative output.** Sanskrit formed new vocabulary whenever speakers required it. Buddhist analysis used **क्षण (*kṣaṇa*)** for a moment treated as a unit of time. नव्य-न्याय (*Navya-Nyāya*) used **अवच्छेदक (*avacchedaka*)** for a delimiter. Mathematical astronomy used **ज्या (*jyā*)** for the sine or half-chord quantity and **त्रिज्या (*trijyā*)** for the radius.[NOTE: jya-trijya-mathematical-terms] Sanskrit also formed specialized compounds and derivatives across medicine, philosophy, law, poetry, and the sciences. The operations of समास (*samāsa*), कृत् (*kṛt*), and तद्धित (*taddhita*) generated these expressions without altering the grammatical architecture.

**Semantic extension.** An established word can acquire another use without surrendering its earlier range. **यन्त्र (*yantra*)** can describe restraint, an instrument, a geometric device, or a machine. Different Sanskrit disciplines employ **धर्म (*dharma*)** and **ब्रह्म (*brahman*)** with specialized precision while earlier uses remain available. This is extension within a stable sound-form, not one meaning replacing another on an invented timeline.

The five categories ask five different questions. Did the Vedic domain authorize the form? Did a manuscript or writing convention produce the difference? Did usage deviate from a calibrated शब्दः (*śabdaḥ*)? Did Sanskrit generate a new expression? Did an established word extend into another use? A historical sequence cannot answer those questions until the evidence has first been placed in the correct category.

## 2.6 The English Contrast

The OED method fits English because English changed in the manner the method tracks. Old English *hlāfweard* (loaf-keeper) became *lord*. Old English *cniht* (boy or servant) became modern *knight* as both its pronunciation and meaning changed. Old English marked case across nouns, adjectives, and pronouns. Modern English retains only a small residue in pronouns such as *I/me/my* and *he/him/his*. Old English also marked three grammatical genders, which modern English no longer carries as a productive noun system.

The OED tracks natural-historical change: sounds erode or disappear, case and gender systems are reduced, meanings shift, and new layers of vocabulary replace or cover older ones.

Sanskrit presents a different record. **यन्त्र (*yantra*)**, **धर्म (*dharma*)**, and **ब्रह्म (*brahman*)** remain recognizable across their extended uses. The eight-case system and three grammatical genders remain available. The inherited धातवः (*dhātavaḥ*), the sonomeric grid, and the grammatical operations continue to generate new expressions.

The OED method accurately documents English drift because no invariant English corpus calibrates pronunciation and grammar for later speakers. Sanskrit has such a calibrant in the Vedas. Deccan College has spent decades collecting the variation that can be measured against it. That collection can document Sanskrit's resistance to drift, but only after the recorded forms have been separated into the five categories above.

## 2.7 What the Dictionary Tests

Sanskrit's architecture allows variation around the calibrant. Speakers can deviate, and manuscripts can differ. New disciplines can generate words, while established words can extend into new uses. All four can occur while the underlying architecture remains invariant.

The stronger test compares the architecture across the corpus. Do any sources require an incompatible sonomeric grid? Do they replace the inherited grammatical operations with another system? Does later Sanskrit lose the capacity to analyze and generate forms available in earlier sources? Or do the Vedas, the grammatical disciplines, and continuing Sanskrit use retain one architecture across the two domains?

The dictionary examines forms recorded in written sources. Those forms represent only one layer of the system that keeps Sanskrit calibrated. The Vedas preserve its sound and grammatical architecture. The वर्णमाला (*varṇamālā*) organizes reusable sonomers through anatomical coordinates and grammatical properties. Pāṇini documents operations already present in Vedic use. The प्रातिशाख्य (*Prātiśākhya*) and शिक्षा (*Śikṣā*) disciplines specify Vedic sounds that do not receive reusable grid addresses in the लौकिक (*laukika*) domain. The eleven पाठाः (*pāṭhāḥ*) re-encode the same Vedic words through different recitation forms.

Chronologically arranging recorded uses tests their sequence. It does not demonstrate that Sanskrit's complete architecture changed.

## 2.8 Reclassifying the Evidence

The thirty-five published volumes, 6,056 pages, 1,500 corpus texts, and ten million Scriptorium slips remain empirically valuable. Reclassifying their evidence changes the imported method while retaining the collected record.

The same corpus can test the engineered Sanskrit thesis when its evidence is sorted through the five categories in §2.5. Forms authorized within the Vedic domain can be separated from copying and spelling differences. Recorded departures can be compared with a calibrated शब्दः (*śabdaḥ*) instead of being mistaken for the history of Sanskrit itself. New technical vocabulary can be tested against the generative operations of कृत् (*kṛt*), तद्धित (*taddhita*), and समास (*samāsa*). The uses of **यन्त्र (*yantra*)**, **धर्म (*dharma*)**, and **ब्रह्म (*brahman*)** can show semantic extension without requiring the earlier meanings to disappear.

The reclassification also replaces the imposed chronology. The dates assigned to the *Ṛgveda*, Pāṇini, and Patañjali do not come from the works themselves. They force a created architecture whose origin remains undated into the pyramid's sequence, where each part can be ranked as early, late, primitive, or developed.

Relative sequence remains available. Patañjali's महाभाष्य (*Mahābhāṣya*) cites the *Aṣṭādhyāyī*. The वार्त्तिकानि (*Vārttikāni*) comment on Pāṇini and are themselves cited by Patañjali. Yāska's निरुक्त (*Nirukta*) engages Vedic material, and later commentators cite Yāska. These internal relationships can place one text before or after another without inventing a year. Where no source fixes even a relative position, the date should remain open.

## 2.9 Remove PIE from the Sanskrit Syllabus

The change must begin with the Government of India. The Ministry of Education currently distributes teaching material that presents a genealogy of reconstructed *"Proto-Indo-Aryan"*, *"Vedic Sanskrit"*, Pāṇini, and *"Classical Sanskrit"* in successive historical stages. Universities reproduce this sequence through courses on Sanskrit history, linguistics, and comparative philology.[NOTE: pie-indian-university-curricula]

Indian universities should remove PIE from every Sanskrit syllabus that presents it as the language's ancestor. A Sanskrit student should not have to accept an imaginary parent before examining the recorded language. The curriculum should begin with the architecture the student can hear, recite, parse, and test.

**Begin with Sanskrit's own analytical disciplines.** Teach the वर्णमाला (*varṇamālā*) as a sonomeric grid organized through anatomical coordinates and प्रयत्न (*prayatna*). Teach the धातुपाठ (*Dhātupāṭha*) as an inherited inventory of semantic atoms. Teach शिक्षा (*Śikṣā*), the प्रातिशाख्यानि (*Prātiśākhyas*), निरुक्त (*Nirukta*), and व्याकरणम् (*Vyākaraṇam*) as complementary analyses of a language in use. These disciplines give students the categories through which the Hindu continuum understood Sanskrit.

**Teach the two domains before introducing a chronology.** Students should first understand why the वैदिक (*vaidika*) domain preserves invariant content and why the लौकिक (*laukika*) domain allows people to generate new expressions. Once they understand the different purposes, they can recognize why the labels *"Vedic Sanskrit"* and *"Classical Sanskrit"* falsely turn two domains into two periods.

**Teach the धातुः (*dhātuḥ*) before the botanical root.** Students should examine how the धातवः (*dhātavaḥ*) generate words, preserve semantic identity, and combine with उपसर्गाः (*upasargāḥ*) and प्रत्ययाः (*pratyayāḥ*). The English word *root* can then be taught as a translation imposed by European philology, rather than as Sanskrit's own description of its grammatical atom.

**Compare recorded languages without placing an imaginary parent above them.** Sanskrit, Greek, Latin, Avestan, and the other recorded languages can still be compared. Students can examine recurring sounds and meanings, then test whether descent from PIE explains the evidence better than Sanskrit's radiance into receiving languages. The evidence, rather than PIE, should determine the direction of transmission.

**Move PIE into the history of European philology.** Students should learn who constructed it, which assumptions shaped the reconstruction, how the starred forms multiplied, and why European universities placed it above Sanskrit. Its claims can then be examined as part of a nineteenth-century intellectual project instead of being installed at the beginning of Sanskrit instruction as an established parent.

Deccan College can lead this change. Its current MA Linguistics syllabus teaches comparative reconstruction, reconstructed proto-stages, genetic classification, and the family-tree model. Its Sanskrit and Lexicography syllabus places Vedic grammar within Indo-European linguistics. The institution helped preserve this imported framework after 1948. It can now become the first major Indian institution to remove PIE from Sanskrit's ancestry, reinstate Sanskrit's own analytical categories, and publish a replacement curriculum that other universities can adopt.[NOTE: pie-indian-university-curricula]

The resulting curriculum would teach Sanskrit's sound architecture, semantic atoms, generative operations, two domains, preservation systems, and distributed calibration. Indian students deserve to encounter that architecture before anyone places an imaginary language above it.
