# Chapter 9 Rewrite Plan — The Subcontinental Sound-Field

Working title remains: **Chapter 9 — The Subcontinental Superset**

Purpose of this plan: preserve the new Ch9 argument before rewriting prose or building the remaining figures. The revised chapter should become a figure-led demonstration that Sanskrit is not imported, copied, or merely inherited. It is a curated, gridded selection from the subcontinental sound-field, followed by a visible engineering move: *mahaprana*.

---

## 1. Core Argument

Chapter 7 shows the vocal apparatus. Chapter 8 shows the *varnamala* as a structured sonomeric grid. Chapter 9 now asks where the material for that grid came from.

The answer is not one parent language. That is the botanical frame. The answer is a sound-field.

The chapter's primary claim:

> Sanskrit's base sound-inventory looks like a curated survey of the subcontinental mouth-field. Languages the orthodoxy classifies as Dravidian supply 20 of the 23 pre-*mahaprana* Sanskrit consonant positions. Austro-Asiatic/Munda languages supply 18. Mixed non-Indo-Aryan, non-Dravidian sets supply 18 or 19. The remaining gaps are not random absences; they are mouth-neighbors, exactly the kind of near-field positions an engineered grid would regularize. Sanskrit then snaps the field to a cleaner grid and adds the *mahaprana* breath-pressure axis.

The final hammer:

> Sanskrit does not look imported. It looks surveyed, selected, snapped to grid, and then expanded through *mahaprana*.

Special emphasis:

> Retroflexion is not just one feature in the survey. It is the geographic fingerprint. Chapter 9 should establish that the retroflex row belongs to the subcontinental sound-field; Chapter 16 will later make the full polemical case against the Racial Arya Thesis from that fingerprint.

## 2. Engineering Preface Before the Survey

The survey should not begin with numbers. It should begin with why engineering would behave this way.

Use a short opening section that says:

- A good sound inventory does not collect every possible mouth-sound.
- It stays compact enough to be teachable and preservable.
- It keeps sounds distinct enough that forms do not collapse.
- It makes sounds essence-bearing: each selected sonomer carries a clear physical signature in place, effort, breath, voicing, and nasal coupling.
- These same design attributes will be formalized in Chapter 10 through the *sutra-laksana* / atomic-concision test.
- Here they explain the rationale for *snap-to-grid*.

Draft paragraph:

> A sound-inventory is an engineering problem before it is a statistical problem. The system must stay compact, or speech becomes overloaded. It must preserve distinction, or similar sounds collapse into each other. It must make each selected sound carry a clear physical signature: where the mouth acts, how the breath moves, whether the voice vibrates, whether the nasal channel opens. Chapter 10 will name these attributes more formally. Here they explain why Sanskrit's sound-field behaves like a designed selection rather than a loose accumulation.

Then:

> Natural speech gives zones and neighbors. Engineering turns zones into coordinates. That is why the missing cells in the survey matter. A missing Sanskrit cell is not automatically an absent sound. If the same kind of sound appears nearby in the mouth, the gap may be exactly where Sanskrit regularized the field.

This sets up the key interpretive move: missing cells can be neighbors, not absences.

### 3.1 Terminology Guardrail: *Dravida* vs "Dravidian"

The chapter must use the word **Dravidian** carefully.

Ancient India already used **द्रविड / द्राविड (*draviḍa / drāviḍa*)** as a regional and cultural designation, especially for the southern subcontinent. That older Indic usage is not the same as the modern pyramidal family-label **Dravidian**, and it certainly is not the same as an Indo-Aryan-vs-Dravidian civilizational split.

When the chapter says "Dravidian-classified languages," it should be explicit:

> I am using the pyramid's present linguistic nomenclature for the sake of argument. The label "Dravidian" here means languages the modern orthodoxy classifies under that family-name. The book is not accepting the Indo-Aryan-vs-Dravidian divide. It is testing whether the orthodoxy's own categories can hide the sound-field evidence.

The chapter should also explain why so-called **Indo-Aryan** languages are excluded from the primary survey:

> The first survey deliberately excludes the languages the orthodoxy labels Indo-Aryan. If Hindi, Marathi, Bengali, Punjabi, or Gujarati are included first, the orthodoxy can dismiss the result as Sanskritic inheritance or Sanskrit influence. The stronger test asks what remains when those languages are held aside. The answer is still substantial: languages the orthodoxy classifies as Dravidian supply 20 of the 23 base positions; Austro-Asiatic/Munda languages supply 18; mixed non-Indo-Aryan, non-Dravidian sets supply 18 or 19.

Suggested body paragraph:

> A terminology warning is needed before the survey begins. Older India knew **द्रविड / द्राविड (*draviḍa / drāviḍa*)** as a southern regional designation. The modern family-label **Dravidian** is the pyramid's nomenclature, and I use it here only to test the pyramid's own categories. Nothing in this chapter accepts an Indo-Aryan-vs-Dravidian civilizational divide. For the same reason, the first survey holds aside the languages the orthodoxy labels Indo-Aryan. If those languages are included first, the result can be dismissed as Sanskritic influence. The stronger question is what the non-Indo-Aryan side of the orthodoxy's own taxonomy still preserves.
