## C03-002 - Show how accepted categories prevent the thesis from forming

**Source:** [§3.1, pre-emptive enclosure](/Users/paragtope/projects/writing/books/atomicSanskrit/as_1_03_strategic.md:35)  
**Action:** REPLACE  
**Status:** APPLIED — 2026-07-23  
**Applied:** [as_1_03_strategic.md](/Users/paragtope/projects/writing/books/atomicSanskrit/as_1_03_strategic.md:37) — 2026-07-23  

### Original

> The discipline has not opposed the engineered Sanskrit thesis; until now, there has been no engineered Sanskrit thesis to oppose. Its defense is pre-emptive. The discipline has instead produced the conditions under which the thesis could not be formed. To formulate it, one must reject the botanical metaphor. To reject the metaphor, one must recover the *dhātuḥ* as a structural constituent rather than a biological organ. To recover the *dhātuḥ*, one must take Sanskrit's own self-conception of permanence seriously. Each step is blocked by the step before it. What looks like consensus is not a position defended against challengers. It is an enclosure that prevents the challenge from being assembled.

### Proposed

No institution had to refute an engineered-Sanskrit thesis, because the accepted categories made that thesis difficult to formulate. A student first learns that *dhātuḥ* means root, that Sanskrit descends from PIE, and that Pāṇini codified a language already changing through time. By the time the student meets the evidence, the vocabulary has already decided what kind of evidence it can be. Rejecting that enclosure requires the student to recover the *dhātuḥ* as a structural constituent and to examine Sanskrit's own account of permanence before accepting the botanical account.

## C03-003 - Split the two kinds of shared sound-form

**Source:** [§3.6, nānārtha and anekaśabda](/Users/paragtope/projects/writing/books/atomicSanskrit/as_1_03_strategic.md:125)  
**Action:** REPLACE  
**Status:** APPLIED — 2026-07-23  
**Applied:** [as_1_03_strategic.md](/Users/paragtope/projects/writing/books/atomicSanskrit/as_1_03_strategic.md:129) — 2026-07-23  

### Original

> **Sanskrit is not naive about words with more than one meaning.** The discipline catalogued the phenomenon and gave it names — **नानार्थ (*nānārtha*)** and **अनेकार्थ (*anekārtha*)**, *many-meaning*: a single form standing over more than one sense. The lexicons built whole lexical classes for such forms — the **अमरकोश (*Amarakośa*)**, the most-memorized thesaurus in the lineage-chain, sets aside its **नानार्थवर्ग (*nānārtha-varga*)**, and Hemacandra gave a dictionary over to them entirely, the *Anekārtha-saṃgraha*. **गो (*go*)** is cow, and earth, and speech, and the ray of light — one form, four referents, and the reciter holds all four without strain. **हरि (*hari*)** is Viṣṇu, and Indra, and lion, and horse, and the color green — one form, and the verse settles which. The analysts pressed the question further and split it in two: a single word stretched across many senses — *ekaśabda*, one-word — against several distinct words collapsed onto one form — **अनेकशब्द (*anekaśabda*)**, many-words. Sanskrit holds the second kind natively: **अज (*aja*)** is the goat, *the driven one*, from ⟪अज्⟫ (*aj*, *to drive*) — and *a-ja* is the Unborn of the Gītā (*ajo nityaḥ*), the privative of ⟪जन्⟫ (*jan*, *to be born*). Livestock and the eternal share one sound-form: a dhātu-derivation beside a privative, and no one has ever claimed the goat *became* the deathless Self. That is precisely the distinction the pyramid's reversal-story needs and will not draw. *Bound* is *anekaśabda*: two words in one sound-form, not one word turned inside out.[NOTE: nanartha-homonymy]

### Proposed

**Sanskrit is not naive about words with more than one meaning.** The discipline catalogued **नानार्थ (*nānārtha*)** and **अनेकार्थ (*anekārtha*)**, in which one form extends across several senses. The **अमरकोश (*Amarakośa*)** sets aside a **नानार्थवर्ग (*nānārtha-varga*)** for such forms, and Hemacandra devoted the *Anekārtha-saṃgraha* to them. **गो (*go*)** can mean cow, earth, speech, or a ray of light. **हरि (*hari*)** can indicate Viṣṇu, Indra, a lion, a horse, or the color green. The surrounding sentence or verse tells the listener which sense is active.

The analysts also distinguished one word extended across several senses from several words that happen to share one sound-form. The second relation is **अनेकशब्द (*anekaśabda*)**, many words. **अज (*aja*)** can be the goat, *the driven one*, from ⟪अज्⟫ (*aj*, *to drive*), while *a-ja* is the Unborn of the Gītā, a privative formation from ⟪जन्⟫ (*jan*, *to be born*). A *dhātu*-derivation and a privative share the same sound-form, yet no one claims that the goat became the deathless Self. English *bound* works the same way: two words share one form without one meaning having reversed into its opposite. The chapter argues that the two *asuras* belong to this second relation.[NOTE: nanartha-homonymy]

### Original

> Four alternatives for one form. Three interpretations of the word by a deed — *vaidika* action-derivations: the evil-caster who hurls off misfortune (⟪अस्⟫), the cast-down flung from his station (⟪अस्⟫), and the breath-bearer who holds the life-force (*asu*). The fourth, *a-sura*, is *laukika* — a relational rendering from the curated world, *asurāḥ suravirodhinaḥ*, "opponents of the *suras*," which leaves *sura* underived. The discipline completes it: *sura* is the shining one, from the *dhātu* ⟪सुर्⟫ (*sur*, *to shine*), and *a-sura* is its privative — the un-shining, the one who gives no light, the one who holds it back.[NOTE: sura-dhatu-dipti] **Separate the derivations and the two asuras stand apart. The *asura* the Ṛgveda praises and the *asura* of the adversary are not one word. They are two** — *asu-ra*, the breath-bearer the mantras just praised, and *a-sura*, the withholder, the other word in the same sound-form.

### Proposed

Yāska records four alternatives for the same form. This book treats the first three as action-based possibilities: the evil-caster who hurls off misfortune (⟪अस्⟫), the cast-down figure flung from his station (⟪अस्⟫), and the breath-bearer who holds the life-force (*asu*). It treats the fourth, *a-sura*, as the *laukika* relational parse: *asurāḥ suravirodhinaḥ*, "opponents of the *suras*." Yāska does not himself supply the entire distinction developed here.

The *Dhātupāṭha* supplies the next step: *sura* is the shining one, from ⟪सुर्⟫ (*sur*, *to shine*), and *a-sura* is its privative — the un-shining one who gives no light and holds it back.[NOTE: sura-dhatu-dipti] **Separate the derivations and the two asuras stand apart. The *asura* the Ṛgveda praises and the *asura* of the adversary are not one word. They are two** — *asu-ra*, the breath-bearer praised in the mantras, and *a-sura*, the withholder who occupies the same sound-form.

## C03-005 - Unpack the three fields before the Vedic test

### Original

> Chapter 0 established three concurrent fields: the *vaidika* holds the invariant, the *laukika* is a curated transmission, the *prākṛtika* flows. The pyramid lays the three end to end as a single line of time and ranks the rungs. It demotes the *vaidika* — the timeless — to the *primitive*, the crude archaic origin. It promotes the *laukika* to the codified peak, crediting its order to Pāṇini the "codifier." The *prākṛtika* becomes the falling-away that followed. Two moves, one flattening: the ascent performs heroic erasure — praise Pāṇini so no one asks whether the order was already engineered; the descent performs containment — set Sanskrit's peak in the past, and the living civilization becomes downstream of its own lost perfection, a permanent candidate for outside stewardship. This is containment by category theft: three differences of *purpose* rewritten as one ladder of *quality in time*. It is the *asura* move again — a difference of kind rewritten as a change over time — worked now on the whole language.

### Proposed

Chapter 0 established three concurrent fields. The *vaidika* holds invariant language and content. The *laukika* uses the same calibrated language while its corpus is selected, expanded, and sometimes lost. The *prākṛtika* changes through ordinary life.

The pyramid places these three fields end to end and turns purpose into chronology. It calls the *vaidika* primitive and archaic. It credits the order visible in *laukika* Sanskrit to Pāṇini the “codifier.” It then treats *prākṛtika* change as the later falling-away. This ladder performs two operations at once: praise for Pāṇini erases the engineering that preceded his documentation, while placing Sanskrit's supposed peak in the past makes the living civilization appear downstream from its own language and in need of outside stewardship. Three differences of purpose have become a hierarchy of quality in time.

Are the vedas "archaic?"  If you ignore the negativity associated with archaic, the vedas absolutely are *very old*.  **Very very old**.

Is laukika newer?  Not necessarily.  It is possible that some laukika poems and prose predated the vedas and some laukika poems are prose are newer.  We do not know.  What we do know is that the laukika domain is *engineered* for continued usage across time and the vedas are not.  Which is why Panini, as someone who was analyzing the grammar, classified the two as modes.  The vedas belong to the "chhandasi" mode where the tonality is the basis for recitation and "bhasha" mode which simply means language.
