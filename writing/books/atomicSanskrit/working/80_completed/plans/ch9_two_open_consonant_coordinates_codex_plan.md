# Chapter 9: The Two Open Consonant Coordinates — Codex Plan

## Status

**Completed research and deployment plan. Last reviewed 2026-07-19.**

This document replaces the exploratory treatment formerly carried by endnote `sound-volume-two-empty-cells`. The deployed note is now `sound-volume-two-open-coordinates`.

## Purpose

Chapter 9 extends Sanskrit's five broad mouth stations across seven consonant rows:

> 5 stations × 7 rows = 35 possible coordinates

The independent consonant inventory occupies thirty-three:

- 25 *sparśa*
- 4 *antaḥstha*
- 4 *ūṣman*

Two coordinates remain open:

1. ***kaṇṭhya–antaḥstha*** — the open back-mouth semivowel coordinate
2. ***oṣṭhya–ūṣman*** — the open labial-fricative coordinate

The revised argument should ask why an engineered matrix leaves these two coordinates outside its independent inventory. The answer may provide unusually strong evidence that the subcontinental sound-field was surveyed before Sanskrit's reusable sonomer matrix was selected.

## Central Thesis

The geometry makes both open positions visible. The primary evidence establishes that Sanskrit deploys the labial-fricative articulation contextually as ***upadhmānīya*** before *p/ph* while keeping it outside the independent-sonomer inventory. The *Taittirīya-Prātiśākhya* also preserves variation among teachers over the precise environments in which that substitution applies.

The emerging thesis is:

> Sanskrit's architects surveyed a broad subcontinental sound-field, selected its base articulatory coordinates, and expanded them through systematic operations. Sounds generated by a complete reusable operation could enter the independent matrix; marginal or context-bound articulations could remain outside it. The two open coordinates preserve the residue of that selection.

The field argument is distributional. Its center is the checked forest-belt and southern sample: Korku, Mundari, Ho, Tamil, Telugu, and Kannada use neither [ɰ] nor [ɸ] as an independent consonant in the inventories examined for this project. A positive result elsewhere in the subcontinent would not erase that recurrent core pattern. Sanskrit's treatment of [ɸ]-like *upadhmānīya* can therefore be examined against the same sound-field used throughout the book.

Northwestern and corridor languages remain useful completeness controls, but they cannot carry the manuscript's historical claim. They may be addressed after the forest-belt and Dravidian pattern has established the thesis. Centuries of contact make their present inventories evidence of present speech rather than direct witnesses to the field Sanskrit's architects surveyed. Contemporary Kannada and Tamil speakers who pronounce English words such as *phone* demonstrate the same limit: present articulatory ability does not establish an ancient independent consonant.

## Survey Selects the Material; Engineering Adds the Operations

The plan must distinguish the sounds surveyed in the field from the operations Sanskrit's architects applied to them. The distinction resolves the apparent tension created by the two *mahāprāṇa* series. The book argues that those series were engineered even though the complete aspirated matrix did not already exist in the surveyed sound-field.

The field supplies the five base stop stations:

> *ka · ca · ṭa · ta · pa*

Sanskrit then crosses each station with two binary controls:

- voice off / voice on
- *alpaprāṇa* / *mahāprāṇa*

The operation produces four complete stop series:

| | Unvoiced | Voiced |
|---|---|---|
| *alpaprāṇa* | क च ट त प | ग ज ड द ब |
| *mahāprāṇa* | ख छ ठ थ फ | घ झ ढ ध भ |

The *mahāprāṇa* sounds did not need to recur throughout the natural field before Sanskrit could admit them. Once the architects had selected five stable stop stations, controlled breath could be applied systematically at every station. The resulting ten sounds form two complete series, combine independently with the vowel axis, and enter atoms, words, and operations throughout the language.

> **The field supplies the base coordinates. Engineering adds the orthogonal breath operation.**

The two open coordinates behave differently.

### The Open Semivowel Coordinate Completes No Selected Operation

The four *antaḥstha* sounds arise through the explicit *ik → yaṇ* relationship:

> *i → y · u → v · ṛ → r · ḷ → l*

A fifth semivowel would require a fifth member of that operational relationship. Sanskrit selects no such vowel-semivowel pair. Placing [ɰ] in the open rectangle would complete the geometry while leaving the operation incomplete.

### The Open Labial-Fricative Coordinate Receives a Contextual Output

Sanskrit produces the bilabial-fricative articulation at a defined sound junction:

> *visarga + labial stop → upadhmānīya*

The sound remains a contextual output. It does not independently enter the fourteen-vowel axis, build atoms, and participate throughout the language as **ख** or **घ** can. Promoting it into the consonant plane would turn a governed junctional result into a reusable input.

This produces the full architectural distinction:

- **Surveyed base** — articulatory coordinates selected from the field
- **Promoted generated series** — complete operations such as *mahāprāṇa* applied across every stop station
- **Contextual generated sound** — *upadhmānīya* produced where a junction requires it
- **Unselected coordinate** — [ɰ], which completes no selected Sanskrit operation

### Paragraph for Later Manuscript Deployment

> Sanskrit selected its base articulatory coordinates from the subcontinental sound-field and then expanded them through systematic operations. The *mahāprāṇa* rows are the result of one such operation: controlled breath applied across every stop station. Marginal sounds received different treatment. The velar semivowel remained outside the reusable inventory because it completed no selected vowel-semivowel relationship. The bilabial fricative was retained as *upadhmānīya*, generated precisely where a sound-junction required it, while remaining outside the independently combinable inventory.

The concise refrain is:

> **Survey selects the material. Engineering determines the operations.**

## Established Geometry and Verified Evidence

### 1. The Rectangle Is an Analytical Extension

Figure 9.5 is the book's analytical cross of categories supplied by the *Śikṣā* and *Prātiśākhya* disciplines: five broad *sthāna* columns with five *sparśa* rows, one *antaḥstha* row, and one *ūṣman* row. The figure does not reproduce a received ancient diagram.

The argument must keep that status explicit. The rectangle makes the architecture visible; it should never be presented as a recovered ancient diagram.

### 2. Sanskrit Distinguishes Independent and Contextual Sounds

Chapter 8 already explains the distinction through *varṇaḥ* and contextual realization. The deployed example is now Ṛgveda 1.1.2, **अग्निः पूर्वेभिर् (*agniḥ pūrvebhir*)**, where the *visarga* before **प** may become the bilabial breath-sound written **ᳶ**. Pāṇini later documented the transformation in *Aṣṭādhyāyī* 8.3.37; the sound is called *upadhmānīya*. *Taittirīya-Prātiśākhya* 9.2 supplies the governing principle: before a voiceless consonant, *visarjanīya* takes the corresponding spirant of the same place. Rule 9.4 records a teacher-level exception for the guttural and labial stop series, so the chapter must acknowledge recensional variation. Modern phonetics identifies the pure labial output as [ɸ]-like; that IPA label interprets the Sanskrit description rather than replacing it.

This is the load-bearing fact:

> Sanskrit knows the labial-fricative articulation, assigns it a precise boundary function, and withholds independent status from it.

### 3. The Four Semivowel Operations Are Explicit

Pāṇini documents the four *ik → yaṇ* correspondences in ***iko yaṇ aci*** (*Aṣṭādhyāyī* 6.1.77):

| Vowel before *a* or another vowel | Resulting semivowel movement |
|---|---|
| *i + a* | *ya* |
| *u + a* | *va* |
| *ṛ + a* | *ra* |
| *ḷ + a* | *la* |

The open *kaṇṭhya–antaḥstha* coordinate asks a concrete fifth question:

> What vowel + *a* would produce the open back-mouth semivowel coordinate?

The nearest IPA coordinate is [ɰ], the voiced velar approximant. The body should retain the operational *X + a* question. Any proposed vowel counterpart belongs in the full endnote only after the relationship is verified across Sanskrit phonetics and languages that actually use [ɰ].

### 4. Modern Controls Remain in the Research Ledger

Torwali is spoken in upper Swat, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, northern Pakistan. Wayne A. Lunsford's phonemic table contains neither [ɰ] nor [ɸ]. His phonetic account nevertheless records both as endpoints of contextual weakening:

- /sigəl/ may surface through [siɣəl̥] as [siɰəl̥], "sand";
- /tigel/ may surface through [tiɣel̥] as [tiɰel̥], "word";
- /upʰur/ may surface through [ufur̥] as [uɸur̥], "lightweight."

The [ɰ] forms develop from intervocalic /g/ or /ɣ/. The [ɸ] form develops from /pʰ/, and Lunsford reports speaker variation between [f] and [ɸ]. Indus Kohistani supplies a different modern result by admitting an unvoiced bilabial fricative independently. These findings correct the research record and illustrate the effects of language-specific selection and contact. They remain outside the Chapter 9 argument because neither modern result establishes the ancient sound-field.

### Reader's Mouth Test: *Phone, Front, Fast*

The reader should meet the distinction through sounds already present in contemporary Indian speech. Many Indian readers routinely pronounce English or absorbed words such as:

- **phone** — **फ़ोन**
- **front** — **फ़्रण्ट / फ्रंट**
- **fast** — **फ़ास्ट / फास्ट**

In [f], the lower lip approaches or touches the upper teeth while air continues through the narrowing. These words make the labiodental articulation immediately available to the reader. The next instruction should ask the reader to move the teeth out of the operation and create the same continuous friction using the two lips. That produces the neighboring bilabial [ɸ].

The prose should respect both facts:

1. [f] exists commonly in the contemporary speech of Indian readers; its presence is real regardless of the route by which the words entered.
2. [f] remains a *dantoṣṭhya* articulation and therefore does not occupy the strict *oṣṭhya–ūṣman* coordinate represented by [ɸ].

Do not dismiss *phone*, *front*, *fast*, or similar words as irrelevant loanwords. Their job is to place the familiar articulation in the reader's own mouth. The architectural question then turns on the precise movement from familiar [f] to neighboring [ɸ], and on Sanskrit's decision to retain [ɸ] contextually as *upadhmānīya* while leaving it outside the independent matrix.

Questions to resolve:

## The Core Field and the Modern Control

The manuscript follows the same evidentiary geography as the rest of the book. Korku, Mundari, and Ho represent the forest belt; Tamil, Telugu, and Kannada represent the Dravidian field. Across these six checked inventories, neither [ɰ] nor [ɸ] enters as an independent consonant. The pattern recurs across two language formations and a wide central-to-southern field.

Sanskrit's architecture supplies the other half of the argument. Its matrix leaves both coordinates open, while the phonetic disciplines preserve an [ɸ]-like articulation as contextual *upadhmānīya*. The architecture therefore recognizes more than it promotes into the reusable inventory. The open coordinates record selection from a field, followed by engineering over the selected material.

Modern speech provides a useful control on historical inference. Kannada and Tamil speakers can pronounce [f] in English and absorbed vocabulary, just as contact-rich northwestern languages may admit or generate neighboring articulations today. Such evidence demonstrates present articulatory availability. It cannot reconstruct the repertoire of the ancient field. The corridor survey may complete the account after the core case has landed, but it does no foundational work in the thesis.

The deployed sequence is:

1. Sanskrit distinguishes independent sonomers from contextual outputs.
2. The forest-belt and Dravidian sample repeatedly leaves both target coordinates outside its independent inventories.
3. Sanskrit retains the labial articulation under a governed condition without promoting it into the matrix.
4. The architects selected a reusable field and then expanded it through complete operations such as *mahāprāṇa*.
5. The inventory pattern supports field selection without assigning a date to that selection.

Possible refrain:
