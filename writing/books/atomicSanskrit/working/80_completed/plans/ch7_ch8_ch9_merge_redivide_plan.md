# Ch7-Ch9 Merge/Re-Divide Plan

> Date: 2026-06-08. This plan rechecks the current Ch7, Ch8, Ch9 drafts and the
> newer Ch9 subcontinental sound-field plan. It proposes a cleaner three-chapter
> sequence: instrument -> field -> grid.

---

## 1. Core Decision

The current Ch8 and Ch9 are carrying the right material, but the order is
backwards for the reader.

Current sequence:

1. Ch7 maps the vocal instrument.
2. Ch8 reveals Sanskrit's selected *varnamala* grid.
3. Ch9 then backs up and surveys the subcontinental sound-field from which the
   grid was selected.

Recommended sequence:

1. **Ch7 — Oṃ: The Anatomy of Sound.** The human vocal instrument.
2. **Ch8 — The Subcontinental Sound-Field.** The field of available sound
   material.
3. **Ch9 — The Varṇamālā: The Sonomeric Grid.** Sanskrit's engineered selection
   from that field.
4. Ch10 then builds the *dhatuḥ* atom from selected, measured sonomers.

This order lets the reader watch the construction emerge: first the instrument,
then the sound-field, then the engineered Sanskrit grid, then the atom.

---

## 2. Why Re-Divide

Chapter 7 is already doing its job. It starts with Oṃ, makes the body audible,
introduces the vocal apparatus, explains consonants and vowels, and gives the
reader the modern and Sanskrit vocabulary of speech science. It should remain
mostly intact.

Chapter 8 currently reveals too much too early. It gives the *varnamala*,
sonomer, grid, *ayogavaha*, snap-to-grid, *akshara*, audiograph, Pāṇini-was-
second, and the six architectural characteristics. All of that is strong, but it
lands better after the reader has seen the subcontinental field.

Chapter 9 currently has the right question: Sanskrit as selection from the
subcontinental sound-field. The new atlas plan makes that chapter much stronger,
but much of Ch9's present prose still relies on broad regional description. The
new version should be figure-led and method-led: survey the field, show coverage,
explain gaps as neighbors, then hand the reader to Ch9 for the grid reveal.

Do not merge Ch8 and Ch9 into one chapter. The sound-field survey and the
*varnamala* reveal each need their own chapter. Combining them would make one
large chapter do two different jobs: empirical field survey and architectural
reveal.

---

## 3. Methodological Guardrail

This distinction has to be explicit before the atlas numbers enter:

**Spoken sound-field.** What speakers can actually produce and hear in living
speech, including contextual and allophonic realizations.

**Sonomeric coordinate.** What a language promotes into an independent,
contrastive, teachable unit of structure.

This prevents a false reading of the Tamil comparison. Tamil speakers produce
voiced stop sounds in real speech. The standard contrastive inventory usually
does not count them as separate voiced-stop phonemes because they are contextual
realizations. Sanskrit's engineering move is different: it promotes voicing and
breath into independent rows of the grid.

The atlas figures therefore compare inventories as contrastive coordinates.
They do not claim to exhaust every phonetic realization in speech. Where the
chapter speaks about the broader sound-field, it can use qualitative language;
where it cites coverage numbers, it should say that the numbers are based on
harmonized inventory coordinates.

This is also where the *mahaprana* argument becomes clean:

1. The base subcontinental field supplies many place/manner positions.
2. Sanskrit snaps the field to selected coordinates.
3. Sanskrit then promotes breath pressure into a full independent axis.

### Ch8 — The Subcontinental Sound-Field

**Recommended title:** `Chapter 8 — The Subcontinental Sound-Field`

**Purpose:** show that Sanskrit's selected grid is not imported and not merely
inherited. The field is subcontinental. The material is visible before the grid
is formally revealed.

**Opening move:**

The chapter begins from Ch7's instrument:

> Chapter 7 mapped the instrument. This chapter surveys the field: the sounds
> that subcontinental speech communities selected from that instrument before
> Sanskrit's grid is placed on the table.

Then state the methodological distinction:

> The chapter keeps two levels separate. A language may produce a sound in
> speech without treating it as an independent coordinate. Sanskrit's special
> move is to turn selected sounds into stable sonomeric coordinates.

**Proposed sections:**

#### 8.1 The Sounds Already Here

- Establish that every language selects from the human instrument.
- Explain that a region can carry a stable sound-field across many named
  languages.
- Introduce the central question:
  > What sound-material does the subcontinent already carry before Sanskrit's
  *varnamala* resolves it into a grid?

#### 8.2 A Sound Is Not Always a Slot

- Define spoken sound-field versus sonomeric coordinate.
- Use Tamil as the clarifying example:
  - Tamil speech includes voiced stop realizations.
  - Tamil's standard contrastive inventory does not treat them as independent
    voiced-stop coordinates in the way Sanskrit does.
  - Sanskrit makes voicing and breath explicit rows.
- This paragraph protects the chapter from script-confusion and
  phoneme-vs-phone confusion.

#### 8.3 How We Map the Sounds

- Move and revise current Ch9 §9.3.
- Use Ch7/old Ch8 vocabulary:
  - *sthana* = place;
  - *karana* = active articulator;
  - *prayatna* = effort/manner;
  - *prana* = breath pressure;
  - *ghosha* = voicing/resonance;
  - *anunasika* = nasal coupling.
- Define the atlas method:
  - maps consonants into shared place x manner coordinates;
  - does not measure vocabulary, descent, prestige, or script;
  - asks whether a body-coordinate appears in a language's inventory.

#### 8.4 A Note on Draviḍa and Dravidian

- Use ancient *draviḍa / drāviḍa* carefully as a regional/cultural southern
  word.
- Use modern `Dravidian` only as the orthodoxy's classification label, not as a
  civilizational admission.
- Explain why orthodoxy-labeled "Indo-Aryan" languages are initially excluded:
  the first test uses the pyramid's own non-Indo-Aryan categories so the result
  cannot be dismissed as Sanskrit influence.

#### 8.5 The Stable Sound-Field

- Move and shorten current Ch9 §9.2.
- Keep:
  - phonology is conservative;
  - recitation cross-checks the field;
  - sound-fields without preservation infrastructure drift visibly.
- Revise the old conclusion:
  - Do not say the modern field is identical to the ancient field.
  - Say it is conservative enough, cross-checked enough, and coherent enough to
    be evidence.

#### 8.6 The Southern Survey: 20 of 23

- Use Tamil + Toda + Kurukh.
- State the target carefully:
  - the test temporarily holds the ten *mahaprana* stops aside;
  - the comparison target is the 23-cell Sanskrit base;
  - this does not demote *mahaprana*; it isolates the base field before the
    breath-pressure axis is added.
- Result:
  - Tamil + Toda + Kurukh cover 20/23.
  - Missing: ल स श.
  - These are neighbors/refinement cells, not remote absences.
  - ल can be explained as a lateral snapped from the alveolar/front-coronal
    band into Sanskrit's dental/front-coronal coordinate.
  - स can be explained as an alveolar fricative snapped forward into Sanskrit's
    dental sibilant coordinate.
  - श should not be explained as the same simple alveolar snap. It belongs to
    the broader sibilant-band regularization: Sanskrit stabilizes three
    sibilant coordinates — dental स, retroflex ष, and palatal श.

#### 8.7 The Munda / Austro-Asiatic Survey: 18 of 23

- Use Korku + Mundari + Santali, with the Santali caveat.
- Result:
  - 18/23.
  - Missing: ल स ण ष श.
  - Missing cells are neighbors or same-manner refinements.
- Then add the Santali-free control:
  - Korku + Mundari + Burushaski = 18/23.
  - Missing: ल स ण र श.
- Optional endnote:
  - Santali + Burushaski = 19/23, missing ल स ण श.

#### 8.8 The Gaps Are Neighbors

- This is the conceptual center of the field chapter.
- Use the plan's hammer:
  > The gaps are not where the argument fails. The gaps are where the
  > engineering becomes visible.
- Explain:
  - natural speech gives zones;
  - engineering chooses coordinates;
  - Sanskrit will snap laterals, sibilants, nasals, and tap/trill positions to a
    stable grid.
  - For the southern 20/23 result, the three missing cells should be narrated
    carefully: ल and स are near-field snaps from the alveolar/front-coronal
    band; श is the palatal member of Sanskrit's three-sibilant regularization,
    not merely an alveolar sibilant moved by one step.

#### 8.9 The Retroflex Band

- Give retroflexion special attention without doing Ch16's full job.
- State:
  - the subcontinent does not merely contain occasional retroflex sounds;
  - it carries a retroflex band;
  - Sanskrit turns that band into a complete row: ट ठ ड ढ ण, and extends the
    logic through ष and later regional visibility of ळ.
- Forward pointer:
  - Ch16 follows the retroflex fingerprint in detail.

#### 8.10 Breath in the Field

- Introduce *mahaprana* as the next engineering move.
- Use the mahaprana-strip sensitivity result:
  - holding *mahaprana* aside raises similarity for central/southern base
    inventories;
  - that supports the claim that *mahaprana* behaves like an engineering layer
    above a shared base field.
- Include the English bridge:
  - *backhand*, *blockhead*, *crackhead*, *pighead*, *bighead*, *hitchhike*,
    *hedgehog*, *pothole*, *foghorn*, *like hell*, *hip-hop*, *Grubhub*.
  - English speakers can feel stop + breath across compounds or boundaries.
  - Sanskrit turns that gesture into a stable sonomeric contrast.

#### 8.11 What the Field Shows

- Synthesize:
  - no single parent language is being sought;
  - the field is distributed;
  - the family labels do not predict the sound-field;
  - geography does;
  - Sanskrit does not look imported.
- Close with:
  > The field is here. The next chapter shows what Sanskrit does with it:
  > selection, grid, breath, timing, and visible sonomers.

  should become `regional`, `subcontinental`, `field`, or `source-region`.

## 10. One-Paragraph Target Summary

Chapter 7 maps the human voice as the original instrument and gives the reader
the anatomical and Sanskrit vocabulary of sound. Chapter 8 then surveys the
subcontinental sound-field: not genealogy, not script, but the bodily
coordinates available across regional speech. The survey shows that the base
material for Sanskrit's grid is already here, that missing cells are mouth-
neighbors, and that *mahaprana* behaves like a breath-pressure engineering layer
above the shared field. Chapter 9 then reveals the *varnamala*: Sanskrit's
selected, ordered, timed, and visible sonomeric grid. Only after that does
Chapter 10 build the *dhatuḥ*, the semantic atom.
