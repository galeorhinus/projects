## 1. What this doc plans

***Appendix Part 10 — The Asuric Pyramid's Playbook*** is the *personal-mind companion* for the asuric pyramid's operation. The body chapters prosecute the *architecture*. AP10 lets the reader recognize the *small interiors* that compound into the pyramid — without re-litigating each instance.

The pyramid does not survive because its operators are strong. It survives because there are so many of them — each one as small as the last, each small interior reinforcing the next. AP10 is the catalog of the small interiors.

**Length target:** 18–28 pages. Slightly larger than the earlier 15–25-page proposal because §3 expanded from a five-asura structural map into a thirteen-defect personal-mind catalog.

**Grounding move.** §3 is organized around interior asuric-mind defects — *cannot bear a praised cousin*, *grudge that outlives reason*, *hospitality-as-trap*, *boon used the moment it lands*, *predator under court cover*, *identity worn to deceive*, *possessive of what cannot be owned*, *sycophantic enforcer*, *fortress-vanity*, *spite at the foundation*, *multiplies copies of himself*, *willful blindness for the favourite*, *mocker's vocation*. Each carries 3-5 named asuras (drawn heavily from the lesser-known band — the famous five appear only as referents) and a modern personal-mind face at the named-individual-operator level (the senior professor, the editor, the committee chair, the doctoral advisor).

The *Itihāsa-Purāṇa* corpus is the *paramparā*'s catalog of these small interiors — preserved across many generations because the defects recurred and a *sampradāya* that could not hand down the recurrence would leave the next generation unarmed. AP10 makes the catalog explicit at the modern personal-mind scale.

### 5.1 §3 opener

*Itihāsa-Purāṇa* kept the catalog. Across many generations of *paramparā*, the *Mahābhārata*, the *Rāmāyaṇa*, the *Bhāgavata*, and the *Mārkaṇḍeya* and *Devī* corpora preserved not a gallery of monsters but a taxonomy of interior defects — the exact small modes the human mind enters when it organizes itself in *asuratva*. Vyāsa and Vālmīki and the *Purāṇa-kāra*s did not enumerate the asuras because the asuras were powerful. They enumerated them because the defects recurred, and because a *sampradāya* that could not hand down the recurrence would leave the next generation unarmed.

What today's academy stages is the same small interiors, multiplied. The *asuric pyramid* is a name for the multiplication; the defects are the thing. What looks from the outside like coordinated strategy is, at the interior, a few thousand of the same small wounds the *Mahābhārata* mapped a long time ago — each one as petty as the last. No one is running it. The petty wounds find each other; the petty wounds do the work.

The dial here is *small*, not large. The asuric mind is *petty*, not powerful. Duryodhana's defect is not strategic brilliance — it is that he cannot bear a praised cousin. Śakuni's defect is not tactical depth — it is a grudge that outlived its occasion by a generation. The senior professor, the editor, the committee chair inherit the same interiors — one to one, defect by defect. The senior professor who torches a junior career is not executing a doctrine; he is hosting Duryodhana's wound. The editor who weaponizes a forty-year-old slight is not strategizing; he is housing Śakuni.

Thirteen defects recur across the *Itihāsa-Purāṇa* corpus. Each carries a named asura — drawn heavily from the lesser-known band, because the famous five have absorbed too much of the available attention and the smaller asuras carry the defect more cleanly — and each shows up today wearing the face of a specific senior professor, editor, dean, or reviewer.

### 5.2 §3.1 — Cannot bear a praised cousin

The asuric mind organizes itself around a specific other being honoured in its presence. Praise spoken of the cousin lands as personal subtraction, and every breath afterward arranges itself toward making sure no one in any room speaks that cousin's name first without a counter-speech. The smallness is not that he wants praise. The smallness is that someone else's praise feels like injury.

Duryodhana (दुर्योधन) cannot enter the Mayasabhā without his cousin's prosperity registering as his own wound; the famous slip into the illusory pond is incidental, the burning interior is the point — every Pāṇḍava breath in Indraprastha is something subtracted from him. Śiśupāla (शिशुपाल) has reserved the right to a hundred insults against Kṛṣṇa as if the count itself were a possession, and he discharges them at the Rājasūya specifically because the cousin is being honoured first. Kaṃsa (कंस) cannot rest while a sister's child grows up praised in another household, and converts every messenger into an annunciation of his own subtraction. Madhu (मधु), whose name the asura-clan inherits, sets the template earlier: a brother's gift becomes the source of the unbearable comparison.

The senior reviewer would rather torch the field than concede that the outsider's reframing was the better one all along, because conceding means the cousin is praised in his room. The Ivy-League Sanskritist spends his whole career writing takedowns of every Indian scholar receiving recognition and calls it a discipline, because takedown is the only idiom in which the cousin can be discussed without his own subtraction. The newly-tenured assistant professor, the moment his contract clears, corners the most senior Indian-trained scholar in the room to publicly correct on a Sanskrit point he half-remembers — the public correction is the counter-speech the interior required the second the cousin was named.

The defect is the wound, not the strategy.

### 5.3 §3.2 — Grudge that outlives reason

The asuric mind preserves a wound past every condition under which the wound was coherent. The original injury is decades or lifetimes gone, the original actors are dead or irrelevant, and the grudge has become the only furniture left in the interior. Letting it heal would mean the life organized around it was air.

Śakuni (शकुनि) carries Gāndhāra's humiliation across a full generation of court life, polishing the dice through years in which no one alive remembers the original wrong; the dice game is the discharge of a grudge that ran on its own metabolism long after Bhīṣma had forgotten the matter. Aśvatthāmā (अश्वत्थामा), denied the gem and cursed to wander, keeps the wound for the *kalpa* — the curse is not the defect, the inability to let the wound close is the defect. Rāhu (राहु), severed at the throat, still chases the sun and moon through every eclipse because nothing has happened to him since the betrayal; the betrayal is still all he has. Karṇa, when he turns asuric — and Vyāsa is precise that he turns asuric only along the grudge axis — cannot release the chariot-wheel humiliation even when Kṛṣṇa offers him an exit; the wound is preferred to the exit.

The editor's forty-year grudge against an institution shapes every peer-review assignment he hands out, and he calls it scholarly judgment because the grudge has metabolized into a personality. The retired professor, twenty years after the tenure case, still writes the listserv post against the colleague who voted against him with the same heat as the first week — the post is the only structure his interior knows how to produce. The displaced senior figure, having lost the institutional fight cleanly, spends the next decade writing anonymous letters to tenure committees about people who beat him, because the anonymous letter is the form in which the wound stays open.

Healing would empty the room.

### 5.4 §3.3 — Willful blindness for the favourite

The asuric mind knows. It has been told plainly, more than once, by people whose judgment it cannot dismiss. It nods, sighs, and lets the favoured one do what the favoured one wants — because saying no would require being the figure who said no, and the small comfort of pretending the decision is not his is the whole of the interior. The darkness feeds on the asked-as-weather posture: *what is happening, what shall we do*, as if the speaker were not the one refusing to stop it.

Dhṛtarāṣṭra (धृतराष्ट्र) hears Vidura plainly, hears Sañjaya plainly, hears Kṛṣṇa plainly, and still asks at Kurukṣetra what his sons and the Pāṇḍavas did — the question is the costume the refusal wears. Akampana (अकम्पन), at Rāvaṇa's council, urges abduction over open confrontation knowing exactly what the path costs — the counsel is volitional indulgence of the brother-king's appetite dressed as practical advice; the indulgence is the defect, not the tactical preference. Vṛṣaparvan (वृषपर्वन्), the Daitya king, indulges Śarmiṣṭhā's cruelty toward Devayānī past every warning Śukrācārya offers him, because correcting the daughter would require being the father who corrected her.

The department chair knew the bully-advisor was destroying students and chose, each year, to call it a personality matter outside his remit — *outside his remit* is the favoured one's costume worn by the chair himself. The senior Indian academic in the Western department tells you privately the framing is wrong, then co-signs the hostile review because the chair is a friend of forty years; the private concession is what the volitional blindness sounds like aloud. The dean indulges his star postdoc's hatred of the Indic-*paramparā* senior scholar and quietly defunds her seminar to keep his protégé happy — the defunding is the favouritism without the figure of the favouriting hand.

What the favoured one wants, the asura calls weather.

### 5.5 §3.4 — Hospitality-as-trap

The asuric mind cannot strike from the open. It requires the door to be opened, the bowl to be filled, the guest to relax — and only then will it close its hand. The small pleasure is specifically the violation of the trust the welcome generated. The host's smile is the whole weapon, and the ego requires the win that comes from trust violated; the win from open force is not enough.

Ilvala (इल्वल) feeds his brother Vātāpi to the unsuspecting guest as cooked meat and then calls the brother out from the stomach — the entire scheme runs on the guest having sat down to eat. Jaṭāsura (जटासुर) approaches the Pāṇḍavas in the forest dressed as a *brāhmaṇa*, accepts their hospitality, and only then carries off Draupadī and the younger brothers. Aghāsura (अघासुर) opens his serpent-jaws as a cave the cowherd boys are welcome to enter and rest in; the welcome is the weapon's shape. Pūtanā (पूतना) arrives at Nanda's household as a beautiful nurse offering the breast to the infant — the breast-offer is the entire attack. Bakāsura (बकासुर), in the village arrangement at Ekacakrā, is the protector the villagers feed by rotation — the protection-bargain is the trap; the tribute-meal becomes him.

The peer reviewer invites the heterodox manuscript in with collegial language and then guts it in confidential comments he knows the author will never see — the collegiality at the door is the precondition of the gutting in the kitchen. The mentor-figure's pastoral warmth toward young researchers is the cover under which their best ideas are systematically extracted and never credited; the pastoral idiom is operationally Ilvala's invitation to eat. The department styles itself a haven for the Indic student, accepts him, and closes around his career the moment his framing threatens the supervisor's lifework — the haven was the trap's name, not its falsification.

What was opened to him is what he uses.

### 5.6 §3.5 — Mocker's vocation

The asuric mind has organized itself into a permanent counter-speech. Its whole working life is the running tally of insults delivered, and the ego requires being the loudest dissenter in any assembly where the despised one is being honoured. The audience's discomfort is the food. There is no project of his own — only the rehearsed objection, and his standing among other mockers depends on his count.

Śiśupāla (शिशुपाल) returns here because the defect has two faces: the cousin-wound funds the vocation, and the vocation is the hundred-insult ledger Kṛṣṇa keeps until the count closes. Dantavakra (दन्तवक्र) inherits the same vocation directly from Śiśupāla, picks up the running quarrel at his cousin's death, and continues it as if the quarrel itself were the inheritance. Tāḍakā (ताडका) has no project except harassing Viśvāmitra's *yajña* — the disruption is the whole of her interior, and the *ṛṣis*' work is her only orientation point. Kālayavana (कालयवन) lives his whole life arranged toward one confrontation with Kṛṣṇa; nothing else of him exists, and the orientation is the entire self. Subāhu (सुबाहु), in the *Rāmāyaṇa*, IS the disturbance of Viśvāmitra's sacrifice — nothing else of him survives.

The conference contrarian's entire intellectual identity is built on being the man who heckled the keynote, and he keeps a running count of how many times he has done it — the count is the ledger Śiśupāla kept aloud. The X-account troll-academic has produced nothing in twenty years but can reliably ruin any junior scholar's book launch with a sneering thread; the sneering thread is the only artefact he produces, and his standing among his cohort of troll-academics depends entirely on the reliability of the disruption. The student of the senior Indologist keeps the dead supervisor's feud with a particular Indian scholar going long after both originators have stopped caring — the inherited mockery is Dantavakra picking up the quarrel from Śiśupāla because the inheritance is the only thing the interior contains.

He has nothing to offer the assembly except the disturbance of it.

### 5.7 §3.6 — Boon used the moment it lands

The asuric mind, given a gift, has only one use for it — and that use is against the giver. The gratitude lasts as long as it takes to lift the arm. The interior cannot imagine a second purpose: no building, no protecting, no flourishing — only the immediate conversion of the favour into a weapon aimed at the favouring hand. The pettiness is the speed.

Vṛkāsura (वृकासुर), granted by Śiva the power to incinerate any head he touches, lifts the palm toward Śiva's own head the moment the boon settles, because no second use ever entered the interior; Viṣṇu has to step in as the brāhmaṇa-trickster to redirect the palm. Bhasmāsura (भस्मासुर) repeats the pattern exactly — the boon's recipient and the boon's first target are the same figure; the interior could imagine no other application. Tāraka (तारक) turns every gift Brahmā gave him against the deva-loka that gave them, because nothing in him could imagine using a gift for anything except hurting the giver.

The mentee, the day after the recommendation letter clears, posts the thread about how the mentor was always second-rate — the thread is the boon's first use, and the speed between the letter clearing and the thread going up is the diagnostic. The tenured beneficiary, the week his tenure clears, opens the takedown of the senior who hired him — and never writes anything else for the rest of his life because no other motion ever occurred to him. The committee chair with lifetime appointment spends meetings extracting petty apologies from younger colleagues who cannot vote him out — the small daily humiliations are the boon's only use he ever found.

He thanks no one. He turns the gift around.

### 5.8 §3.7 — Predator under court cover

The asuric mind has been issued a credential — general of the army, queen's brother, fortress-father, court-protected son — and has decided the credential is a license over the bodies around it. The ego requires that no woman, no student, no junior in the perimeter be outside its reach. The darkness feeds on the certainty that the men around it will look away, and on the long impunity of having grabbed before and not been answered.

Kīcaka (कीचक), the queen's brother and general of Virāṭa's army, treats Sairandhrī's body as included in the establishment he protects; the court-cover is the whole of his confidence. Bāṇāsura (बाणासुर) cannot bear that his daughter Uṣā might choose a face he did not approve — the thousand arms are not the point; the point is that the fortress's whole purpose narrows to controlling whose name she may speak. Narakāsura's (नरकासुर) sixteen thousand are not desire — they are inventory; the small daily pleasure is the count itself, the ledger reread. Śūrpaṇakhā (शूर्पणखा) demands Rāma as if his independent standing were paperwork awaiting her signature, and mutilates Sītā the moment the paperwork is refused — the mutilation is what the refused inventory-grammar produces. Virādha (विराध), in the Daṇḍaka forest, carries off Sītā as if forest-jurisdiction were the same as ownership.

The famous professor's harassment of graduate students is known to everyone, and the chair refuses to see it because seeing it would mean admitting he traded his juniors' bodies for a line on his own annual report. His grants pay the salaries; that is Kīcaka's generalship in the room — the colleagues will look away because their kids' tuition depends on him. The doctoral advisor refuses to let his Indian graduate student publish without his name and his approval, framing the control as protection; the protection-framing is Bāṇāsura's fortress described to the daughter as her safety. The patriarch advisor controls every aspect of his Indian female graduate student's career and freezes her out the moment she takes a postdoc he did not arrange — the freezing-out is the credential reasserting that she was inventory the whole time.

The license was assumed, never granted.

### 5.9 §3.8 — Identity worn to deceive

The asuric mind cannot approach as itself. It requires a costume — nurse, *sannyāsī*, calf, *brāhmaṇa*, playmate, whirlwind, even the despised one's own insignia — and the ego's small pleasure is specifically the gap between what it looks like and what it is. The thrill is passing. The darkness feeds on the audience that played along, and the moment the costume is recognized the asura collapses because there was never anything underneath.

Pauṇḍraka (पौण्ड्रक) wears the *cakra*, the *śaṅkha*, the yellow robes, and claims to be the real Vāsudeva — the costume is the entire claim, and Kṛṣṇa's *cakra* dispatches both the costume and the wearer in one motion because there was no second layer. Kālanemi (कालनेमि) sets himself up as a *sannyāsī* on the path Hanumān is travelling, the whole *āśrama* arranged as a stage-set to delay the *mṛtasañjīvanī*. Vatsāsura (वत्सासुर) hides among Kṛṣṇa's calves as a calf — the herd is the disguise, the gap between the herd-member appearance and the predator interior is the whole pleasure. Trinavarta (तृणावर्त) comes as a whirlwind — the weather itself is the costume; the gap between *this is just how the sky is today* and the predator inside the wind is the pleasure. Mārīca (मारीच) becomes the golden deer because nothing in his interior remains except the small pride of being the one who could pass — the deer is the last self-regard of an asura who has nothing else to be.

The Western Sanskritist wears the kurta on the conference stage, speaks the *bhakti* vocabulary fluently, and uses every gesture to route the conversation away from the engineering thesis — the kurta is Pauṇḍraka's *cakra*, and the fluency is the costume's tightness against the body underneath. The Western professor styles himself the authentic interpreter of the Veda and instructs Indian readers that their inherited *paramparā* is the inauthentic one — the inversion is the costume's confidence, the passing-as-authority over those who hold the actual continuity. The hostile academic joins the Indic studies panel as an ally precisely so he can vote down the dissertation from inside the circle — the ally-posture is Vatsāsura among the calves; the herd-membership is the predation's enabling condition.

Recognized, he collapses.

### 5.10 §3.9 — Possessive of what cannot be owned

The asuric mind classifies a being as inventory. A goddess, a wife, a daughter, a scholar of independent standing — all enter the same column. The proposal is acquisition paperwork; the refusal is a maiden's coyness or an administrative oversight to be corrected. The ego cannot hold the sentence *something exists that I cannot take*, and the darkness feeds on the small daily pleasure of treating persons as awaiting his bid.

Śumbha (शुम्भ), hearing of the Devī's beauty, dispatches Sugrīva as messenger with the offer of marriage as if her independent standing were an oversight he is generously correcting — the messenger's mission is the asuric mind's understanding of consent. Sunda and Upasunda (सुन्द-उपसुन्द), inseparable brothers who could not be killed by any other being, see Tilottamā and turn on each other the same hour — neither can hold the sentence *she is not mine to take*, and the small need to be the one who possesses her undoes the bond that protected them both. Andhaka (अन्धक) demands Pārvatī from his adoptive father Hiraṇyākṣa as if a wife-figure-of-the-house were inventory transferable on request; the inventory-grammar applied to a mother-figure is the cleanest form of the defect. Jalandhara (जलन्धर) demands Pārvatī from Śiva as tribute owed; the demand's framing — *give me what is yours* — is the inventory-grammar made explicit.

The senior administrator, hearing of a brilliant independent Indic scholar, immediately schedules a meeting to discuss bringing her into the program — as if her unaffiliated standing were a vacancy he is generously filling, the meeting's framing identical to Sugrīva's mission. The department chair explains to the brilliant junior woman that her refusal to co-author is really just shyness, and that he is doing her a favour by persisting; the *favour-by-persisting* is *no* heard through Śumbha's ear. The dissertation advisor quietly torpedoes the student who cited a Sanskrit-trained *paṇḍit* alongside him, because the divided allegiance was the unbearable thing — the student was inventory the whole time, and the *paṇḍit* citation revealed the inventory had been thinking for itself.

*Mine* is the only grammar he speaks.

### 5.11 §3.10 — Sycophantic enforcer

The asuric mind has no project of its own and substitutes proximity to power for stature. The ego requires being the one chosen first, the indispensable hand, the voice that breaks through scruple on the king's behalf. The darkness feeds on being the man trusted to launder the king's worst hour, and on being seen by superiors as the matched promotion, the trusted enforcer, the first to draw.

Prahasta (प्रहस्त) presents Rāvaṇa's appetite to the Laṅkā court as if it were ordinary business, because being the man trusted to launder the king's worst hour is the only stature he has. Dhūmralocana (धूम्रलोचन) volunteers to fetch the Devī the moment Śumbha frames the assignment as a test of loyalty; the volunteering is faster than the order. Caṇḍa-Muṇḍa (चण्ड-मुण्ड), dispatched the hour after Dhūmralocana fails, are the matched replacement promotion — the pair exist as the king's confirmation that the enforcer-slot is always staffed, and their whole stature is being the next pair forward. Mahāpārśva (महापार्श्व), at council, urges Rāvaṇa to force Sītā if persuasion fails — the small thrill of being the one in the room willing to say the unsayable is the whole of his career. Indrajit (इन्द्रजित्) wields the father's perimeter as his own credential; the filial promotion is what the matched promotion looks like when it is conferred by blood instead of loyalty-test.

The polished dean signs off on every purge of the Sanskrit program and tells himself it is collegiality — the sign-off is what his ambition looks like when it speaks aloud, and what the senior gatekeeper's smile at the last reception bought. The mid-career colleague volunteers to write the takedown review the moment the senior editor of the prestige journal mentions, at lunch, that the Indic engineering thesis is getting too much attention; the lunchtime mention is the assignment, and the volunteering is Dhūmralocana's speed. The associate professor in the closed faculty meeting suggests stripping the South Asia chair from the Indic-trained candidate, because being the first to say the harsh thing is how he signals he is one of the seniors — the harsh thing is Mahāpārśva's contribution to the council, and the signalling is the whole career.

He has nothing of his own to bring.

### 5.12 §3.11 — Fortress-vanity

The asuric mind requires walls — gold cities in the sky, thousand-arm perimeters, endowed-chair letterheads, loopholes drafted in fine print — and confuses the perimeter with the self. The ego requires being looked up at, addressed through the letterhead, defended by procedural clauses. The darkness feeds on the daily inspection of fortifications no one is attacking, and the asura collapses the moment he is asked to stand without the wall.

Maya (मय), the asura-architect, builds Tripura for the three brothers and Mayasabhā for the Pāṇḍavas — the wall is his whole identity; he has nothing to be apart from the perimeter he draws around others, and the small vanity is being the one who built what no one else could. Tārakākṣa (तारकाक्ष), one of the three Tripurā-clients, has identified so completely with the gold city in the sky that the city's destruction by Śiva is operationally his own end; there is no Tārakākṣa apart from the perimeter. Namuci (नमुचि) spends his interior life on the clause — neither wet nor dry, neither day nor night, neither weapon of wood nor metal — because the clause is the only thing he has, and he is vain about it the way a small man is vain about a single phrase he memorized; Indra's foam-at-twilight is the loophole closing on the loophole-drafter. Atikāya (अतिकाय) requires the largeness of his own body to signify; his stature is his argument. Lavaṇāsura (लवणासुर) holds Madhupura on the strength of Śiva's trident — without the trident in hand he is nothing, and Śatrughna's intervention is timed precisely to the unarmed moment.

The endowed-chair professor's CV is so long that he has stopped checking whether his current argument is true, and treats any challenge as evidence of the challenger's insignificance — the CV is Tripura, the perimeter is the self, and a substantive objection registers only as a perimeter-breach. The senior academic's entire defense against the Indic-engineering account is procedural — citation conventions, journal venue, IRB framing — never the substance, because the procedural fortress is the whole of his interior; Namuci's loophole-clause and the senior's procedural clause are drafted by the same hand. The department chair's entire authority is the endowed-chair title, and he collapses into rudeness the moment a younger scholar speaks to him without the honorific — the honorific is Lavaṇāsura's trident, and the rudeness is the unarmed moment exposing what was always there.

Take the wall away and there is no one inside.

### 5.13 §3.12 — Spite at the foundation

The asuric mind cannot bear that anyone has ground to stand on. It steals the scripture it cannot read, poisons the shared river, fouls the subfield with vendettas, fills the air with dust — not because it needs the resource but because removing it from beneath the others is the whole of the pleasure. The ego requires exclusive occupancy of what was never anyone's to own privately, and the darkness feeds on the dead birds at the bank as proof the perimeter holds.

Madhu and Kaiṭabha (मधु-कैटभ) steal the Vedas from Brahmā at the dissolution and carry them into the cosmic waters — they cannot read what they take, the asuras have no use for the Vedas, the theft serves no purpose except that the others cannot then have them; the foundational text is removed because removal is the whole purpose. Hayagrīva-the-asura (हयग्रीव), distinct from the Viṣṇu-form, repeats the move in another corpus — steals the Vedas from Brahmā's reach because the hoarding is the spite. Kālīya (कालीय) settles into the Yamunā and poisons the bend so completely that the cowherds cannot water their cattle and the birds drop dead at the bank; Kālīya does not drink the river, he occupies it. Dhundu (धुन्धु), buried under sand, raises dust-storms over Uttaṅka's region — he eats nothing, builds nothing; the unbreathable air is the entire production. Dhenukāsura (धेनुकासुर) occupies the Tālavana, the palm-grove, and prevents the cowherds from eating its fruit — the grove is not nourishment to him, it is denial-of-grove to them.

The senior academic fouls the entire subfield with personal vendettas and intimidation so no younger scholar can swim in it without paying him tribute — the subfield is the Yamunā-bend, and the vendettas are Kālīya's venom; he does not work the river, he poisons it. The archivist sits on uncatalogued palm-leaf manuscripts for thirty years, denying access to Indian scholars who cannot match his institutional letterhead, publishing nothing himself — the manuscripts are the Vedas Madhu-Kaiṭabha carried to the cosmic waters, and the silence over thirty years is stewardship inverted into hoarding. The editor of the prestige series acquires the Indic manuscript translation rights, sits on them for a decade, and lets the project rot because he cannot stand the translator's framing — the rotting is the whole point, the Tālavana with the fruit denied.

He does not want it. He wants the others not to have it.

### 5.14 §3.13 — Multiplies copies of himself

The asuric mind cannot bear a room in which someone unlike him is breathing. It measures its correctness by the count of mouths repeating its line, and would rather be surrounded by mediocre copies than risk one unmatched face nearby. The ego requires not survival but echo; the darkness feeds on the small comfort of hearing its own voice back, and the asura's continued centrality is enforced by the homogeneity of the room.

Raktabīja (रक्तबीज) holds the boon that every drop of his blood touching the ground rises as another Raktabīja; the battlefield fills with copies until Kālī drinks the blood before it lands. The defect is the boon's choice — of all possible powers, he asked for replication, because the interior could imagine no comfort except more of himself. Hiḍimba and Hiḍimbā (हिडिम्ब-हिडिम्बा), the forest brother-sister pair, are the family-as-multiplication-engine — the defect runs in matched copies; the brother attacks the Pāṇḍavas with the sister's encouragement, the predation is doubled by mirror-presence. Vipracitti (विप्रचित्ति) needs his name to be the name of the Dānava line because the line has no other claim — the persistence is the argument the way an old man's repeated story is the argument. Surapadma (सूरपद्म) surrounds himself with brothers and copies because he cannot bear a room in which someone unlike him is breathing — the homogeneity is the only comfort his interior accepts.

The certified intellectual's every grad student emerges sounding identical to him, and he measures his correctness by how many junior copies repeat his line in their job-market papers — the count is Raktabīja's count, and he cannot bear a single dissertation in his department written in a voice that is not his. The emeritus keeps a private ledger of every student he placed and recites it at dinners, because without the ledger his school of thought has nothing left; the ledger is Vipracitti's genealogy held aloud, the lineage standing in for the argument. The endowed-chair holder collects Indic manuscripts and Indian graduate students alike, locks both away, and rereads his own bibliography at night because the bibliography is the only company he keeps.

The echo is not the argument. It is what the argument was replaced by.
