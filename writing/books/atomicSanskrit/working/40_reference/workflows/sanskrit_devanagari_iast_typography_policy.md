# Sanskrit Devanagari-IAST Typography Policy

**Status:** Governing editorial policy, adopted 2026-08-26  
**Scope:** *Atomic Sanskrit*, its companion volume, essays derived from the book, and public-facing descriptions unless a publication imposes a different house style.

## Purpose

Devanagari is the primary written form of Sanskrit in this book. IAST allows readers who do not know Devanagari to pronounce and identify the same Sanskrit form. Neither audience should have to surrender access so that the other can read comfortably.

The normal reader-facing form therefore places Devanagari first and IAST immediately afterward:

> धातुः (*dhātuḥ*)

Devanagari should appear as a natural part of the prose rather than as a typographic announcement. IAST is the fallback, not the primary display.

## Default Form in Prose

Use Devanagari followed by italic IAST in parentheses whenever an ordinary prose sentence uses a Sanskrit technical term:

> The वेदाः (*vedāḥ*) preserve both the sounds and the grammatical architecture of Sanskrit.

> Each धातुः (*dhātuḥ*) carries a stable semantic capacity.

Use regular weight for both forms. Do not bold every Sanskrit term. Reserve bold for a genuine definition, a major installation moment, or deliberate emphasis that the surrounding English would also receive.

When a term needs explanation, place the plain-English explanation after the pair:

> A धातुः (*dhātuḥ*) is a semantic atom.

The Devanagari and IAST must represent the same grammatical form. If the Sanskrit number or case changes, change both:

> One धातुः (*dhātuḥ*) can generate many forms. Several धातवः (*dhātavaḥ*) follow the same scaffold.

## English Grammar Around Sanskrit

Treat the Devanagari-IAST pair as one indivisible Sanskrit unit. Build the English sentence around it.

Do not attach an English possessive, plural ending, or other English inflection directly to either script.

Avoid:

> धातुः's role  
> *dhātuḥ*'s role  
> धातुःs  
> *dhātuḥs*

Use:

> the role of धातुः (*dhātuḥ*)  
> the धातुः (*dhātuḥ*) inventory  
> the relationship between two धातवः (*dhātavaḥ*)  
> the inventory of semantic atoms

Prefer an English collective when Sanskrit number is irrelevant. Use the correct Sanskrit plural when the Sanskrit form itself carries the argument.

Avoid hybrid English-Sanskrit compounds such as `dhātuḥ-based` when an ordinary construction is available. Write `built from धातवः (*dhātavaḥ*)`, `the धातुः (*dhātuḥ*) inventory`, or `a structure based on semantic atoms`.

## Relationship to Displacement and Restoration

This policy governs one operation: pairing a Sanskrit word with itself across two scripts. It does not govern a second, different operation: pairing a Sanskrit term with its English gloss or equivalent (*dhātuḥ* — semantic atom, *dhātuḥ* ↔ atom). CLAUDE.md's displacement-coinage doctrine governs that second operation, and its rhythm still applies there: 3–6 explicit English-gloss pairings per chapter, solo everywhere between.

IAST does not translate, explain, or replace Devanagari. It allows a reader who cannot read Devanagari to pronounce the same Sanskrit word. The Sanskrit term still stands alone and performs its full conceptual role:

> Each धातुः (*dhātuḥ*) carries a stable semantic capacity.

That sentence enacts displacement. Neither *root* nor even *atom* substitutes for धातुः (*dhātuḥ*) there — the restored word does the work unglossed. Repeating "semantic atom" every time the word appears would constitute over-binding. Repeating IAST does not, because IAST carries no English meaning at all; it carries pronunciation only.

The two rules therefore run independently and simultaneously:

1. Ordinary Sanskrit technical terms appear as Devanagari followed by IAST, on every occurrence, in ordinary prose.
2. English glosses and equivalents appear only when needed, not on every occurrence.
3. The 3–6-per-chapter rhythm applies to Sanskrit-English identity pairings such as धातुः (*dhātuḥ*) ↔ atom — not to the Devanagari-IAST script pair.
4. Between those identity statements, the paired Sanskrit form stands without an English gloss — but still carries its own Devanagari-IAST pair.
5. English inflection never attaches to either script (see above).
6. Derivations and keyed graphical systems retain the documented exceptions.

This preserves both goals at once. Devanagari remains primary and accessible on every occurrence. Restored Sanskrit terms still perform their work without being permanently attached to an English explanation.

## Source Passages

Epigraphs, mantras, sūtras, and quoted śāstric passages use three levels:

1. Devanagari source text.
2. IAST transliteration.
3. English translation or explanation.

When a sandhi breakdown helps the argument, place it between the source text and the continuous IAST. Keep the book's established hyphenation convention for broken sandhi.

Do not bold an entire source passage merely because it is Devanagari. Use the manuscript's quotation hierarchy and reserve emphasis for the precise form under discussion.

## Sanskrit Forms Used as Data

A displayed derivation, paradigm, sound sequence, or formula is data rather than ordinary prose. It does not need parentheses after every Devanagari token when that repetition would obscure the procedure.

For example:

> गम् -> गच्छति  
> भू -> भवति

Identify or explain the forms in paired Devanagari-IAST prose immediately before or after the display. A reader should never have to guess how an important displayed form is pronounced, but the same transliteration need not be repeated inside every cell or arrow.

## Familiar Names and English Borrowings

Established English-facing names and titles do not require Devanagari on every occurrence. This exception includes forms such as:

- Sanskrit
- Veda and Vedas
- Pāṇini
- Ṛgveda
- Upaniṣad
- Rāmāyaṇa
- Mahābhārata

Distinguish an English-facing proper name from an inflected Sanskrit form. Personified Speech is **Vāc** in English prose. The lexical stem is **वाच् (*vāc*)** and the Sanskrit nominative is **वाक् (*vāk*)**, while a mantra may use another inflected form such as **वाचम् (*vācam*)**. Preserve conventional titles and personal names such as *Vāk Sūkta* and Vāk Ambhṛṇī. Never pair one Devanagari form with the transliteration of another.

Words already functioning as ordinary English words, such as *yoga, karma,* and *mantra*, may also remain in their familiar English form when their Sanskrit construction is not part of the argument.

Use the full Devanagari-IAST pair when the written form, pronunciation, derivation, or Sanskrit meaning of any familiar name or borrowed word becomes evidence.

Bibliographic entries, source titles, filenames, identifiers, and exact quotations retain the form required by the source or citation system.

## Headings, Glossary Entries, and Notes

When a heading is built around a Sanskrit technical term, use the full pair unless the term is an established title or familiar name:

> How धातुः (*dhātuḥ*) Becomes क्रियापदम् (*kriyāpadam*)

Glossary headwords use Devanagari first and IAST second. Cross-references may use the IAST headword when required for alphabetical sorting, but the displayed entry must retain both forms.

Endnotes follow the prose rule when explaining Sanskrit. Bibliographic citations and quoted source forms follow their source conventions.

## Scaffold Icons and Other Graphical Notation

The scaffold icons constitute a graphical notation system. An icon identifies a structural shape; Devanagari and IAST identify and pronounce its name. The icon does not replace transliteration, but all three markers do not need to accompany every occurrence.

Use the following hierarchy:

1. **Ordinary prose:** use the full pair without an icon: `गमादि (*gamādi*) accounts for 819 entries.`
2. **First appearance in a figure or key:** show the icon with the full pair: `[icon] गमादि (*gamādi*)`.
3. **Later cells in the same graphical system:** use the icon alone when the established key remains easy to find.
4. **A figure that must stand independently:** repeat a compact label beside the icon when necessary. Prefer Devanagari as the visible label and place the full paired form in the legend, caption, or accessible description.
5. **Captions and alt text:** identify the scaffold with the full Devanagari-IAST pair.

Do not stack icon + Devanagari + IAST on every prose occurrence. In prose, the icon is redundant. In a keyed figure, repeated words are redundant.

The same principle applies to any later visual notation system: install the symbol with the full paired name, then let the symbol perform its defined graphical function.

## Figures and Tables

Figures may make Devanagari primary. A legend, caption, or nearby explanation must keep the figure accessible to readers who rely on IAST.

For a small figure, pair Devanagari and IAST directly when both remain readable. For a dense table or diagram, establish the full pair in the header or legend and use concise Devanagari labels in repeated cells. Do not shrink text or overcrowd a figure merely to repeat transliteration.

Every figure should remain intelligible when separated from the surrounding page. Its caption, legend, or accessible description must restore any pronunciation or identification omitted from its cells.

## Decision Test

Before formatting a Sanskrit form, ask:

1. Is this an ordinary Sanskrit technical term in reader-facing prose? Use Devanagari + IAST.
2. Does English grammar need a possessive or plural? Rewrite the English around the paired form.
3. Is the Sanskrit form itself data inside a derivation, paradigm, or formula? Pair it in the explanation instead of after every token.
4. Is this a familiar proper name, title, or English borrowing? Use the familiar form unless its Sanskrit construction is evidence.
5. Is an established icon or symbol already carrying identity inside a figure? Install the full pair in the key, then use the notation without needless repetition.
6. Can a reader who does not know Devanagari still identify and pronounce the form? If not, add IAST in the nearest natural location.

## Short Rule

> Devanagari leads and IAST keeps the Sanskrit accessible. Pair them in ordinary prose, place English grammar around them, and allow established data and graphical systems to use concise notation after the full form has been identified.
