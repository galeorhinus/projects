Sanskrit assigns no independent sonomer to that coordinate. Its four *antaḥstha* sounds form an operational bridge with four vowel principles: *i → y*, *u → v*, *ṛ → r*, and *ḷ → l*. Pāṇini documents that correspondence in ***iko yaṇ aci*** (*Aṣṭādhyāyī* 6.1.77). The *a* field has no fifth *yaṇ* partner, so [ɰ] would fill the rectangle without completing an unfinished operation in the four-member system.

When *visarga* precedes *p* or *ph*, the breath may be realized as ***upadhmānīya*** [ɸ]. The phonetic disciplines place *upadhmānīya* among the *ayogavāha* sounds: it arises at a boundary under stated conditions and does not enter words as an independently selectable sonomer. Sanskrit can produce the articulation, invokes it where the sound sequence requires it, and withholds independent status from it.

### 6. Bounded Conclusion

The 5×7 analysis leaves two coordinates outside Sanskrit's independent consonant inventory. Pāṇini's four-member *ik → yaṇ* operation explains why the open semivowel coordinate has no fifth operational partner. The *Taittirīya-Prātiśākhya* demonstrates that Sanskrit knows the labial-fricative articulation and generates it under a governed condition without turning it into a reusable sonomer.

The checked Korku, Mundari, Ho, Tamil, Telugu, and Kannada inventories establish a recurrent pattern across the forest-belt and Dravidian field: neither coordinate enters their independent inventories. The observation is distributional; it does not require every language in the subcontinent to yield the same result. Chapter 9 can place that core pattern beside Sanskrit's documented architecture and identify field selection as the book's inference. Modern northwestern and corridor results cannot date that selection because present inventories have passed through centuries of contact and change.

### 8.2 The Four-Question Test

The Chapter 9 body may ask four classroom questions before giving any candidate an independent coordinate:

1. Can the mouth produce the sound reliably?
2. Can the sound begin a stable series of vowel-bearing *akṣaras*?
3. Does it distinguish forms independently, or does a stated environment generate it?
4. Does it complete a recurring operation or series already visible in Sanskrit's architecture?

The second question is an architectural test, not a claim that the manuscript has experimentally verified every one of the fourteen possible combinations for [ɰ] and [ɸ]. Both sounds can occur next to vowels, and no anatomical principle makes an entire vowel-bearing series impossible. Their physical combinability therefore cannot explain their exclusion by itself. The argument turns on independent contrast and architectural role:

| Sound | Physical production | Vowel-bearing use | Independent Sanskrit contrast | Architectural role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **[ɰ]** | possible | possible in principle | none established | completes the drawn rectangle but no fifth *ik → yaṇ* relationship |
| **[ɸ]-like upadhmānīya** | documented in Sanskrit | occurs at a vowel-adjacent boundary | contextual, not independently reusable | governed output of *visarga* before a labial environment |
| Ṛgvedic **ळ [ɭ]** | documented and preserved | visible in forms such as **ईळे** | generated from intervocalic **ड** in the Prātiśākhya account | exact Vedic realization preserved without adding a laukika coordinate |

Modern Indic languages that use **ळ** independently provide the control: the same human articulation can become a reusable coordinate in one linguistic architecture and remain a governed realization in another. Anatomy supplies the possibility; each system assigns the role.

### 8.3 Rejected English Shortcut

Do not explain the **अ/आ** distinction by saying that holding the vowel in English *but* produces the vowel of *curt*, *bird*, or *nurse*. The vowel of *but* varies between [ʌ] and [ə], while the latter group varies sharply between rhotic and non-rhotic English. The comparison would make a Sanskrit distinction depend on one modern English accent. The body should teach ***saṃvṛta*** and ***vivṛta*** through mouth shape and duration; any IPA rendering belongs in the endnote with an explicit approximation marker.

### 8.4 Domain and Mode Deployment

The evidence supports the following reader-facing synthesis:

> Within one architecture, the *vaidika* domain preserves the exact sound-form required by the mantra, including contextual realizations. The *laukika* domain must keep a reusable inventory available for continuing generation. A sound can therefore be essential to Vedic preservation without requiring an independent coordinate in the laukika grid.

Keep the two axes distinct. ***Vaidika/laukika*** identify domains; ***chandas/bhāṣā*** identify operating modes. Chapter 9 may use **ळ** to demonstrate how the two distinctions cooperate, while Chapter 14 should return to the example when it explains calibration across the two domains.
