## Executive Finding

The strongest opening example is **⟪युज्⟫ (*yuj*, to join or yoke)**. Sanskrit itself displays the sequence **युज् → युग् → युक् (*yuj → yug → yuk*)** under operations that Pāṇini later documented. The movement can be seen in the mouth and on the *varṇamālā*: **ज (*j*)** moves from the palatal row to the corresponding **ग (*g*)** in the velar row; **ग (*g*)** can then lose voicing and become **क (*k*)**. The receiving-language family includes Greek *zygon*, Latin *iugum*, Gothic *juk*, and English *yoke*. The semantic field remains join/yoke across the family.

The best second example is **⟪भृ⟫ (*bhṛ*, to bear or carry)**. Sanskrit *bharati*, Greek *pherein*, Latin *ferre*, and English *bear* remain within one semantic field while the initial labial changes its effort: **भ (*bh*)**, Greek **φ (*ph*)**, Latin **f**, and Germanic **b**. This case complements ⟪युज्⟫ because it tests aspiration, voicing, and frication without changing the place of articulation.

The best third example is **⟪जन्⟫ (*jan*, to generate or bring forth)**. The family extends through Greek and Latin *gen-* and Germanic *kin, kind,* and *king*. It already supports Chapter 18's argument that the pyramid placed an imaginary **\*ǵenh₁-** above a recorded Sanskrit atom. It is more expansive than ⟪युज्⟫ and therefore works better after the method has been shown with the simpler yoke family.

**Recommended deployment:**

1. Use **⟪युज्⟫** as the first worked example in Chapter 18.
2. Use **⟪भृ⟫** as the second worked example, proving that the method is not limited to **ज → ग → क**.
3. Retain **⟪जन्⟫** as the larger lexical and civilizational payoff.
4. Move the fuller ranked set into the Source and Reference Companion.
5. Name the institutional project the **Sanskrit Radiance Mapping Project**: *A Dhātupāṭha and Vyutpatti Analysis of Reconstructed PIE Families*.

## What the Method Must Keep Separate

The proposed study will become confused if it treats every sound change as the same kind of event. Four distinct layers have to remain visible.

### 1. Designed alternation inside Sanskrit

Sanskrit sometimes moves a sonomer because the architecture requires a pronounceable or grammatically marked form. The language already uses these relationships in the Veda; Pāṇini later documents them with extraordinary precision. His rules therefore provide evidence that Sanskrit knows these sounds as neighboring coordinates and moves between them under stated conditions.

The clearest case is Pāṇini 8.2.30, **चोः कुः (*coḥ kuḥ*)**, which replaces a palatal with the corresponding velar in its defined environment. The derivation displayed by Aṣṭādhyāyī.com is:

> **सुयुज् + सु → सुयुग् + सु → सुयुक् + षु**  
> *suyuj + su → suyug + su → suyuk + ṣu*

The first move takes **ज (*j*)** to **ग (*g*)**. Pāṇini 8.4.55, **खरि च (*khari ca*)**, then takes the voiced **ग (*g*)** to the voiceless **क (*k*)** before the specified sound class. Sanskrit is not treating these as arbitrary substitutions. The movements follow the coordinates of the sonomer grid.

### 2. Pāṇini's documentation

Pāṇini does not create the architecture or introduce these alternations into Sanskrit. He identifies their conditions and encodes them compactly. The research project should therefore use phrasing such as:

> Sanskrit uses the movement; Pāṇini later documents it.

This distinction is especially important when a Vedic example predates the *Aṣṭādhyāyī*. Every worked case should look first for a Vedic form, then use Pāṇini to explain the operation already visible there.

### 3. Prakrit and Apabhraṃśa change recorded by Hemacandra

Hemacandra meticulously records the ways Sanskrit sounds change in Prakrit and Apabhraṃśa. These are not designed substitutions within invariant Sanskrit. They show what happens when words circulate through naturally changing languages.

The most useful rules for this project include:

- **Siddha-Hema 8.1.177:** noninitial, unclustered consonants between vowels may disappear in the Prakrit field that Hemacandra describes.
- **8.1.187:** **ख घ थ ध भ (*kh gh th dh bh*)** may become **ह (*h*)** between vowels. His examples include transformations such as *mukha → muha*, *megha → meha*, and *svabhāva → sahāva*.
- **8.2.77:** consonant clusters may be simplified and assimilated; examples include *varga → vagga* and *cakra → cakka*.
- **8.2.83:** **ज्ञ (*jñ*)** may simplify, as in *jñāna → jāṇa / ñāṇa*.
- **8.4.396:** Apabhraṃśa may voice intervocalic **क ख त थ प फ (*k kh t th p ph*)** into **ग घ द ध ब भ (*g gh d dh b bh*)**.

Hemacandra usually documents the resulting forms and their conditions. He does not need to claim that every change shares one universal physiological cause. The present project can place his recorded transformations on the sonomer grid and test whether the same kinds of movement appear in Greek, Latin, Germanic, Iranian, and other receiving languages.

### 4. The conventional PIE account

Comparative philology reverses the proposed direction. It places a reconstructed form above Sanskrit, Greek, Latin, and the other recorded languages, then describes each recorded family as a descendant. A serious Sanskrit-centered study must state that account accurately before testing an alternative.

For example, the conventional account reconstructs a palatovelar such as **\*ǵ** behind Sanskrit **ज (*j*)**, Greek or Latin **g**, and Germanic **k**. The Sanskrit-centered proposal asks whether the recorded Sanskrit sonomer and its documented coordinate movements explain the field more economically. A single family cannot settle direction. The force of the case will come from repeated success across hundreds of families, especially where Sanskrit supplies:

1. a recorded dhātu,
2. a transparent semantic architecture,
3. productive Sanskrit molecules,
4. Vedic use,
5. Pāṇinian documentation of relevant sonomer movement, and
6. transformations comparable to those Hemacandra records in natural Indic languages.

## Detailed Finalist 1: ⟪युज्⟫

### Sanskrit record

The *Dhātupāṭha* gives **युजिँर् योगे (*yujĩr yoge*)**, the atom associated with joining, yoking, and union. Sanskrit molecules include:

- **योग (*yoga*)** — joining, union, disciplined integration
- **युक्त (*yukta*)** — joined, connected, fitted, appropriate
- **युग (*yuga*)** — a yoke; by extension, a joined span or age
- **युनक्ति (*yunakti*)** — joins or yokes

The Sanskrit family displays all three final sonomers relevant to the comparison: **ज (*j*)**, **ग (*g*)**, and **क (*k*)**.

### Pāṇinian documentation

Pāṇini's **चोः कुः (*coḥ kuḥ*, 8.2.30)** documents the palatal-to-velar movement under its stated conditions. His **खरि च (*khari ca*, 8.4.55)** documents the loss of voicing before the specified sound class. The derivation **सुयुज् → सुयुग् → सुयुक्** therefore supplies a complete two-step sonomer map:

1. **ज → ग (*j → g*)**: palatal voiced unaspirated → velar voiced unaspirated.
2. **ग → क (*g → k*)**: velar voiced unaspirated → velar voiceless unaspirated.

The operation changes one coordinate at a time.

### Comparative family

The conventional reconstruction places forms such as Greek **ζυγόν (*zygon*)**, Latin **iugum**, Gothic **juk**, and English **yoke** beneath a reconstructed form conventionally written **\*yugóm** or assigned to a **\*yeug-** family. The [University of Texas lexicon](https://lrc.la.utexas.edu/lex/master/0785) describes the field as “to yoke, join, tie together.”

### Proposed Sanskrit Radiance analysis

The Sanskrit-centered account begins with the recorded and productive atom **⟪युज्⟫**. Sanskrit's own architecture already links **j**, **g**, and **k** through visible coordinate moves. Receiving languages preserve members of that same sonomer neighborhood while retaining join/yoke semantics.

This does not prove direction by itself. It does something more useful for the opening demonstration: it removes the claim that the foreign **g/k** forms require an imaginary ancestor merely because Sanskrit displays **j**. Sanskrit itself supplies the bridge.

### Recommendation

**Use in the body.** This should replace ⟪अस्⟫/⟪भू⟫ as the first worked *vyutpatti* example.

## Detailed Finalist 2: ⟪भृ⟫

### Sanskrit record

The *Dhātupāṭha* gives **भृञ् भरणे (*bhṛñ bharaṇe*)**, to bear, carry, support, or bring. Sanskrit forms include:

- **भरति (*bharati*)** — bears or carries
- **बिभर्ति (*bibharti*)** — bears or sustains
- **भरण (*bharaṇa*)** — bearing, maintaining, supporting
- **भार (*bhāra*)** — a load or burden

### Comparative family

The [University of Texas lexicon](https://lrc.la.utexas.edu/lex/master/0229) groups Sanskrit *bharati* with Greek **φέρω (*pherō*)**, Latin **ferō/ferre**, Gothic **bairan**, and the English family **bear, birth, burden**. The semantic field remains bearing, carrying, and bringing.

### Sonomer map

All four prominent outcomes remain labial:

| Form | Sonomer | Physical change |
|---|---|---|
| Sanskrit *bhar-* | **भ (*bh*)** | voiced aspirated labial stop |
| Greek *pher-* | **φ (*ph*)** | voiceless aspirated labial stop in the older pronunciation |
| Latin *fer-* | **f** | voiceless labial fricative |
| Germanic *ber-* | **b** | voiced unaspirated labial stop |

This family shows a receiving field changing effort while keeping the mouth in roughly the same region. It therefore complements ⟪युज्⟫, whose first movement changes place and whose second changes voicing.

### Hemacandrian comparison

Hemacandra records several kinds of movement involving aspiration and voicing. His **8.1.187** shows voiced and voiceless aspirates moving to **h** between vowels, while **8.4.396** shows intervocalic voiceless stops becoming voiced in Apabhraṃśa. Neither rule is an exact formula for every Greek, Latin, or Germanic outcome. Together they demonstrate the broader Indic method needed here: specify the original sonomer, identify which physical feature changed, state the environment, and preserve the semantic trail.

### Recommendation

**Use in the body immediately after ⟪युज्⟫.** Keep the explanation physical and visual. A small row showing **भ → φ/ph → f → b** will let a general reader see that vivimorphosis changes the receiving language without erasing the Sanskrit field.

## Detailed Finalist 3: ⟪जन्⟫

### Sanskrit record

The *Dhātupāṭha* gives **जनी प्रादुर्भावे (*janī prādurbhāve*)**, associated with coming forth, appearing, being born, or generating. The family includes:

- **जन (*jana*)** — a person or people brought forth
- **जनन (*janana*)** — generation or birth
- **जननी (*jananī*)** — mother, she who brings forth
- **जात (*jāta*)** — born or arisen
- **जन्मन् (*janman*)** — birth

### Comparative family

The [University of Texas lexicon](https://lrc.la.utexas.edu/lex/master/0566) places the Sanskrit family beside Greek and Latin **gen-**, Latin *genus* and *gignō*, and Germanic forms behind **kin, kind,** and **king**. The conventional account places **\*ǵenh₁-** above all of them.

### Sonomer map

The first movement again follows the **ज → ग (*j → g*)** coordinate relation already visible inside Sanskrit. Germanic **k** adds the voicing change seen in **ग → क (*g → k*)**. Unlike ⟪युज्⟫, this family then develops a much larger semantic canopy: birth, kind, kin, generation, genus, nation, and king.

### Recommendation

**Keep as the chapter's expansion case.** Once ⟪युज्⟫ has demonstrated the sound method and ⟪भृ⟫ has shown that the method generalizes, ⟪जन्⟫ can reveal the scale of the lexical canopy. The existing *king* argument should remain the hammer:

> That single word is the whole indictment in miniature.

## Two Additional Cases Worth Testing Before Final Deployment

### ⟪वच्⟫ (*vac*)

This may become as strong as ⟪युज्⟫ because Sanskrit supplies **वच् (*vac*)**, **वक्ति (*vakti*)**, and **वग्धि (*vagdhi*)**, while Latin supplies *vōx/vocāre* and Iranian supplies closely related recorded forms. Pāṇini's palatal-to-velar documentation fits the Sanskrit alternation directly. The case needs a clean Vedic example and a short account of the labiovelar notation **\*kʷ** used by comparative philology.

### ⟪अज्⟫ (*aj*)

Greek *agō* and Latin *agere* preserve the drive/lead field of Sanskrit *ajati*. This is a very clean **j → g** comparison. It may serve the Source and Reference Companion better than the body because readers are less likely to know the Sanskrit atom or its molecules.

## Cases Rejected for the Pilot

### ⟪अस्⟫ / ⟪भू⟫ and Latin *sum, esse, fuī, futūrus*

This example remains valuable, but it introduces suppletion before the reader has learned the basic method. It also requires two Sanskrit atoms and several Latin stems. The project should retain it as an advanced case, while Chapter 18 begins with one atom and a visible sonomer path.

### ⟪दृश्⟫ and *theory*

The manuscript correctly moved this proposed derivation into Lost and Found. The semantic relation between seeing and theory is attractive, but the sound path requires too many uncertain operations and risks making the method appear unconstrained.

### ⟪मा⟫, *measure*, and *mother*

This is one of the book's most important semantic claims. It should not carry the burden of introducing the comparative method because readers may spend all their attention on the *mātṛ/mother* dispute.

### ⟪कृत्⟫ and the cutting family

Chapter 10 already develops this family at length. It can become a later stress test after the project establishes its protocol with easier cases.

## Proposed Chapter 18 Sequence

1. Introduce the traditional fivefold *vyutpatti* method already present in the chapter:
   - **वर्णागम (*varṇāgama*)** — a sound is added.
   - **वर्णविपर्यय (*varṇaviparyaya*)** — sounds exchange position.
   - **वर्णविकार (*varṇavikāra*)** — a sound changes form.
   - **वर्णनाश (*varṇanāśa*)** — a sound disappears.
   - The resulting word remains joined to the dhātu through an intelligible extension of meaning.
2. Show **⟪युज्⟫ → युग् → युक्** inside Sanskrit.
3. Place Greek *zygon*, Latin *iugum*, and English *yoke* beside that internal Sanskrit map.
4. Show **⟪भृ⟫ → ph/f/b** as the second physical pattern.
5. Return to **⟪जन्⟫ → gen/kin/king** and let the existing lexical canopy provide the payoff.
6. State the research handoff: Indian universities can now apply the same explicit method to each reconstructed PIE family.

This sequence moves from a small, visible operation to a second independent operation and then to a large lexical family. It teaches the method before asking the reader to accept its widest consequence.

## Proposed Research Protocol

The institutional project should create one record for every candidate family.

| Field | Required entry |
|---|---|
| Sanskrit atom | Devanāgarī, IAST, *Dhātupāṭha* number, stated semantic field |
| Vedic record | Earliest relevant mantra within the Vedic domain, without imposing chronology among Vedic styles |
| Sanskrit molecules | Derived words and verbal forms that show the semantic architecture |
| Designed alternations | Relevant Vedic forms and later Pāṇinian documentation |
| Indic natural-language evidence | Prakrit/Apabhraṃśa outcomes recorded by Hemacandra and other analysts |
| Pyramid's reconstruction | Exact starred form, source, date, and proposed sound laws |
| Recorded foreign forms | Greek, Latin, Iranian, Germanic, Slavic, Celtic, Anatolian, and other forms with source dates |
| Sonomer map | Every addition, deletion, reordering, or feature change placed on the articulated grid |
| Semantic map | The shared center and every proposed extension |
| Competing direction | What evidence is offered for PIE → Sanskrit, and what evidence supports Sanskrit → receiving language |
| Result | Strong, promising, weak, or rejected |

The first computational phase should test whether a small set of coordinate movements repeatedly accounts for the foreign families. The project becomes persuasive when the same movements recur across many atoms without inventing a new exception for each word.

## Guardrails for the Study

1. **Do not claim that Pāṇini created an alternation.** Locate Vedic use first wherever possible.
2. **Do not turn Hemacandra into a universal sound law.** Cite the language, environment, and examples he actually records.
3. **Do not treat resemblance as proof.** Require a semantic field, a defensible sonomer path, and a family of related forms.
4. **Do not hide the conventional account.** Place its exact reconstruction and rules beside the proposed alternative.
5. **Do not use one family to establish direction.** Direction emerges from the full pattern, transmission evidence, relative explanatory power, and the distribution of complexity.
6. **Do not force every foreign word into Sanskrit.** A rejected candidate strengthens the method by showing where it stops.
7. **Do not use chronology to decide the category in advance.** Recorded dates can sequence surviving evidence; they cannot convert an imaginary reconstruction into a recorded language.
8. **Keep Sanskrit's generativity visible.** A dhātu surrounded by a productive molecular family provides more evidence than an isolated look-alike.
