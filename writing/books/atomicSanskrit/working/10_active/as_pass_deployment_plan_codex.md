# PASS Deployment Plan

**Status:** Active planning document  
**Term:** **PASS — the Principle of Architectural Selection and Scope**

## 1. Purpose

PASS names the overarching engineering principle that the manuscript already
uses before giving it a name:

> Sanskrit's architecture selects a form for what it contributes, weighs the load that form places on the rest of the system, and gives it only the scope in which the architecture can carry that load.

The principle joins two decisions that should remain distinct:

1. **Architectural selection:** Does this sound, form, or operation add a function that the architecture needs?
2. **Architectural scope:** Where may that selected resource operate without creating excessive collision, ambiguity, duplication, crowding, or exposure to entropy?

The book repeatedly examines a field of possibilities, identifies what a form
or operation contributes, considers the load it would place on the
architecture, and explains why Sanskrit gives it a particular scope. PASS
makes that existing method explicit.

PASS should give the reader one repeatable method for understanding:

- how the subcontinental sound-field and the wider mouth-and-mind field
  supplied material that Sanskrit selected, disciplined, compressed, or
  bounded;
- why a sound earns an independent sonomer coordinate;
- why a physically possible sound can remain outside the reusable grid;
- why Sanskrit gives some sounds restricted or contextual use;
- why the *vaidika* domain preserves forms that the *laukika* domain does not use for new composition;
- why an additional grammatical option can be valuable inside an invariant corpus but burdensome across newly composed content;
- why the svara architecture contains some contrasts and leaves other physically possible contrasts unused.

### Deployment Discipline

PASS is one of this book's original analytical contributions, but the reasoning
did not originate when the acronym appeared. The manuscript already uses it in
the subcontinental mouth-and-mind analysis, the sonomer grid, the svara
architecture, the two-domain design, and the preservation system. Naming the
principle allows the reader to recognize that continuity. It should not become
a label attached to every example.

The published manuscript should use the name in three places:

1. **Chapter 9** introduces the principle and demonstrates it through the
   consonant and vowel coordinates.
2. **Chapter 16** recalls the principle once and shows that the same reasoning
   explains the different permissions of the *vaidika* and *laukika* domains.
3. **Appendix Part 8** gives the complete repeatable method and technical
   evidence.

The glossary and one endnote may define and qualify the term. Chapters 10, 14,
and 15 should explain their evidence directly without adding PASS callbacks.
The intended manuscript deployment is approximately **eight to twelve visible
uses**, including captions, glossary, and endnotes.

PASS should follow the model of a serious diagnostic framework: let the reader
encounter the reasoning first, define it when Chapter 9 reaches the coordinate
test, demonstrate it through strong cases, and then allow the reader to
recognize the operation without seeing the acronym on every page.

### One Principle Across the Book

The manuscript applies the same principle at several scales:

1. **The subcontinental field supplies the material.** Chapter 8 surveys the
   physical sound-field. Chapter 17 extends the survey through the mouth and
   mind: retroflexion, reduplication, receiver grammar, the de-centered doer,
   and folded action. Sanskrit does not copy that field indiscriminately. Its
   architecture selects, disciplines, compresses, or bounds what it can use.
   Both chapters demonstrate the reasoning without naming PASS.
2. **At the sonomer level, four criteria apply PASS to a proposed reusable
   coordinate:** reliable production, stable combination with the vowel
   architecture, independent distinguishability, and completion of a recurring
   operation.
3. **At the domain level, ten designed-variation categories apply PASS to
   differences between the *vaidika* and *laukika* domains.** The ten
   categories identify what a Vedic variation contributes. The analysis then
   examines the load accompanying that contribution, the support that bounds
   the load, and the scope appropriate to the variation.
4. **At later architectural scales, the same reasoning remains visible.**
   Sanskrit selects compact atoms, governs their combinations, preserves
   additional range where that range serves a purpose, and restricts operations
   whose unrestricted use would create ambiguity or entropy. These chapters do
   not need to repeat the acronym.

The hierarchy is therefore:

- **PASS:** the general principle;
- **the subcontinental sound-field and mouth-and-mind survey:** the field
  evidence from which architectural selection becomes visible;
- **four sonomer criteria:** PASS applied to reusable sound coordinates;
- **ten designed-variation categories:** PASS applied to the contributions of
  Vedic domain differences.

The short codes for the ten categories belong only in Appendix Part 8 and its
figures. Body prose should use their full descriptive names.

## 2. The Five Scope Outcomes

PASS should classify a selected resource by scope rather than force every resource into a yes-or-no account.

### Included

The resource belongs to Sanskrit's reusable architecture. It can combine broadly, generate new forms, and remain distinguishable across new *laukika* composition.

Examples to test:

- the ordinary sonomer grid;
- productive *vibhakti* relations;
- ordinary *lakāra* forms;
- closely bound *upasargas* in new *laukika* composition.

### Restricted

The architecture uses the resource only under a stated phonetic or grammatical condition.

Examples to test:

- *upadhmānīya* before **प/फ**;
- *jihvāmūlīya* before **क/ख**;
- specified hiatus;
- *pluta* duration under specified conditions.

### Vaidika

The resource operates across the Vedic domain but does not belong to the reusable *laukika* system. Its contribution within the invariant Vedic corpus can repay a collision or redundancy load that would become burdensome in new composition.

Examples to test:

- separated or floating *upasargas*;
- additional Vedic *vibhakti* forms;
- *leṭ*;
- pitch patterns and other recitational dimensions that belong to the exact received passage.

### Lineage-Bounded

The resource belongs to a named Vedic lineage or a narrower body of received passages rather than to the Vedic domain as a whole.

Examples to test:

- half-**ए** and half-**ओ** in the named Sāmavedic lineages;
- Ṛgvedic **ळ**.

### Excluded

The architecture assigns the proposed resource no independent coordinate. It may be physically pronounceable, yet fail to add enough recurring, distinguishable, combinable function to justify a reusable position.

Examples to test:

- [ɰ] at the proposed **अ + अ** glide coordinate;
- [ɸ] at the excluded labial-fricative coordinate.

The two excluded coordinates should be analyzed beside two Restricted Vedic sounds:

- the velar region of [ɰ] beside ***जिह्वामूलीय (*jihvāmūlīya*)*** [x];
- the bilabial [ɸ] beside ***उपध्मानीय (*upadhmānīya*)*** [ɸ] or a closely corresponding realization.

The comparison is structurally parallel but phonetically unequal. *Upadhmānīya* closely realizes the proposed [ɸ] sound itself. *Jihvāmūlīya* uses the same velar region as [ɰ], but it is a voiceless fricative rather than a voiced approximant. In both cases, Sanskrit demonstrates control of the articulatory region while declining to grant the proposed sound an unrestricted sonomer coordinate.

## 3. The PASS Profile

PASS should produce a soft architectural assessment rather than a numerical score or a universal pass/fail verdict. Each candidate receives a **PASS profile** with four parts:

1. **Contribution:** What distinction, expressive range, grammatical breadth, compositional freedom, metrical value, or error-detection value does the candidate add?
2. **Load:** What collision, ambiguity, duplication, crowding, interpretive demand, or exposure to entropy accompanies that contribution?
3. **Bounding Support:** Which parts of the architecture contain that load? These may include a fixed passage, pitch, meter, syntax, position, inherited interpretation, a restricted phonetic environment, or another rule that prevents the uncertainty from spreading.
4. **Scope Judgment:** How far can the architecture safely extend the resource? The result may be Included, Restricted, Vaidika, Lineage-Bounded, or Excluded.

The first three parts use soft assessments such as **slight, moderate, substantial,** or **strong** where those words help the reader. They should not create a false numerical precision. The explanation and evidence remain more important than the grade.

**Bounding Support** should replace *mitigation* in most body prose. *Mitigation* is accurate, but it sounds administrative and can imply that the problem disappears. A Vedic passage does not eliminate a collision. Its invariance, pitch, syntax, sequence, position, and inherited interpretation **bound** that collision so that it does not reopen under every new use.

A form therefore does not simply “pass PASS” or “fail PASS.” The analysis
identifies the scope appropriate to that form. A form may belong to the
*vaidika* domain without belonging to the reusable *laukika* system. This
clarification belongs in the first definition and should not have to be
repeated throughout the book.

### The Leṭ PASS Profile

*Leṭ* provides the clearest grammatical example:

- **Contribution — substantial:** *leṭ* preserves a modal range that can present action as desired, intended, urged, or approaching realization. *Laukika* Sanskrit distributes much of that range across *loṭ, liṅ, āśīrliṅ,* and *lṛṭ*. This does not mean that every *leṭ* form expresses all four *lakāras* at once. It means that the complete *leṭ* field reaches into semantic territory that *laukika* Sanskrit distributes among those four resources.
- **Load — substantial in new composition:** some *leṭ* forms are identical to *loṭ* forms, especially in the first person, while the broader semantic range also overlaps with several *laukika lakāras*.
- **Bounding Support — strong in the Vedas:** pitch can add grammatical information, while the invariant words, syntax, sequence, position, and inherited interpretation complete the identification when pitch does not settle it alone. The same passage never asks a future reader to resolve a newly created use.
- **Scope Judgment — Vaidika:** the additional range repays the load inside the invariant Vedic corpus. In unrestricted *laukika* composition, speakers would repeatedly encounter the collisions without the complete Vedic interpretive layer, while the other *lakāras* already provide most of the practical benefit. The evidence therefore supports Vaidika scope without supporting Included scope.

This profile expresses the architectural balance without pretending that the architecture supplies a numerical ratio.

## 4. The Repeatable PASS Analysis

Every application should proceed in the same order.

1. **Name the candidate.** Identify the sound, form, operation, or domain freedom under examination.
2. **Show that it is possible.** Demonstrate that the mouth can produce it or that the grammar could accommodate it.
3. **Identify its contribution.** Explain the distinction, expressive range, grammatical breadth, compositional freedom, metrical value, or error-detection value it adds.
4. **Test combination.** Determine whether it combines reliably with the rest of the architecture.
5. **Test distinguishability.** Check whether users can identify it without relying on exceptional context.
6. **Test collision and duplication.** Show what existing sound or form it resembles, overlaps, crowds, or makes ambiguous.
7. **Complete the PASS profile.** Assess Contribution, Load, and Bounding Support in language proportionate to the evidence.
8. **Make the Scope Judgment.** Classify the result as Included, Restricted, Vaidika, or Excluded.
9. **Separate evidence from inference.** Cite the preserved forms and documented operations first. Label the architectural explanation as an inference when the Hindu analytical continuum does not state the design rationale directly.

PASS should remain an explanatory principle. Pāṇini and other documenters can verify rules and conditions, but the manuscript should not present them as the creators of the architecture.

#### A Modern Printed Cue for Jihvāmūlīya

A contemporary printed recitation text provides a reader-friendly example. In **धृष्टकेतुश्चेकितानः काशिराजश्च वीर्यवान्**, the visarga before **क** calls for *jihvāmūlīya*. The photographed edition prints **(ख)** after the visarga as a convenient pronunciation cue.

The notation should be described precisely:

- ***जिह्वामूलीय (*jihvāmūlīya*)*** is commonly analyzed as a voiceless velar fricative [x];
- **ख** is an aspirated voiceless velar stop [kʰ];
- the printed **(ख)** is therefore a recitational or typographic approximation, not a claim that the two sounds are identical;
- the approximation works because both sounds use the velar region and **ख** gives an ordinary Devanagari reader a familiar route toward the required friction or aspiration.

This modern notation strengthens the PASS explanation. Sanskrit preserves a special velar sound before **क/ख**, while many printed texts avoid promoting that sound into the ordinary consonant grid and instead cue it through a familiar neighboring sonomer. The architecture keeps the sound **Restricted** by its environment.

## 9. Writing Rules

- Explain PASS through concrete examples before abstracting it.
- Use active voice and ordinary words.
- Preserve causal links between Contribution, Load, Bounding Support, and Scope Judgment.
- Do not compress the explanation into labels alone.
- Do not say that a candidate passes or fails PASS without naming the scope being assessed.
- Do not personify the method with constructions such as **PASS assigns**,
  **PASS decides**, or **PASS permits**. Sanskrit's architecture selects or
  restricts the feature; PASS describes the reasoning.
- Use the acronym only where the named method itself is under discussion.
  Elsewhere, explain the architectural decision directly.
- Do not call the method proprietary in the manuscript. Its originality should
  emerge from the analysis and its repeatable explanatory power.
- Do not describe a sound as impossible merely because Sanskrit excludes its independent coordinate.
- Do not claim that one rare sound overturns the surveyed-field argument.
- Name the forest-belt and southern languages used for comparison rather than relying on politically loaded group labels in body prose.
- Credit Pāṇini, the Prātiśākhyas, and later analysts for documentation and explanation, not invention.
- Use *productive* only after explaining that it means available for speakers to create new expressions.
