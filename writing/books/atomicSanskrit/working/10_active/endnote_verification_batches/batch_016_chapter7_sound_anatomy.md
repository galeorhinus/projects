## Proposed Manuscript Corrections

Proposals 2-5 were approved and applied. Follow-up research showed that proposal 1 is unnecessary.

### 1. §7.1 — retained after further verification

**Current:**

> The Indian classical disciplines describe the voice as the original instrument.

**Decision:** No body change. Current Indian music instruction explicitly calls the human voice **Aadi Vadya**. The older *Nāṭyaśāstra* gives the same architecture different words: the body is a vīṇā, notes arise first from the body, and instruments reproduce the singer's notes. The endnote now distinguishes current terminology from the older formulation.

### 2. §7.3 — limit the burst analogy to stops and state the bol sequence accurately

**Current:**

> Most consonants are controlled bursts — which is why the tabla analogy works. The hand striking the drumhead is an event of contact; the tongue striking the palate is the same. Tabla *bols* are spoken before they are played because the drum is taught from the mouth. The mouth is the source.

**Proposed:**

> Stop consonants are controlled contact-and-release events, which is why the tabla analogy works for them. A hand strikes the drumhead; the tongue makes and releases contact at a chosen place in the mouth. Tabla compositions can be recited as *bols* and then rendered on the drums, carrying the rhythmic pattern between voice and instrument.

**Reason:** Fricatives, nasals, and approximants are not bursts. The source supports bol recitation in oral pedagogy but not the universal claim that bols are always spoken before a learner plays.

### 3. §7.7 — replace the false four-part internal classification

**Current:**

> Every sound requires a specific degree of contact. The mouth might clamp shut entirely, touch lightly, constrict the airflow, or make no contact at all. The śāstric terms mark these levels with absolute precision. **स्पृष्ट (*spṛṣṭa*)** means fully touched. **ईषत्स्पृष्ट (*īṣat-spṛṣṭa*)** means lightly touched. **ईषत्संवृत (*īṣat-saṃvṛta*)** means lightly closed, constricting the air without full contact. Finally, **अस्पृष्ट (*aspṛṣṭa*)** means entirely untouched.

**Proposed:**

> Every sound requires a particular degree of contact or opening. The grammatical account names five internal efforts. **स्पृष्ट (*spṛṣṭa*)**, fully touched, describes the stops and nasals. **ईषत्स्पृष्ट (*īṣat-spṛṣṭa*)**, slightly touched, describes the *antaḥstha* sounds. **ईषद्विवृत (*īṣad-vivṛta*)**, slightly open, describes the *ūṣman* sounds. **विवृत (*vivṛta*)**, open, describes the vowels. A fifth term, **संवृत (*saṃvṛta*)**, describes short **अ (*a*)** in ordinary use; during grammatical analysis, the same vowel is treated as *vivṛta*.

**Reason:** The *Laghu-siddhānta-kaumudī* explicitly says **ādyaḥ pañcadhā**, “the first is fivefold.” The current paragraph gives four different labels and substitutes *īṣat-saṃvṛta* and *aspṛṣṭa* for the source's *īṣad-vivṛta*, *vivṛta*, and special *saṃvṛta*.

### 4. §7.8 — remove the false *anupradāna* switch

**Current:**

> **बाह्य प्रयत्न (*bāhya prayatna*)** is the external effort — it manages the final output: voicing (***अनुप्रदान (*anupradāna*)***, which toggles between श्वास (*śvāsa*) breath and नाद (*nāda*) resonance), aspiration, breath pressure, and nasal coupling.

**Proposed:**

> **बाह्य प्रयत्न (*bāhya prayatna*)** describes what happens beyond that oral contact: whether the glottis supplies breath or voice, whether the release carries little or much breath, whether the nasal passage opens, and how pitch moves.

**Reason:** *Anupradāna* is a broader term for a secondary feature, not the name of a binary switch between *śvāsa* and *nāda*. The Sanskrit account also includes accent among the external efforts.
