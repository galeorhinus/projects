## Proposed Manuscript Corrections

These changes have **not** been applied. They require author approval.

### Chapter 3 §3.2 — connect the Mamluk institution to the Delhi example directly

**Current:**

> Islamic conquest built political formations on that sanction; the Delhi Sultanate's Slave Dynasty was named for the slave-warrior elite the framework produced.

**Proposed:**

> Islamic conquest carried slave-warrior institutions into the subcontinent. The Delhi Sultanate's Slave Dynasty was named for the *mamlūk* institution from which its first rulers emerged.

**Reason:** Jackson directly documents the Ghurid introduction of the *mamlūk* institution into Hindustan and the careers of Aybak and Iltutmish. The current sentence asserts a broader causal chain from specific Quranic verses to the dynasty.

### Chapter 3 §3.2 — keep the Assalāyana conclusion within what the sutta states

**Current:**

> The dharmic architecture is the exact opposite. Buddha's observation in the *Assalāyana Sutta* — that the bordering nations of Yona and Kamboja have only two categories in society: masters and slaves — is the dharmic primary-source confirmation: the binary belonged to foreign-bordering nations, not to the Indic civilizational core. The racial Arya thesis did not project a universal human pattern onto Indic antiquity. It projected an Abrahamic pattern onto a civilization that had none.

**Proposed:**

> The dharmic record places that binary elsewhere. In the *Assalāyana Sutta*, the Buddha observes that Yona, Kamboja, and other bordering countries have only two social categories: masters and slaves. He also says that a master can become a slave and a slave can become a master. The racial Arya thesis projected the pyramid's master-slave pattern onto Indic antiquity despite this record.

**Reason:** The sutta identifies the border-country binary and its reversibility. It does not by itself prove that no master-slave relation ever existed anywhere in the Indic interior.

### Chapter 3 §3.4 — state the documented Rāmāyaṇa/Homer dispute without asserting an unverified direction

**Current:**

> Elements such as Rāma lifting the great bow, Sītā's abduction, a war across the sea to recover her, and warriors identified from the walls of a besieged city made their way into the Homeric epics. We do not know whether Homer had read the *Rāmāyaṇa* or received Rāma's stories through other tellers, but those stories had already been legendary for thousands of years before him.
>
> The pyramid's chronology reversed that borrowing. It presented these *Rāmāyaṇa* patterns as Homeric originals and recast Vālmīki as the borrower.

**Proposed:**

> Several narrative patterns appear in both the *Rāmāyaṇa* and the Homeric epics: the lifting of a great bow, the abduction of the central woman, a war fought across the sea to recover her, and warriors identified from the walls of a besieged city. Albrecht Weber treated the Homeric versions as the originals. He argued that Helen's abduction and the siege of Troy supplied the model for Sītā's abduction and the war at Laṅkā.
>
> The pyramid's chronology decided the direction before the comparison began. Once Homer was placed first and Vālmīki later, the shared patterns became evidence that Vālmīki had borrowed.

**Reason:** Weber's printed argument is verified. The checked sources do not establish as historical fact that these episodes traveled from the *Rāmāyaṇa* into Homer or that the stories were already legendary for thousands of years before Homer.

### Chapter 3 §3.4 — distinguish Latin *liber* from *liberalis*

**Current:**

> Latin *liber-* meant free, but also generous, open-handed, unstinting.

**Proposed:**

> Latin *liber* meant free. The related *liberalis* also carried the sense of generosity and open-handedness.

**Reason:** The first meaning belongs directly to *liber*; the extended senses belong to *liberalis* and its descendants.

### Chapter 4 — retain the Sanskrit equivalents

No body correction is required on lexical grounds. The checked Pali reads ***ayyo*** and ***dāso***. These are regular inflected Pali forms corresponding to Sanskrit **आर्य (*ārya*)** and **दास (*dāsa*)**, not unrelated words. The main endnote now records the source wording and explains the relationship.
