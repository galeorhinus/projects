# Chapter 9: The Two Open Consonant Coordinates — Codex Plan

## Status

**Active research and deployment plan.**

This document replaces the exploratory treatment currently carried by endnote `sound-volume-two-empty-cells`. The existing body paragraph, Figure 9.5, and endnote remain provisional until the sound distributions have been verified from language-specific sources.

## Purpose

Chapter 9 extends Sanskrit's five broad mouth stations across seven consonant rows:

> 5 stations × 7 rows = 35 possible coordinates

The independent consonant inventory occupies thirty-three:

- 25 *sparśa*
- 4 *antaḥstha*
- 4 *ūṣman*

Two coordinates remain open:

1. ***kaṇṭhya–antaḥstha*** — the missing back-mouth semivowel
2. ***oṣṭhya–ūṣman*** — the missing labial fricative

The revised argument should ask why an engineered matrix leaves these two coordinates outside its independent inventory. The answer may provide unusually strong evidence that the subcontinental sound-field was surveyed before Sanskrit's reusable sonomer matrix was selected.

## Central Thesis

The two open coordinates do not represent articulations Sanskrit's architects failed to imagine. The geometry makes both positions visible. Sanskrit also demonstrates direct knowledge of the labial fricative by deploying it contextually as ***upadhmānīya*** before *p/ph* while withholding independent-sonomer status.

The emerging thesis is:

> Sanskrit's architects surveyed a broad subcontinental sound-field, selected its base articulatory coordinates, and expanded them through systematic operations. Sounds generated by a complete reusable operation could enter the independent matrix; marginal or context-bound articulations could remain outside it. The two open coordinates preserve the residue of that selection.

Torwali sharpens the thesis. It is spoken in upper Swat on the northwestern mountain rim. A PHOIBLE source inventory records the missing velar approximant [ɰ] independently and the bilabial fricative [ɸ] contextually as a realization of /p/. The central forest-belt survey and the Dravidian survey do not currently show either articulation as a broadly recurrent independent coordinate. Torwali may therefore preserve the edge of the surveyed field rather than overturn it.

## Survey Selects the Material; Engineering Adds the Operations

The plan must distinguish the sounds surveyed in the field from the operations Sanskrit's architects applied to them. The distinction resolves the apparent tension created by the two *mahāprāṇa* series. The book argues that those series were engineered even though the complete aspirated matrix did not already exist in the surveyed sound-field.

The field supplies the five base stop stations:

> *ka · ca · ṭa · ta · pa*

Sanskrit then crosses each station with two binary controls:

- voice off / voice on
- *alpaprāṇa* / *mahāprāṇa*

The operation produces four complete stop series:

| | Unvoiced | Voiced |
|---|---|---|
| *alpaprāṇa* | क च ट त प | ग ज ड द ब |
| *mahāprāṇa* | ख छ ठ थ फ | घ झ ढ ध भ |

The *mahāprāṇa* sounds did not need to recur throughout the natural field before Sanskrit could admit them. Once the architects had selected five stable stop stations, controlled breath could be applied systematically at every station. The resulting ten sounds form two complete series, combine independently with the vowel axis, and enter atoms, words, and operations throughout the language.

> **The field supplies the base coordinates. Engineering adds the orthogonal breath operation.**

The two open cells behave differently.

### The Missing Semivowel Completes No Selected Operation

The four *antaḥstha* sounds arise through the explicit *ik → yaṇ* relationship:

> *i → y · u → v · ṛ → r · ḷ → l*

A fifth semivowel would require a fifth member of that operational relationship. Sanskrit selects no such vowel-semivowel pair. Placing [ɰ] in the open rectangle would complete the geometry while leaving the operation incomplete.

### The Missing Labial Fricative Is Generated When Needed

Sanskrit produces the bilabial-fricative articulation at a defined sound junction:

> *visarga + labial stop → upadhmānīya*

The sound remains a contextual output. It does not independently enter the fourteen-vowel axis, build atoms, and participate throughout the language as **ख** or **घ** can. Promoting it into the consonant plane would turn a governed junctional result into a reusable input.

This produces the full architectural distinction:

- **Surveyed base** — articulatory coordinates selected from the field
- **Promoted generated series** — complete operations such as *mahāprāṇa* applied across every stop station
- **Contextual generated sound** — *upadhmānīya* produced where a junction requires it
- **Unselected coordinate** — [ɰ], which completes no selected Sanskrit operation

### Paragraph for Later Manuscript Deployment

> Sanskrit selected its base articulatory coordinates from the subcontinental sound-field and then expanded them through systematic operations. The *mahāprāṇa* rows are the result of one such operation: controlled breath applied across every stop station. Marginal sounds received different treatment. The velar semivowel was not admitted because it completed no selected vowel-semivowel relationship. The bilabial fricative was retained as *upadhmānīya*, generated precisely where a sound-junction required it, while remaining outside the independently combinable inventory.

The concise refrain is:

> **Survey selects the material. Engineering determines the operations.**

## What Is Already Established

### 1. The Rectangle Is an Analytical Extension

The *Śikṣā* and *Prātiśākhya* disciplines supply the place and manner categories. They do not print the book's 5×7 rectangle. Figure 9.5 crosses the received five broad *sthāna* columns with the five *sparśa* rows, one *antaḥstha* row, and one *ūṣman* row.

The argument must keep that status explicit. The rectangle makes the architecture visible; it should never be presented as a recovered ancient diagram.

### 2. Sanskrit Distinguishes Independent and Contextual Sounds

Chapter 8 already explains the distinction through *varṇaḥ* and contextual realization. In ***rāmaḥ paśyati***, *visarga* may be realized as ***upadhmānīya*** before the labial stop. The *Prātiśākhya* disciplines preserve *upadhmānīya* among the *ayogavāha* sounds.

This is the load-bearing fact:

> Sanskrit knows the labial fricative, assigns it a precise boundary function, and discards it from the independent reusable matrix.

### 3. The Four Semivowel Operations Are Explicit

Pāṇini documents the four *ik → yaṇ* correspondences in ***iko yaṇ aci*** (*Aṣṭādhyāyī* 6.1.77):

| Vowel before *a* or another vowel | Resulting semivowel movement |
|---|---|
| *i + a* | *ya* |
| *u + a* | *va* |
| *ṛ + a* | *ra* |
| *ḷ + a* | *la* |

The open *kaṇṭhya–antaḥstha* coordinate asks a concrete fifth question:

> What vowel + *a* would produce the missing back-mouth semivowel?

The nearest IPA coordinate is [ɰ], the voiced velar approximant. The body should retain the operational *X + a* question. Any proposed vowel counterpart belongs in the full endnote only after the relationship is verified across Sanskrit phonetics and languages that actually use [ɰ].

### 4. Torwali Supplies the Northwestern Edge Case

Torwali is spoken in upper Swat, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, northern Pakistan. The current PHOIBLE query records:

- [ɰ] as an independent inventory entry
- [ɸ] as a contextual realization of /p/

Both entries must be checked against Wayne A. Lunsford's Torwali description or another language-specific primary grammar before publication. The current endnote mentions only Torwali [ɰ] and must be corrected to include its [ɸ] evidence if the source confirms it.

### Reader's Mouth Test: *Phone, Front, Fast*

The reader should meet the distinction through sounds already present in contemporary Indian speech. Many Indian readers routinely pronounce English or absorbed words such as:

- **phone** — **फ़ोन**
- **front** — **फ़्रण्ट / फ्रंट**
- **fast** — **फ़ास्ट / फास्ट**

In [f], the lower lip approaches or touches the upper teeth while air continues through the narrowing. These words make the labiodental articulation immediately available to the reader. The next instruction should ask the reader to move the teeth out of the operation and create the same continuous friction using the two lips. That produces the neighboring bilabial [ɸ].

The prose should respect both facts:

1. [f] exists commonly in the contemporary speech of Indian readers; its presence is real regardless of the route by which the words entered.
2. [f] remains a *dantoṣṭhya* articulation and therefore does not occupy the strict *oṣṭhya–ūṣman* coordinate represented by [ɸ].

Do not dismiss *phone*, *front*, *fast*, or similar words as irrelevant loanwords. Their job is to place the familiar articulation in the reader's own mouth. The architectural question then turns on the precise movement from familiar [f] to neighboring [ɸ], and on Sanskrit's decision to retain [ɸ] contextually as *upadhmānīya* while leaving it outside the independent matrix.

Questions to resolve:

5. Does the source that lists Tamil [ɸ] provide native or absorbed lexical examples, allophonic conditions, and recordings?

7. Where does [f] enter contemporary Kannada, Telugu, Tamil and other Indian speech, and does any of that evidence bear on the strict [ɸ] coordinate?

## The Two Explanatory Accounts

The evidence should be presented through two accounts after the corridor table is complete.

### Account One: Migratory Amnesia

The migration account must explain the following sequence if the corridor survey finds the target sounds along the route:

1. The migrating population carries [ɰ], [ɸ], or both toward the subcontinent.
2. The sounds weaken or disappear as independent contrasts after entry.
3. The same population acquires the subcontinent's retroflex mouth and other field-features.
4. The resulting Sanskrit matrix matches the destination field rather than the supposed source field.
5. Sanskrit somehow retains [ɸ] precisely as *upadhmānīya* while excluding it from the independent matrix.

This is the appropriate location for the monsoon satire. It should follow the data rather than substitute for it.

Possible heading:

> **What the Monsoon Made Them Forget**

Possible refrain:

> The migrants forget the sounds they carried and acquire the architecture of the land they enter.

### Account Two: The Surveyed Field

The engineering account predicts:

1. Sounds recurrent across the subcontinental field receive independent reusable coordinates.
2. Sounds confined to the northwestern rim, scattered languages, or narrow contexts remain outside the core.
3. A useful contextual articulation can be retained without being promoted into the independent matrix.
4. The Sanskrit grid resembles the broad destination field because that field was the source material surveyed by the architects.

Possible refrain:

> The architects listened to the field, selected its reusable sounds, and left the marginal coordinates open.

### Comparative Prediction Table

| Observation | Migratory account must explain | Surveyed-field account predicts |
|---|---|---|
| [ɰ] and [ɸ] occur along the northwestern corridor | why the migrants lose or demote them | why edge sounds remain outside the shared core |
| forest-belt and Dravidian surveys leave both coordinates broadly unfilled | why destination languages reshape the migrant inventory so completely | why low-recurrence sounds are not selected |
| Sanskrit uses [ɸ] contextually as *upadhmānīya* | why a supposedly lost sound survives under precise conditions | why a known articulation is retained only where useful |
| Sanskrit completes retroflex and other subcontinental rows | why migration produces a destination-shaped matrix | why a survey of the destination field produces that matrix |
