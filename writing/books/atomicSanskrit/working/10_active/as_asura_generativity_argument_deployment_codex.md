## 1. Deployment Claim

The Ṛgveda uses the *asura* sound-form in two opposed fields. In passages that praise Indra, Varuṇa, Agni, Mitra, and Rudra, the action supports ***asu-ra***: the breath-bearer, the holder of life-force who sustains, protects, measures, or releases. In Ṛgveda 5.40.5, Svarbhānu pierces Sūrya with darkness and leaves the worlds unable to find their field. The action supports ***a-sura***: the un-shining one who opposes and withholds radiance.

Sanskrit's generative architecture establishes that both formations are available. The independent frequency of *sura* cannot determine whether Sanskrit can form *a-sura*. The Ṛgveda's repeated use of privative formations such as ***a-dabdha*** demonstrates that a privative word does not depend upon frequent independent use of its positive base. Once both formations are available, the action described in each mantra identifies the word being used.

The Ṛgveda supplies its own luminous field through ***svar, sūrya, sūri,*** and ***sūra***. This Vedic evidence carries the argument before the *Dhātupāṭha* enters it. The grammatical continuum then confirms that field by preserving **⟪षुर्⟫**, realized as **⟪सुर्⟫**, with the meanings **ऐश्वर्यदीप्त्योः (*aiśvarya-dīptyoḥ*)** — sovereignty and shining. Pāṇini's documentation presupposes and uses the inherited inventory; it does not claim to have created it.

The argument therefore has two joined parts:

1. **Generativity establishes availability.** Sanskrit can generate ***asu-ra*** and ***a-sura*** without waiting for a separately stored lexical entry.
2. **Context identifies usage.** The praised sovereign sustains and releases life; Svarbhānu pierces Sūrya with darkness. The deed tells the two words apart.

The manuscript should state this directly. It should not concede a chronology in which one reading appeared after the other.

## 2. Core Passage

The primary evidence is Ṛgveda 5.40.5:

> यत्त्वा सूर्य स्वर्भानुस्तमसाविध्यदासुरः । अक्षेत्रविद्यथा मुग्धो भुवनान्यदीधयुः ॥
>
> *yat tvā sūrya svarbhānus tamasāvidhyad āsuraḥ |* *akṣetravid yathā mugdho bhuvanāny adīdhayuḥ ||*
>
> *When Svarbhānu the asura pierced you, O Sūrya, with darkness, the worlds looked about bewildered, like one who no longer knew the field.*

The surface form in the verse is ***āsuraḥ***, with long **ā**, an adjectival form built from *asura*. The lengthened vowel is not itself the privative. The deployment must make the actual argument: the underlying *asura* sound-form has more than one available Sanskrit derivation, and the action in this mantra identifies the adversarial ***a-sura*** reading.

## 3. Argument Sequence for the Body

Use this sequence wherever the complete argument appears:

1. Languages use words in context.
2. English *bound* can describe a captive held in chains or a hare leaping away. The sentence prevents confusion.
3. Sanskrit likewise supplies two derivations on the *asura* sound-form: ***asu-ra*** and ***a-sura***.
4. Sanskrit's architecture can generate the privative formation even when the positive base appears rarely or not at all in the surviving corpus.
5. Ṛgveda 5.40.5 supplies the decisive context: Svarbhānu pierces Sūrya with darkness, hides his radiance, and leaves the worlds unable to find their field.
6. Before making the final Svarbhānu argument, name the other Rigvedic antagonists described as *asura* or *āsura*: Pipru, Varcin, and Namuci. Their actions establish the antagonist field before etymology enters the discussion.
7. Varcin and Svarbhānu also demonstrate that a radiant name does not guarantee radiant conduct. A name may describe a capacity, title, or appearance; the action reveals how that capacity is used.
8. Śumbha supplies the later continuum example. His name belongs to a field of shining and splendour, while his actions enact containment and the desire to lord over others.
9. Return to Ṛgveda 5.40.5. Svarbhānu's action selects ***a-sura***, the un-shining and radiance-opposing word.
10. Laudatory passages in which an *asura* sustains, guards, measures, or releases activate ***asu-ra***, the breath-bearer.
11. Both readings operate within the Ṛgveda. No semantic reversal or internal chronology is needed.
12. Historical linguistics nevertheless grants itself thousands of starred PIE forms with no surviving occurrence while refusing a formation generated by Sanskrit's own architecture and selected by the mantra's action.

- **Namuci** is called *āsura* in Ṛgveda 10.131.4. The mantra says that the Aśvins separated the *surā-soma* while drinking it in Namuci's company and then helped Indra in his deeds. His name is associated with **न + ⟪मुच्⟫ (*na + muc*)**, the one who does not release. This Rigvedic mantra does not describe Namuci withholding anything. The later Vedic account makes that action explicit when Namuci withholds Indra's strength.
- **Svarbhānu**, called *āsura* in Ṛgveda 5.40.5 and 5.40.9, carries both ***svar*** and ***bhānu***, radiance, in his name while piercing Sūrya with darkness.

The body needs Pipru, Varcin, and Namuci only as a short sequence. Svarbhānu remains the complete demonstration. Exact translations, morphology, variant interpretations of the names, and the later Namuci account belong in the endnotes.

The sequence establishes a rule that should be stated plainly: **a name or title does not decide the moral direction of a capacity.** Brilliance, power, intelligence, and sovereignty are capacities. The action shows whether a being uses them to sustain what should flow or to contain and possess it.

### OLD DRAFT — 3.6 The Asura Analysis: Action, not Faction

use - English, like Sanskrit and many other languages, includes words that are identical in form but opposite in action. A captive lies *bound* — tied, pinned, unable to move a limb. A hare goes *bounding* over the hedge — leaping, springing, moving as freely as a thing can move. The captive is held fast; the hare leaps free. Both actions use the same syllable. No English speaker confuses the two: *the prisoner lay bound to the chair* and *watch the deer bound across the field* fall on opposite sides without a moment's thought. The sentence around the word tells the reader which word is being used.

Now put a philologist on the pyramid's payroll in front of that word — a man with an agenda. He announces that the captive who lies *bound* is in truth *free*, and that the reader who sees a prisoner is deluded. First you would doubt his competence. Then, remembering who signs his cheques, you would doubt his motive — and wonder what could drive so ridiculous a claim.

That is exactly what Western philology has done with the word **असुर (*asura*)**.

The pyramid's historical linguists tell the story in three stages.

First, they treat *asura* as one inherited word meaning "lord" or "powerful being" and link it to the Iranian *ahura*. This allows them to invent an imaginary people of an imaginary race, speaking an imaginary language filled with imaginary words, and then send one branch toward present-day Iran and another into northwestern India. They assign the Rigvedic passages in which Indra and Varuṇa act as protagonists to this supposedly original meaning. Yet the pyramid never describes Indra as a protagonist when it analyzes the word. It reduces him to a "lord" or "powerful being." The imaginary migrants can therefore enter India with *asura* still meaning "lord."

Second, they treat the Rigvedic passages in which Svarbhānu, Namuci, Pipru, and Varcin act as antagonists as evidence that the same word was beginning a semantic reversal. Their own chronology does not support a clean sequence: Svarbhānu appears in Mandala 5 and Varcin in Mandala 7, inside the family books the pyramid itself calls old. Yet the antagonist uses are still made to point toward a later meaning in which asura names beings opposed to the Devas.

The pyramid's vocabulary reveals this maneuver. It calls the Rigvedic uses for Indra and Varuṇa "positive" or "praised," while calling the uses for Svarbhānu and Namuci "negative" or "hostile." **Sanskrit is not naive about words with more than one meaning.** The discipline catalogued **नानार्थ (*nānārtha*)** and **अनेकार्थ (*anekārtha*)**, in which one word extends across several senses. The **अमरकोश (*Amarakośa*)** sets aside a **नानार्थवर्ग (*nānārtha-varga*)** for such words, and Hemacandra devoted the *Anekārtha-saṃgraha* to them. **गो (*go*)** can mean cow, earth, speech, or a ray of light. **हरि (*hari*)** can indicate Viṣṇu, Indra, a lion, a horse, or the color green.
