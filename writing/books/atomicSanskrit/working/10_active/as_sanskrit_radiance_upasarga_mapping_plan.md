# Sanskrit Radiance Through the Upasargas

## A Comparative Map of Sanskrit, Greek, Latin, and the PIE Intermediary

**Status:** Active research plan  
**Parent project:** [Sanskrit Radiance Mapping Project](as_sanskrit_radiance_mapping_project_proposal.md)  
**Provisional book deployment:** A compact worked case in Chapter 19 §19.7; the full inventory and source record in Appendix Part 1; a later public research dataset under the Sanskrit Radiance Mapping Project.

## 1. Purpose

The Sanskrit Radiance Mapping Project currently begins with a Sanskrit atom and follows the molecules generated from it into receiving languages. Upasargas require a parallel map. They do not contribute a lexical meaning alone; they change the direction, extent, completion, relation, or force of an action expressed through a Sanskrit atom. Greek and Latin preserve conspicuously similar particles, prepositions, and verbal prefixes:

| Sanskrit | Greek | Latin | Shared field |
|---|---|---|---|
| **अप (*apa*)** | **ἀπό (*apó*)** | ***ab- / abs-*** | away, off, from |
| **परि (*pari*)** | **περί (*perí*)** | ***per-*** | around, through |
| **प्र (*pra*)** | **πρό (*pró*)** | ***pro-*** | forward, before |
| **उप (*upa*)** | **ὑπό (*hypó*)** | ***sub-*** | under, toward, near |

These correspondences raise a larger architectural issue. If Sanskrit atoms traveled outward and became botanical in receiving languages, the directional operators attached to those atoms may also have traveled. The research must therefore examine the transmission of an operating system, not merely the resemblance of isolated words.

## 2. The Pyramid's Account

Comparative philology does not say that Sanskrit **अप (*apa*)** became Greek **ἀπό (*apó*)** or Latin ***ab***. It reconstructs an unrecorded PIE particle, commonly written **\*h₂epo**, and makes the three recorded forms its independent descendants:

> **\*h₂epo → Sanskrit *apa* / Greek *apó* / Latin *ab***

Within that account:

1. the reconstructed form meant "away," "off," or "from";
2. an Indo-Iranian vowel merger turned the final **o** into Sanskrit **a**;
3. Greek retained **o** in ***apó***; and
4. Latin lost the final vowel and voiced **p** to **b**, producing ***ab*** and related forms.

The Latin path deserves explicit scrutiny. One recent comparative discussion calls the derivation from **\*h₂epo** semantically attractive while noting that it requires irregular loss of final **o** and voicing of **p** to **b**. The research record must preserve that qualification rather than presenting the Latin form as an automatic result.

The same framework reconstructs PIE "local particles." It proposes that they began as independent spatial adverbs and later became adpositions, prepositions, and verbal prefixes in the descendant languages. Early Vedic and Homeric Greek receive special importance because their particles can remain separate from the verb. The pyramid treats this shared mobility as inherited PIE behavior.

## 3. The Radiance Account to Test

The competing account begins with recorded architecture:

1. Sanskrit preserves a defined set of upasargas within an operating, generative system of atoms and molecules.
2. The Vedic domain displays their mobility and their semantic relation to action.
3. The laukika domain displays their productive attachment to atoms across a vast generated vocabulary.
4. Greek and Latin preserve related particles, prepositions, and verbal prefixes, but the elements become botanical within each receiving language.
5. The starred PIE particle is assembled from those recorded forms and then placed above them as their parent.

The research will test whether Sanskrit's complete architecture, recurrent sound paths, semantic continuity, and plausible transmission routes explain the correspondences more fully than the reconstructed intermediary.

This is an extension of **vivimorphosis**. A Sanskrit atom may travel with one or more directional operators. Once received elsewhere, the operator can become an independent preposition, a productive prefix, a fused part of a word, or a form whose earlier relation to action is no longer visible.

### 3.1 Multiple Upasargas

Sanskrit can place more than one upasarga before the same atom, and each operator can contribute a different part of the resulting movement or meaning. The order is architectural. The operator nearest the atom establishes the first relation; an operator placed outside that combination can modify the composite as a larger unit.

A strict survey of the Ṛgveda identifies **116** two-upasarga composites in **186** passages, formed from **52** different combinations. The most frequent include **अभि + प्र (*abhi + pra*)**, **अभि + आ (*abhi + ā*)**, **अनु + प्र (*anu + pra*)**, **उप + प्र (*upa + pra*)**, and **सम् + आ (*sam + ā*)**. The study describes the pattern as highly productive and only lightly conventionalized: most combinations occur with relatively few atoms, while the operators usually continue to make separate contributions to the resulting meaning. The Rigvedic sample uses pairs; laukika Sanskrit also uses sequences of three.

Greek preserves a substantial part of this architecture. A comparable survey finds **64** multiple-preverb composites in **138** passages of the Homeric poems. Examples include:

| Greek composite | Operator sequence | Literal architecture | Resulting meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| ***ep-ana-títhēmi*** | on + up + place | on-up-place | shut |
| ***ex-apo-díōmai*** | out + away + drive | out-away-drive | drive out of |
| ***amphi-peri-stéphomai*** | on both sides + around + crown | both-sides-around-crown | place around as a crown |
| ***eis-ana-baínō*** | into + up + go | into-up-go | go upward into |
| ***ex-up-an-ístēmi*** | out + under + up + stand | out-under-up-stand | start up from under |

Homeric Greek also allows the operators to remain apart from the verb. Meter sometimes preserves a boundary between them, and the augment can appear between the operator sequence and the verbal base. These features closely resemble the Vedic situation: the elements have not yet collapsed into an indivisible prefix string. Greek therefore carries far more than cognate-looking particles. It carries multiple operators, meaningful ordering, mobility, and compositional interaction with the verb. Modern Greek continues to permit multiple preverbation, although its contemporary categories and constraints require a separate analysis.

Latin carries a broad operator inventory but much less of the transparent stacking system. One comparative catalogue identifies fourteen elements that operate as both prepositions and verbal prefixes, including ***ab, ad, ante, cum/con-, de, ex, in, ob, per, prae, pro, sub, super,*** and ***trans***, together with prefix-only ***dis-*** and ***red-***. Multiple layers do occur, but they are commonly lexicalized. Latin ***expōnō***, "put out" or "set out," is analyzed historically as **ex + po + sinō**; by the recorded Latin stage, ***pōnō*** functions as a verbal base and the inner operator is no longer available to an ordinary speaker as a separately readable component.

The comparison therefore produces a gradient:

| Feature | Sanskrit | Greek | Latin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Large operator inventory | fully visible | substantially preserved | substantially preserved |
| More than one operator on an atom or verb | productive | clearly preserved | possible but much less productive |
| Three-operator sequences | present in laukika Sanskrit | preserved in some Greek forms | uncommon and usually opaque |
| Operators separable from the verb | visible in the Vedic domain | visible in Homeric Greek | limited remnants |
| Each operator remains semantically readable | often | often | frequently reduced by lexicalization |
| Productive ordering architecture | extensive | substantial | weak or fossilized |

The numerical counts cannot be converted into a percentage of Sanskrit retained by Greek because the surveyed texts differ in length, genre, and selection criteria. They still establish the architectural result needed here: Greek preserves multiple upasargas as a functioning design principle, while Latin mainly preserves the inventory and the products left after fusion.

For the Radiance Thesis, this gradient becomes a prediction. A receiving language closer to the transmitted architecture should preserve operator mobility, stacking, ordering, and separate semantic contributions. As vivimorphosis proceeds, the same operators should fuse with verbs, lose independent meanings, and become lexical material. Greek stands nearer the architectural end of that path; Latin stands farther into botanical fusion.

### 3.2 Direction Through Architectural Completeness

Multiple operators alone cannot establish the direction of transmission. Homeric Greek preserves substantial stacking, Old Irish can stack several preverbs, and Slavic languages preserve productive prefix combinations. Raw quantity would therefore produce a weak argument. The stronger comparison begins with the complete architecture in which the operators function.

Sanskrit coordinates:

1. a defined operator inventory;
2. a defined inventory of atoms;
3. productive **upasarga + atom** composition;
4. further derivation through *pratyayas*;
5. a *varṇamālā* and sound operations that regulate the boundaries;
6. mobile deployment in the vaidika domain;
7. attached, generative deployment in the laukika domain; and
8. an internal functional analysis that can classify the same form as *nipāta, gati, karmapravacanīya,* or *upasarga* according to what it does.

Pāṇini 1.4.59, **उपसर्गाः क्रियायोगे (*upasargāḥ kriyāyoge*)**, is load-bearing here. The operator becomes an *upasarga* through its relation to an action. Pāṇini documents a functional architecture rather than assigning every particle one permanent lexical category.

The research must ask whether any proposed non-Indic source preserves this whole bundle. If a receiving language preserves a subset, the complete architecture can explain how that subset arose. The fragment cannot, by itself, generate an explanation for the complete architecture.

> **The architecture predicts the fragment. The fragment cannot predict the architecture.**

This principle also exposes a difficulty inside the PIE reconstruction. Comparative philology can reconstruct individual particles such as **\*h₂epo, \*peri,** and **\*pro**, but the multiple-preverb system does not resolve into an equally complete reconstructed architecture. The published comparative record reports that no single multiple-preverb combination occurs across every sampled language, that the ordering of operators does not follow one simple inherited rule, and that some apparent matches may be calques. The proposed parent therefore supplies isolated operators more readily than it supplies the full operating system.

Direction must consequently be tested through complete molecules rather than through isolated particles. The first candidate set is:

| Sanskrit architecture | Greek or Latin comparison | What must be verified |
|---|---|---|
| **प्र + ⟪इ⟫ (*pra + i*)** | Greek ***pro-eimi*** | operator, atom, generated family, and recorded usage |
| **अप + ⟪भृ⟫ (*apa + bhṛ*)** | Greek ***apo-pherō*** | sound path, semantic continuity, and narrowing |
| **प्र + ⟪भृ⟫ (*pra + bhṛ*)** | Greek ***pro-pherō***; Latin ***pro-ferre*** | recurrence of the same atom under another operator |
| **परि + ⟪भृ⟫ (*pari + bhṛ*)** | Greek ***peri-pherō*** | whether the operator sequence remains readable |
| **सम् + ⟪धा⟫ (*sam + dhā*)** | Greek ***syn-tithēmi***; Latin ***con-dere*** | whether this is one architecture, analogy, or separate formation |
| **प्रति + ⟪स्था⟫ (*prati + sthā*)** | Greek ***anti-stasis***; Latin ***re-sistere*** | operator substitution, atom continuity, and semantic field |

These rows are candidates, not completed findings. A strong case will require an independently recorded Sanskrit atom, a productive operator family, Vedic usage, related molecules under several operators, a plausible route of transmission, and a narrower or less transparent result in the receiving language.

### 3.3 Vibhakti, Upasarga, and Order-Flexible Syntax

The operator system does not function alone. Sanskrit coordinates *vibhakti* with upasargas so that grammatical relations and the direction of action do not depend primarily on where a word appears in the sentence.

| Architectural element | What it contributes |
|---|---|
| ***Vibhakti*** | identifies a participant's relation to the action |
| ***Upasarga*** | changes the direction, extent, completion, or character of the action |
| **Word order** | carries emphasis, rhythm, focus, and the needs of discourse |

This makes Sanskrit **order-flexible**, rather than absolutely order-free. A noun carries its relation through its ending, while the action carries its operator. The sentence can therefore move its constituents for emphasis or meter without requiring position alone to recover the grammar.

The architecture changes when an action-operator becomes a noun-governing preposition. The preposition and its object form a local unit whose internal order matters. Greek and Latin still preserve case systems and therefore retain considerable freedom at the sentence level, but their particles increasingly create fixed local relationships. The full comparison should show this gradient:

| Language field | Relation marking | Action operators | Dependence on position |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sanskrit | *vibhakti* on participants | mobile or attached upasargas | low; order serves emphasis, meter, and discourse |
| Homeric Greek | strong cases | mobile and attached preverbs; prepositions also develop | growing local dependence |
| Latin | cases remain active | prepositions and increasingly fused prefixes | greater dependence on fixed local units |
| Later European languages | reduced case systems | prepositions and lexical prefixes | high dependence on position |

One comparative finding deserves special attention. The old local particles did not develop into primary adpositions in Indic in the same way that they did across most other branches placed under PIE. Later Indic postpositions often arose from nouns, verbs, and adverbs instead. Comparative scholarship explains this divergence through prosody and syntax. The Radiance account must test another explanation: Sanskrit retained the old elements inside its action architecture because *vibhakti* already carried the nominal relations.

### 3.4 The Pyramid's Sequence and Sanskrit's Two Deployments

The pyramid usually presents the history as a movement from independent spatial particles toward grammar:

> PIE local particles or spatial adverbs  
> → adpositions with nouns and preverbs with verbs  
> → fused verbal prefixes

The sequence often branches rather than proceeding as one straight line, but chronology remains its organizing assumption. Vedic mobility is treated as archaic, while the more regular attachment visible in later Sanskrit is treated as grammaticalization.

The book's account begins with one language engine operating across two permission domains. The Veda is read-only: the vaidika domain can keep an operator separate when scope, emphasis, or a passage's fixed form requires separation. Laukika Sanskrit is write-enabled for new composition: it can join the same operator to an atom and use the resulting molecule as part of an unlimited generative vocabulary. One architecture supplies two deployments for two purposes. Their coexistence does not require one to have evolved into the other.

The distinction requires one further clarification. A vaidika upasarga may be positionally separate, but it is textually fixed. The reciter does not decide where to place it. The Vedas preserve the passage, its accent, its sequence, its *padapāṭha*, and its recitational form. The rest of the grammar then allows the listener to bind the separated operator to the intended action. The invariant architecture can therefore permit separation without creating transmission instability.

Brāhmaṇa prose confirms that invariance is sufficient to support this freedom. It preserves separated operators in marked constructions:

| Vaidika prose | Separated architecture | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Jaiminīya-Brāhmaṇa 1.149.7 | **आ … विशति (*ā … viśati*)** | **एनं पुनर् मनः** intervenes |
| Aitareya-Brāhmaṇa 2.24.13 | **उप … गच्छति (*upa … gacchati*)** | **अग्नेः प्रियं धाम** intervenes |
| Jaiminīya-Brāhmaṇa 1.122.11 | **अव … रुन्धे (*ava … runddhe*)** | **पशून्** intervenes |
| Aitareya-Brāhmaṇa 5.11.2 | **आ … दत्ते; निर् … नुदते (*ā … datte; nir … nudate*)** | both operators are separated in the same prose passage |

These prose examples show that the vaidika system binds an operator through an invariant passage, grammar, semantics, accent, and discourse structure.

Laukika Sanskrit carries a different burden. It must generate prose, poetry, and sentences that have never been spoken before. If several operators could move freely among several actions, the listener would repeatedly have to determine which operator belonged to which atom. Attachment makes the relationship local, visible, and reusable. The laukika domain therefore binds the upasarga more strictly because it must support unlimited generation.

#### A Constructed Laukika Ambiguity Test

The body should demonstrate the problem by moving one operator between two actions that can both receive it:

| Construction | Binding | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **रामः नगरम् अभिगच्छति शत्रुं च हन्ति।** | **अभि + ⟪गम्⟫** | Rāma approaches the city and strikes the enemy. |
| **रामः नगरं गच्छति शत्रुं चाभिहन्ति।** | **अभि + ⟪हन्⟫** | Rāma goes to the city and attacks the enemy. |
| **अभि रामः नगरं गच्छति शत्रुं च हन्ति।** | operator left free | A newly generated laukika sentence no longer states locally whether **अभि** belongs to **गच्छति** or **हन्ति**. |

This is a constructed illustration, not a quoted Sanskrit passage. Its purpose is to show why free placement scales poorly in an open generative domain. An invariant vaidika passage never asks a reciter to choose among these arrangements: the sequence and intended relation have already been preserved. Laukika Sanskrit must make the relation clear while generating new expressions, so the operator joins the molecule.

This distinction should be tied directly to Chapter 0's treatment of vaidika and laukika Sanskrit. The upasarga study can then show the distinction operating in a concrete part of the language rather than repeating the domain framework in the abstract.

### 3.5 Domain Flattening in the Receiving Languages

The Radiance Thesis must account for what traveled and what did not. Sanskrit's operators, molecules, and patterns of use could reach another speech-field through recitation, teaching, trade, travel, or bilingual contact. The surrounding vaidika–laukika architecture required a civilizational preservation system and was less likely to travel intact.

The receiving language could therefore encounter both Sanskrit deployments without retaining the reason for their difference:

| Sanskrit source | Governing purpose | Botanical outcome after domain flattening |
|---|---|---|
| mobile operator in the vaidika domain | scope, emphasis, and the fixed form of an invariant corpus | free particle, adverb, or noun-governing adposition |
| attached operator in the laukika domain | local binding for open-ended vocabulary and sentence generation | preverb, prefix, or fused part of a lexical item |

This process should be called **domain flattening** in the research plan. The two Sanskrit deployments enter one receiving speech-field without the domain protocol that explains why one remains mobile and the other attaches. The language then redistributes the inherited forms according to its own syntax and usage.

The body treatment should preserve the following bullet hammer:

> This strengthens the Radiance Thesis because it now explains both outcomes:
>
> - why related particles remain mobile in Greek;
> - why the same particles attach to verbs;
> - why some become noun-governing prepositions;
> - why Latin combinations become opaque through fusion; and
> - why comparative philology mistakes these distributed remnants for evidence of an earlier, less organized parent system.

The claim should not be compressed into “prepositions evolved from upasargas.” Pāṇini identifies a form as an *upasarga* through its relation to an action. The more accurate account concerns a Sanskrit **operator family** whose mobile and attached deployments were redistributed as adverbs, adpositions, preverbs, and prefixes after the domain distinction disappeared.

Candidate body prose:

> Sanskrit knew why the operator remained separate in one domain and attached in the other. The vaidika corpus could preserve separation because the passage, accent, sequence, and recitation kept every relationship intact. Laukika Sanskrit attached the operator to its atom because an open generative language needed that relationship to remain immediately recoverable. When Sanskrit's radiance reached other speech-fields, the forms traveled more easily than the domain architecture that governed them. The distinction flattened. Mobile operators became adverbs and prepositions; attached operators became preverbs and prefixes. The receiving languages preserved the pieces while losing the reason Sanskrit deployed them differently.

Domain flattening now becomes the first operation in this account of vivimorphosis. Sound change, semantic change, externalization, and opaque fusion proceed after the receiving language has lost the distinction that governed the two Sanskrit deployments.

### 3.6 Prākritization in Two Directions

When a receiving language no longer maintains the calibrated relationship among operators, atoms, endings, and sound boundaries, the architecture can become less transparent in two apparently opposite ways:

| Direction | What changes | Example to develop |
|---|---|---|
| **Externalization** | information formerly encoded in an ending or bound element moves into a separate word | Sanskrit case endings replaced by Hindi postpositions |
| **Opaque fusion** | an operator and verb fuse so completely that speakers no longer recognize the inner components | Latin ***expōnō*** from **ex + po + sinō**, with ***pōnō*** becoming an indivisible base |

Hindi does not literally detach Sanskrit *pratyayas*. It often replaces functions once carried by Sanskrit inflectional endings with separate postpositions:

| Sanskrit | Hindi | Relation externalized |
|---|---|---|
| **रामस्य (*rāmasya*)** | **राम का (*Rām kā*)** | possession or relation |
| **रामाय (*rāmāya*)** | **राम को (*Rām ko*)** | recipient or goal |
| **रामे (*rāme*)** | **राम में (*Rām meṃ*)** | location |
| **रामेण (*rāmeṇa*)** | **राम से (*Rām se*)** | instrument or source |

Externalization and opaque fusion look unlike each other on the page, but both make the original coordination harder to see. This gives the book a more precise account of prākritization than a one-way claim that every particle simply separated or every operator simply fused.

Candidate body prose:

> The pyramid mistakes two Sanskrit deployments for stages of evolution. In the vaidika domain, an upasarga can remain mobile because the invariant mantra preserves its position while *vibhakti* identifies the participants. In the laukika domain, the same operator joins an atom so that an open generative language can keep the relationship immediately recoverable. When another speech-field receives the forms without this domain distinction, prākritization can loosen the remaining coordination in either direction: an encoded relation becomes a separate word, as Sanskrit case endings often become Hindi postpositions, or the whole molecule fuses until its internal architecture disappears, as it often does in Latin. Separation and opaque fusion look opposite on the page, but both make the calibrated relationship among the parts less visible.

### 3.7 The Script Parallel

The same directional test applies to the script argument developed in Appendix Part 3. The pyramid begins with Aramaic consonantal writing, calls it the source, and describes Brāhmī's vowel architecture as a later Indian improvement. The book's account starts with the sonomeric system that the script renders:

1. independent vowels;
2. an inherent **अ (*a*)**;
3. *mātrās* that modify that vowel;
4. consonant-vowel *akṣaras*; and
5. conjuncts that preserve articulated clusters.

Under this account, Brāhmī's vowel marks were not an isolated act of graphic cleverness. They gave a visible interface to a sound architecture that already existed. Aramaic preserves the reduced consonantal fragment; Brāhmī renders the fuller system.

> The same reversal appears in language and script. Sanskrit preserves operators, atoms, endings, and the sound-grid that coordinates them; Greek and Latin preserve fragments that became botanical. The sonomeric architecture likewise precedes the written interface, while Aramaic writing preserves only part of what Brāhmī renders. The pyramid begins with the fragment, calls it the source, and treats the complete architecture as a later improvement. Yet the complete system explains how the fragment arose, while the fragment cannot explain the system.

The upasarga treatment should use this as a concise cross-reference rather than duplicate Appendix Part 3's full Brāhmī argument.

## 4. What Must Be Demonstrated

Resemblance begins the investigation; it cannot complete it. Each proposed family must establish:

1. **Sanskrit function:** how the element operates in Vedic and laukika Sanskrit.
2. **Generated range:** the atoms and molecules with which the upasarga combines and the semantic changes it produces.
3. **Receiving-language function:** whether the Greek or Latin form operates as a free particle, preposition, preverb, prefix, or several of these.
4. **Sound path:** what changes in vowels and consonants, described first through Sanskrit's sound categories and then through the receiving language's recorded history.
5. **Semantic range:** where the meanings coincide, narrow, widen, or separate.
6. **Transmission route:** which contact fields could have carried the element and its usage.
7. **Recurrence:** whether the same sound and semantic movements appear across several upasargas.
8. **Conventional account:** the exact PIE reconstruction and every regular or irregular operation required to produce the recorded forms.
9. **Complete molecule:** whether the operator can be followed with the same atom into one or more receiving languages.
10. **Architectural depth:** whether the receiving language preserves only a lexical item or also preserves operator ordering, derivation, mobility, and semantic composition.
11. **Relation to case:** whether the language marks participant relations through endings, adpositions, position, or a combination.
12. **Domain:** whether the Sanskrit example belongs to vaidika mobility, laukika generation, or both.
13. **Botanical outcome:** whether the receiving form remains transparent, becomes a preposition, externalizes a relation, or fuses into an opaque lexical base.
14. **Domain transfer:** whether contact could have transmitted the operator while leaving the vaidika–laukika protocol behind.
15. **Flattened category:** whether the receiving language redistributed the form as an adverb, adposition, preverb, prefix, or opaque lexical element.

### 4.1 Guardrails Against a Weak Directional Claim

The research must not:

1. treat multiple-preverb stacking as uniquely Sanskrit;
2. infer direction from resemblance or complexity alone;
3. turn survey counts from unlike corpora into retention percentages;
4. force every Greek or Latin particle into a Sanskrit family;
5. treat an unresolved row as evidence for either side; or
6. describe vaidika mobility and laukika attachment as chronological stages merely because that sequence is conventional in comparative philology.

The case for direction must rest on recurrence, complete molecules, architectural integration, semantic development, geography, contact, and the relative transparency of each system.

### 5.1 Recorded Forms

| Language or account | Form | Approximate sound | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sanskrit | **अप (*apa*)** | **अप** | away, off; particle and upasarga |
| Greek | **ἀπό (*apó*)** | **अपो** | from, away from; preposition and preverb |
| Latin | ***ab / abs*** | **अब् / अब्स्** | from, away from; preposition and prefix |
| PIE image | **\*h₂epo** | reconstructed | from, away |

### 5.2 Mainstream Derivation

The full source record must state:

- why **\*h₂epo** rather than the recorded Sanskrit form is assigned parenthood;
- which comparison establishes the initial laryngeal;
- which general Indo-Iranian vowel operation produces Sanskrit **a** from the reconstructed final **o**;
- whether Latin's final-vowel loss is regular in this particle;
- whether the change **p → b** is regular in the stated environment; and
- how ***abs-, au-, ā-,*** and other Latin outcomes relate to ***ab***.

### 5.3 Sanskrit Architecture

The Sanskrit side must show **अप** operating with several atoms rather than standing as an isolated dictionary entry. The first record should include Vedic and laukika examples that display:

- physical separation or movement away;
- removal, deprivation, reversal, or exclusion;
- semantic extension beyond literal space; and
- the degree to which **अप** remains mobile or becomes joined to the atom.

Pāṇini belongs after the examples as the documenter of the operating distinctions. The Vedic evidence must establish that he did not create the upasarga or its behavior.

### 5.4 Comparative Result

The pilot will place the two explanations beside one another:

| Account | Starting point | Proposed movement |
|---|---|---|
| PIE descent | reconstructed **\*h₂epo** | one unrecorded particle becomes Sanskrit *apa*, Greek *apó*, and Latin *ab* |
| Sanskritic radiance | recorded Sanskrit **अप (*apa*)** within the upasarga architecture | the operator reaches Greek and Latin speech-fields and becomes botanical there |

The result must identify what each account explains, what it assumes, and where it requires an irregular operation. The conclusion will remain provisional until several upasarga families display recurrent paths.

## 12. Intended Section Claim

The eventual section should establish the research problem without pretending that one resemblance settles historical direction:

> The pyramid calls Sanskrit **अप**, Greek **ἀπό**, and Latin ***ab*** three descendants of the imaginary **\*h₂epo**. Sanskrit supplies more than one member of that comparison. It preserves the operator inside a generative architecture, shows it acting upon recorded atoms, coordinates it with *vibhakti* and derivation, and deploys it differently across the vaidika and laukika domains. When Sanskrit's radiance reached other speech-fields, the operators traveled more easily than the domain architecture that governed them. Greek redistributed the mobile and attached forms as particles, prepositions, and preverbs; Latin preserved a broad inventory whose older combinations often became opaque through fusion. The Sanskrit Radiance Mapping Project tests whether domain flattening and vivimorphosis explain these distributed remnants more fully than an imaginary parent assembled from them. The architecture predicts the fragments. The fragments do not predict the architecture.
