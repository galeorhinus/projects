## 8.1 One Language, Two Preservation Burdens

Sanskrit had to remain unchanged while continuing to serve a changing world.
Exact preservation protects what has already been received, while continuing
use requires words and expressions for circumstances that no earlier
composition could have listed in advance. Sanskrit meets these different
demands through one architecture operating in two domains.

The Vedas encode far more than Sanskrit's language engine. This book
concentrates on that one layer: the Vedic encoding of Sanskrit's sounds, atoms,
operations, relations, and generative grammar. Later volumes in the *Second
Shanti* series will examine other architectures preserved in the Vedas.

Computer systems provide a useful comparison because they distinguish between
information that may be used and information that may be changed. A read-only
file can be opened, consulted, and run, but its contents cannot be overwritten.
A write-enabled space allows users to create and revise new material. Sanskrit
makes a comparable distinction between its two domains. The ***वैदिक
(*vaidika*)*** corpus remains read-only: people recite it, study it, analyze it,
and use it as a calibrant, while its transmitted content stays unchanged. The
***लौकिक (*laukika*)*** domain is write-enabled: speakers use the same Sanskrit
architecture to create new words, poems, arguments, sciences, and stories.

The comparison concerns permissions applied to content. It does not divide
Sanskrit into two engines. The sonomers, atoms, affixes, case relations,
compounds, and sentence operations belong to one language architecture. The
*vaidika* domain preserves a fixed body of expression under exact transmission
requirements. The *laukika* domain applies the same architecture to an open
number of expressions that may never have appeared before.

The read-only permission keeps the Veda beyond revision without making its
mantras silent or inert. They continue to sound as students learn them, teachers
explain them, reciters compare them, and analytical disciplines expose their
internal operations. A later composer cannot revise a received passage or add a
new composition to the Veda, while the Veda continues to serve as the primary
measure against which Sanskrit remains calibrated.

The word *bounded* applies only after a mantra enters exact transmission. The
mantras were seen by ***मन्त्रद्रष्टारः (*mantra-draṣṭāraḥ*)*** — ṛṣis and
ṛṣikās — but we do not know when each mantra was seen, when it entered its
Veda, or whether mantras preserved in one Veda were seen before those preserved
in another. The read-only comparison begins with the preservation duty: a
received mantra may be recited, studied, and decoded, but not rewritten.

Pāṇini's scope markers prevent a familiar chronological substitution. Terms
such as ***छन्दसि (*chandasi*)*** and ***भाषायाम् (*bhāṣāyām*)*** state where
an operation applies. The *chandasi* rules help students understand the Sanskrit
preserved in the Vedas, while the *bhāṣāyām* rules help them understand laukika
compositions and create new ones. The Vedas did not become rigid because Pāṇini
documented their language. Their received form was already under exact
transmission; his analysis made its operations explicit. *Vaidika* and
*laukika* describe the broad civilizational domains, while *chandasi* and
*bhāṣāyām* mark operational scopes inside Pāṇini's analysis. The pairs remain
related without becoming interchangeable, and neither pair describes one
Sanskrit changing into another.

## 8.2 The Shared Architecture

The Vedic corpus and laukika Sanskrit sound different often enough that a
student trained only in laukika usage needs additional instruction before
approaching many Vedic passages. Accent, contextual articulations, verbal
resources, alternate endings, and inherited recitational requirements all
contribute to that experience. Those visible differences operate inside a
shared system rather than replacing it.

The opening of the Ṛgveda already displays the architecture:

> अग्निमीळे पुरोहितं यज्ञस्य देवमृत्विजम् ।  
> होतारं रत्नधातमम् ॥
>
> *agnim īḷe purohitaṃ yajñasya devam ṛtvijam |*  
> *hotāraṃ ratnadhātamam ||*
>
> I praise Agni, the household priest, the divine priest of the *yajña*, the
> invoker, the finest giver of treasure.

The passage uses recognizable case relations, a finite verb, compounds, sound
junctions, and the same sonomeric organization that operates in laukika
Sanskrit. Its recitational form also preserves information that ordinary
laukika writing does not require, including pitch accent and the Ṛgvedic
realization **ळ** in **ईळे (*īḷe*)**. The additional specification does not
erase the common architecture beneath it.

The Devī Sūkta provides another compact example:

> अहं रुद्रेभिर्वसुभिश्चराम्यहमादित्यैरुत विश्वदेवैः ।
>
> *ahaṃ rudrebhir vasubhiś carāmy aham ādityair uta viśvadevaiḥ |*
>
> I move with the Rudras and the Vasus; I move with the Ādityas and all the
> Devas.

Here **अहम् (*aham*)** is the first-person pronoun and **चरामि
(*carāmi*)** is a first-person verb. The ***तृतीया बहुवचनम्
(*tṛtīyā bahuvacanam*)*** — instrumental plural — forms **रुद्रेभिः
(*rudrebhiḥ*)**, **वसुभिः (*vasubhiḥ*)**, **आदित्यैः (*ādityaiḥ*)**, and
**विश्वदेवैः (*viśvadevaiḥ*)** show more than one formation operating inside a
single mantra. A student may need the Vedic setting to understand every detail,
but the sentence does not become a different language merely because the domain
preserves a wider range of forms.

The common architecture can be followed from the mouth outward:

| Layer | Shared Sanskrit architecture |
|---|---|
| Sound | articulated sonomers organized by place and effort |
| Junction | governed changes when sounds meet |
| Atom | semantic constituents that support further formation |
| Molecule | atoms joined with operators and affixes |
| Nominal form | gender, number, and case relations |
| Verbal form | person, number, action, time, and mode |
| Sentence | recoverable relations under flexible ordering |
| Composition | compounds, derivation, repetition, meter, and style |
| Transmission | teaching, analysis, comparison, correction, and memory |

Each domain draws on this architecture according to its task. The *vaidika*
domain preserves every required realization in its inherited place. The
*laukika* domain relies on operations that speakers can apply repeatedly while
creating new expression. One domain can preserve a contextual sound
or alternate ending that the other does not need to promote into ordinary
productive use.

Pāṇini recorded this distribution with exceptional precision. His
*Aṣṭādhyāyī* can mark one operation ***chandasi*** and another
***bhāṣāyām*** because both belong inside the field he analyzed. He did not
invent the architecture that the Vedas already display. Sanskrit was
engineered, encoded in the Vedas, and decoded by many; Pāṇini's decoding is the
finest.

## 8.3 The Vaidika Preservation System

The *vaidika* domain preserves more than the words themselves. It preserves the
sequence of the words, the sounds at their junctions, the duration of vowels,
the placement of pitch, and the received forms associated with each
***शाखा (*śākhā*)***.
Because these features travel together, a reciter inherits an expression whose
parts continue to constrain one another.

A written text can lose a sound while leaving the letters apparently intact,
whereas recitation makes that loss audible. The student recites before a
teacher, other students, senior reciters, and a community familiar with the
received form. Since that distributed community already possesses the auditory
measure, it can detect a deviation and correct it as it occurs. Meter, accent,
sequence, and the different ***पाठ (*pāṭha*)*** forms provide additional checks
because an error that passes through one channel may still disturb another.

The analytical disciplines explain the same material from several directions.
***शिक्षा (*śikṣā*)*** describes sound and instruction, while the
***प्रातिशाख्य (*Prātiśākhya*)*** texts document the phonetic requirements of
particular Vedic corpora. ***छन्दस् (*chandas*)*** examines measured
composition; ***निरुक्त (*nirukta*)*** unfolds words through their actions and
formations; and ***व्याकरणम् (*vyākaraṇam*)*** analyzes the language operations
that make the expressions intelligible. These disciplines allow distributed
scholars, teachers, and students to inspect the reference corpus with increasing
precision without creating an office authorized to revise it.

The read-only permission enables a kind of local precision because exact
transmission preserves the passage's sequence. If an operator stands apart from
its atom, the inherited sequence, grammatical relations, recitational form, and
explanation continue to identify its function. If a sound appears only under a
specific junction, the passage continues to reproduce that junction. If two
forms overlap, accent and inherited context can help preserve their intended
function.

This local precision does not make the Veda grammatically loose. It means that
the complete preserved environment contributes information. A modern engineer
would recognize the same distinction between data interpreted inside a fixed
protocol and data expected to travel through an unrestricted interface. The
first can rely on conditions that accompany every use. The second must make its
relationships recoverable whenever a new user creates a new combination.

Vedic prose shows that *chandasi* covers more than meter. Pāṇini's *chandasi*
rules include operations found in Brāhmaṇa prose, where syllable count does not
compel their form. The domain preserves a complete received environment rather
than a set of metrical options alone.

## 8.4 The Laukika Preservation System

The *laukika* domain faces another burden. Its speakers must discuss objects,
institutions, sciences, arguments, and circumstances that no fixed corpus could
have listed beforehand. A language that changed its architecture whenever the
world changed would eventually lose the measure it was meant to preserve.
Laukika Sanskrit instead allows usage to expand while the architecture remains
stable.

Its generative capacity begins with a finite set of reusable resources.
Sonomers combine into larger sound-forms, while atoms combine with affixes and
operators. Nominal endings mark relations, verbal forms identify action,
person, number, and mode, and compounds join several concepts into a new
molecule. Speakers can repeat these operations to create a valid word or
sentence that no earlier composition contains.

This is the productive kernel: a reusable set of operations that creates new
expression without requiring an authority to amend the language each time the
world presents something new. A newly coined laukika word must still conform to
the architecture. Its novelty lies in the combination and use, not in a
decision to replace the sonomer grid, abandon the atoms, or revise case
relations.

The surviving laukika corpus follows another path from the Veda because new
compositions can enter it. Communities continue to copy, teach, perform, quote,
and explain material they value, while other material may be released,
forgotten, destroyed, or displaced. The corpus becomes selected, accretive, and
lossy even as the Sanskrit architecture remains calibrated. The language is
invariant; the content forms a tended field.

Stable architecture and open generation allow Sanskrit to remain worldly
without becoming botanical. A natural language adapts by changing through
ordinary use across generations. Laukika Sanskrit adapts through generation:
speakers apply a stable architecture to new circumstances. Its ability to meet
the world does not depend on turning every inherited form into raw material for
uncontrolled drift.

Sanskrit preserves this arrangement through more than rules. A constructed
language may begin with a compact grammar and a planned vocabulary, yet later
speakers can revise both if no civilizational calibrant commands long-term
responsibility. Sanskrit joins generative architecture to ***संस्कृति
(*saṃskṛti*)***: people
preserve the language because it carries a larger purpose, and the Veda provides
a reference that no later speaker may rewrite for convenience.

## 8.5 How Calibration Moves Between the Domains

The Vedas encode Sanskrit's architecture without listing every possible
laukika word. A finite reference corpus cannot contain all outputs of an
unbounded generative system, but it can preserve the invariants in operation.
Later analysts can then identify the sonomers, atoms, affixes, relations, and
transformations from which new expressions can be formed.

Calibration moves from the preserved Vedic reference into laukika generation.
The analytical disciplines decode and explain the operations already present in
the Veda, while teachers and students apply them when learning and generating
laukika Sanskrit. New expression can then be compared with the architecture
without being inserted into the reference corpus.

Keeping calibration directional protects the architecture from two opposite
failures. If the reference corpus could be rewritten, each generation could
alter the measure and then declare itself correct. If the language could not
generate beyond the reference corpus, it would become unable to serve changing
circumstances. Sanskrit keeps the reference beyond revision while allowing new
composition outside it.

The preserved Vedic reference also explains why documentation never became
codification. Codification would place an external authority above a drifting
language and make that authority the source of order. Sanskrit begins with the
opposite arrangement: the order already operates in the Veda and in the
language, while the analyst makes it explicit. Pāṇini's achievement lies in the
depth and precision of his decoding, not in supposedly stabilizing a language
that lacked form before him.

Correction follows the same direction because a later composition can expose an
analytical possibility, demonstrate a derivation, or teach the architecture
with unusual clarity without altering the Vedic expression that preserves the
measure. Calibration permits explanation to expand while preventing commentary
from becoming a new apex.

## 8.6 One Society, Deliberately Separated Responsibilities

The same people can participate in both domains. A person may learn a Vedic
recitational form and also speak, teach, analyze, or compose laukika Sanskrit. A
household may preserve a śākhā while remembering stories associated with its
***कुलदेवता (*kuladevatā*)***. A teacher may explain a mantra, teach
vyākaraṇam, and write a new commentary. The boundary applies to what may be
revised, not to who may learn.

Since the same people can participate in both domains, the two permissions
divide responsibilities rather than populations. Vedic affiliation assigns an
exact preservation duty to a lineage, while laukika participation allows the
same people to engage philosophy, medicine, mathematics, poetry,
administration, drama, story, and public teaching. Knowledge can move between
these activities even though a new poem or commentary can never be inserted
into the received Veda.

The historical details varied across regions, households, śākhās, and
***सम्प्रदाय (*sampradāya*)*** affiliations. A full account must be built from
specific cases rather than a claim that every Brahmin household performed the
same set of tasks. The following matrix identifies the evidence such a study
should collect:

| Responsibility | Evidence to collect |
|---|---|
| Vedic preservation | Veda, śākhā, recitational specialization |
| Domestic life | associated ***गृह्यसूत्र (*Gṛhyasūtra*)*** and household observances |
| Kula and sampradāya | *kuladevatā*, philosophical affiliation, teaching lineage |
| Laukika memory | ***इतिहास-पुराण (*itihāsa-purāṇa*)***, ***कथा (*kathā*)***, ***स्मृति (*smṛti*)***, and local narrative |
| Analysis | śikṣā, nirukta, vyākaraṇam, ***मीमांसा (*mīmāṃsā*)***, and commentary |
| Public activity | teaching, temple, court, medicine, mathematics, poetry, and administration |

The permission model allows people, knowledge, and analytical skill to move
across the boundary while keeping the inherited content beyond revision.
Historical case studies can show how different communities implemented that
distinction in practice.[NOTE: vaidika-laukika-household-responsibility-cases]

## 8.7 Sound: What Earns Reusable Status

Chapter 9 established that the human mouth can produce more sounds than
Sanskrit's independent sonomer grid contains. A sound earns a reusable
coordinate only when it can support a distinct and productive role across the
architecture. Other sounds can remain available under governed conditions.

Ṛgvedic **ळ** shows how exact preservation operates. The opening mantra
contains **ईळे (*īḷe*)**, and the Ṛgvedic phonetic discipline documents the
environment in which **ड** becomes **ळ**, with a corresponding treatment of
**ढ**. The recitational form preserves the sound wherever the inherited passage
requires it. Laukika Sanskrit does not need to promote **ळ** into the ordinary
independent grid merely to prove that the mouth can pronounce it.

The [ɸ]-like articulation called ***उपध्मानीय (*upadhmānīya*)*** makes the
same principle visible at a sound junction. In Ṛgveda 1.1.2, **अग्निः
पूर्वेभिर् (*agniḥ pūrvebhir*)**, the breath of the ***विसर्ग
(*visarga*)*** before **प**
can be shaped at the lips and written **ᳶ**, yielding **अग्निᳶ
पूर्वेभिर्** in the relevant recitational rendering. The architecture preserves
the articulation under the condition that produces it.
Because the sound serves as a governed output at a boundary, the architecture
does not need to turn it into an independent sonomer available at every lexical
position.

These examples distinguish three operations that alphabetic descriptions often
collapse:

1. an independent sonomer occupies a reusable coordinate;
2. a contextual articulation appears when a stated sound environment produces
   it;
3. a lineage-preserved realization remains exact in the Vedic passages that
   require it.

The *vaidika* domain can preserve the second and third with complete fidelity
because the relevant passage and its transmission remain fixed. The *laukika*
domain relies on the first when speakers generate new words and sentences. Both
deployments reveal selection by function.

## 8.8 Operators: Fixed Context and Local Binding

An ***उपसर्ग (*upasarga*)*** changes the action expressed by an atom. Pāṇini first documented
the relationship in 1.4.59, ***उपसर्गाः क्रियायोगे (*upasargāḥ
kriyāyoge*)***: the listed particles function as operators when joined in
meaning with an action. He then stated the ordinary placement in 1.4.80,
***ते प्राग्धातोः (*te prāg dhātoḥ*)***, placing them before the atom.

The next two rules preserve a wider Vedic deployment. Rule 1.4.81,
***छन्दसि परेऽपि (*chandasi pare 'pi*)***, allows the operator to follow;
1.4.82, ***व्यवहिताश्च (*vyavahitāś ca*)***, allows separation. Since these
rules also operate in Vedic prose, the freedom cannot be explained away as a
concession to meter.

The Aitareya-Brāhmaṇa supplies a clear prose example:

> आ द्विषतो वसु दत्ते, निर् एनम् एभ्यः सर्वेभ्यो लोकेभ्यो नुदते, य एवं वेद ।
>
> *ā dviṣato vasu datte, nir enam ebhyaḥ sarvebhyo lokebhyo nudate, ya evaṃ
> veda.*
>
> Whoever knows in this way takes wealth from the hostile one and drives him out
> from all these worlds.

The operator **आ (*ā*)** stands apart from **दत्ते (*datte*)**, with
**द्विषतो वसु (*dviṣato vasu*)** between them. The operator **निर् (*nir*)**
likewise stands apart from **नुदते (*nudate*)**, separated by **एनम् एभ्यः
सर्वेभ्यः लोकेभ्यः (*enam ebhyaḥ sarvebhyaḥ lokebhyaḥ*)**. The prose
passage preserves both relationships because its complete sequence and
inherited explanation remain stable.[NOTE: aitareya-brahmana-separated-upasargas]

Open-ended laukika composition creates a different requirement. A new sentence
may contain several atoms that can accept the same operator. If the operator
floats among them, the reader must determine which action it modifies. Local
attachment keeps that relationship visible while allowing the larger sentence
to retain flexible order.

Consider a deliberately constructed illustration:

> रामः नगरम् अभिगच्छति शत्रुं च हन्ति ।  
> *rāmaḥ nagaram abhigacchati śatruṃ ca hanti.*  
> Rāma approaches the city and kills the enemy.

The attached **अभि (*abhi*)** belongs to **गच्छति (*gacchati*)**. If a writer
moves the operator beside the second action, **अभिहन्ति (*abhihanti*)**, it
changes that action instead. A fixed Vedic passage can preserve a separated
binding because no later reciter is free to rearrange the sequence. Laukika
composition allows the writer to create a new sequence, so local attachment
protects the intended molecule.

This distribution reverses the pyramid's evolutionary story. Comparative
philology treats free particles as earlier material that supposedly fused into
prefixes. Sanskrit's architecture already contains both deployments and assigns
them by scope. Pāṇini recorded a locally bound productive form and a wider
Vedic freedom inside the same analysis.

## 8.9 Nominal and Verbal Range

The same method can be applied to nominal endings and verbal resources, but each
form must first be explained inside its passage. A visible difference
establishes distribution without establishing age, decay, or loss.

The Devī Sūkta's *tṛtīyā bahuvacanam* forms show the method. **रुद्रेभिः
(*rudrebhiḥ*)** and **वसुभिः (*vasubhiḥ*)** use the longer **-ebhiḥ**
formation, while **आदित्यैः (*ādityaiḥ*)** and **विश्वदेवैः
(*viśvadevaiḥ*)** use **-aiḥ**. Both formations participate in the same
grammatical relation. Meter may help explain the selection in a particular
line, but the forms themselves demonstrate that the Vedic domain preserves more
than one available realization. Their coexistence cannot establish that one
form is a chronological ancestor of the other.

Pitch accent and ***प्लुत (*pluta*)*** duration require the same care. The recitation lineages
continue to preserve ***उदात्त (*udātta*)***, ***अनुदात्त
(*anudātta*)***, and ***स्वरित (*svarita*)*** because the Vedic expression
includes pitch information. Laukika writing and ordinary speech do not require
the same three-way marking. Describing accent as lost hides the
domain in which people continue to preserve it.

The ***लेट् (*leṭ*)*** verbal resource offers a more demanding test. Pāṇini
documented it under *chandasi* in 3.4.6-8 and related it to meanings otherwise
served by *liṅ*, with additional stated uses. He then devoted 3.4.94-100 to its
endings and their operations. That treatment shows an analyzed resource with a
defined scope.

Some first-person *leṭ* forms overlap in visible form with laukika
***लोट् (*loṭ*)***. For
the atom **⟪भू⟫ (*bhū*)**, both analyses can yield **भवानि, भवाव, भवाम
(*bhavāni, bhavāva, bhavāma*)**. The overlap supports an architectural test,
but it does not yet prove why a complete *leṭ* paradigm does not enter ordinary
laukika generation.

A productive verbal resource must provide enough benefit to justify every new
form and every additional ambiguity it introduces. The test should compare the
complete paradigms, the meanings carried by *leṭ*, the meanings already
available through *loṭ*, ***लिङ् (*liṅ*)***, ***आशीर्लिङ्
(*āśīrliṅ*)***, and ***लृट् (*lṛṭ*)***, the role of Vedic accent,
and the reader's ability to recover the intended analysis in a newly composed
sentence. If the added forms create broad overlap while contributing little
semantic range, keeping the complete paradigm outside ordinary laukika
generation would follow the same selection principle seen in the sonomer grid.
If the passages show a distinction that remains both useful and recoverable,
the explanation must change. The evidence, rather than the analogy, must decide
the result.[NOTE: let-lot-coordinate-test]

The remaining differences require the same passage-first analysis:

| Feature | What the research must establish |
|---|---|
| additional infinitive formations | what each formation does in its Vedic passage |
| injunctive forms | how the unaugmented form functions and how its meaning remains recoverable |
| augment behavior | where the augment appears, where it does not, and what conditions govern the difference |
| alternate pronouns and endings | the relation each form marks and the compositional range it provides |
| domain-scoped affixes | the exact Pāṇinian condition and a preserved example |
| voice and mode distinctions | how each deployment changes the action, participant, or stance |

The pyramid turns every Vedic feature into an archaic survival whenever ordinary
laukika Sanskrit deploys another resource. An architectural explanation must
instead show what the feature does before judging why its deployment differs.
Each form earns its explanation inside the architecture.

## 8.10 Fixed Composition and Open Style

The four Vedas use distinct styles because their compositions serve different
functions, while the Brāhmaṇas, Āraṇyakas, and Upaniṣads introduce still more
variation. Laukika Sanskrit expands the range through prose, ***काव्य
(*kāvya*)***, ***शास्त्र (*śāstra*)***, drama, story, commentary, and the
distinct voices of individual authors.

A chronology trained on botanical language turns these differences into stages.
The oldest material is expected to sound archaic, while open worldly use is
expected to sound modern. Sanskrit's two-domain architecture breaks that
inference. Vaidika expression follows the style and preservation requirements
of its corpus. Laukika expression follows the purpose chosen by its composer,
using a generative architecture designed for worldly application.

An early laukika composition could sound more familiar to a modern
student than a Vedic passage composed within another style. Familiarity would
show that the composition uses the open generative domain; it would not date the
composition. A modern composer could likewise imitate a Vedic surface without
turning the new work into Veda, because style cannot change the permission
attached to the corpus.

The distinction resembles the difference between a file format and the content
created with it. A new document can use an old and stable format. An old
document can use specialized features that ordinary new documents seldom need.
The visible style does not establish when the underlying architecture was
created, and the architecture does not force every composition to sound alike.

Open composition gives laukika Sanskrit its unusual reach because the language
can remain invariant while poets, analysts, storytellers, physicians,
mathematicians, and teachers develop new styles. Their compositions enter a
curated and vulnerable field: communities may preserve them, release them,
forget them, or lose them to destruction. The permission to compose remains
open even when particular compositions disappear.

## 8.11 Two Permissions, One Civilizational Architecture

The engineering of Sanskrit prepared for natural entropy and deliberate
asuric assault. Entropy changes pronunciation, words, meanings, and memory
without requiring an antagonist. Asuric formations act deliberately when they
destroy teaching institutions, shame caretakers, monopolize interpretation,
replace Sanskrit's categories, or remove the language from public use.

The *vaidika* domain protects exact memory by placing the reference beyond
revision and distributing its transmission across lineages. The *laukika*
domain keeps Sanskrit present in changing circumstances by allowing speakers to
generate new expression through the same architecture. If Sanskrit preserved
only a fixed corpus, society could lose the ability to apply its measure to a
new world. If Sanskrit allowed every generation to revise the reference, those
with power could rewrite the measure.

The two permissions prevent those failures from collapsing into one another.
Vaidika preservation keeps laukika Sanskrit measurable. Laukika generation
keeps the calibrant socially usable. Distributed teaching and analysis prevent
either function from becoming the property of one apex.

These two permissions explain why the domains can differ without forming a
chronology. A read-only corpus and a write-enabled generative domain are
concurrent parts of one design. The first preserves expressions under complete
inherited conditions; the second reuses the language architecture in
combinations that no inherited corpus could predict individually. Sanskrit
remains exact where exactness is the duty and generative where the world
requires new expression.
