Proposal only. No manuscript changes have been made.

## About the Series

**Section structure: KEEP.** At about 835 words, this short front-matter essay reads better as one continuous statement than as a miniature chapter with several headings.

**REPHRASE the abstract opening** (`as_0_00_about_series.md`, opening three paragraphs). The present phrases - "the civilizational architecture they hold," "a system of calibrants," and "architecture that can recur across scale" - ask the reader to absorb the series vocabulary before seeing a concrete instance. Keep the claims, but let the Vedas and Sanskrit perform visible actions first. Suggested sequence:

> The Vedic corpus preserves more than prayers, stories, and ceremonial forms. It also preserves ways of organizing sound, memory, knowledge, and social life. Modern readers are rarely taught to look for that architecture.
>
> Each volume in *Second Shanti* brings one part of it into view. This first volume begins with Sanskrit because Sanskrit provides the clearest working example: its sounds can be tested, its rules can be followed, and its preservation can still be heard.

**EXPLAIN the subtitle sequence before interpreting it.** Keep the current three-part block on radiant, calibrant, and fractal, but lead with the fuller sentence already favored in discussion: "These three words in the subtitle represent a sequence." Then let each paragraph show the action: radiance shares; a calibrant supplies a measure; fractality repeats that ordering principle in another domain.

**JOIN the explanation after the blockquote to the block itself.** The prose after the three terms currently repeats their definitions at a higher level of abstraction. Replace the repetition with one concrete bridge: Sanskrit radiates forms into other languages, supplies a measure without a central office, and shows how distributed order can recur beyond language.

**REPHRASE the attacks paragraph** so the attackers act on named objects. Suggested form:

> Ancient, Abrahamic, and modern asuric formations attacked this architecture in different ways. Some destroyed institutions and lineages. Others obscured categories, redirected patronage, or taught Hindus to distrust what their own civilization had preserved. Those attacks reveal why the architecture threatens a pyramid: it sustains order without placing an apex in command.

**KEEP the promise of later volumes.** It establishes the series without casually revealing this volume's central demonstration. Avoid adding further examples from later volumes here.

**KEEP the eclipse ledger** beginning "Sanskrit remains present..." Its repetition is earned because it lets the reader see preservation and concealment operating at the same time. Consider setting the clauses as a short visual list during implementation; do not rewrite them into a compact summary.

**REPHRASE the methodological disclaimer** near line 35. The current sentence sounds defensive and uses abstract phrases such as "historical-linguistic comparison" and "a different category of object." Suggested form:

> Comparison can reveal real relationships among languages, and chronology can arrange evidence. The theft begins when those tools are allowed to decide in advance what Sanskrit is. A botanical model can describe a natural language well and still distort an engineered calibrant.

**EXPLAIN `vaidika` and `laukika` with the distinction before the labels.** The current paragraph assumes the reader can follow domains, modes, chronology, and Pāṇini at once. Use the visible facts first: some forms belong to mantra and meter; others belong to worldly use. Then introduce `vaidika` / `laukika` and `chandasi` / `bhāṣāyām` as Sanskrit's own ways of locating those uses.

**REPHRASE the lineage opening** (line 88). "Held the central position" is abstract. Suggested form:

> Sanskrit reached the present through lineages that heard, analyzed, taught, and corrected it. Within that work, **व्याकरणम् (*vyākaraṇam*)** means analysis, separation, or unfolding: an operation performed on something that already exists.

**MOVE the list of modern advocates to the endnote unless each person is praised or criticized in the body.** The current body sentence names nine people without telling a new reader what each contributed. The endnote is the better location under the manuscript's existing rule for personal names. The body can say that Indian scholars and advocates maintained this position under institutional pressure.

**SPLIT the architecture-at-scale sentence** (line 92). It currently introduces six technical terms, the Vedas, Pāṇini, and recitation in one paragraph. Suggested order: first list the scale in one sentence; then explain that the Vedas preserve the working architecture; then identify Pāṇini as its finest surviving documenter and the recitation systems as the machinery that kept it audible.

**REPHRASE the origin paragraph** (line 94) so it does not sound like an academic limitation-of-scope statement:

> This volume does not try to date Sanskrit's beginning. The lineage preserves **अपौरुषेय (*apauruṣeya*)**, without human authorship, while Chapter 17 offers an explicitly marked speculation for readers who require a human account. The argument here begins from what anyone can inspect: the engineering survives in the language, the Vedas preserve it, and the analytical lineages explain how it works.

**KEEP the final two beats**, but attach the chapter map directly to them after the heading merge.

## Chapter 0

**Section structure: KEEP.** Eight sections across roughly 3,000 words give the reader regular places to pause, and each section performs a distinct task.

**KEEP §0.1's zero-and-infinity puzzle.** It is concrete, memorable, and suitable for a non-specialist. The paragraph connecting set theory, fullness, and metaphysics should state more plainly that set theory is an analogy, not a claim that the mantra teaches modern mathematics.

**REPHRASE the opening of §0.2.** "Treats inquiry as the way order is approached" is compressed. Suggested form:

> A civilization that begins from *pūrṇam* does not approach order by waiting for a final command. It approaches order through inquiry. The seeker asks what holds, tests the answer, and accepts the discipline that the inquiry requires.

**EXPLAIN the catalogue of disciplines through one common action.** After Mīmāṃsā and the Brahmasūtra, say that Ayurveda examines the body, Nyāya tests inference, Sāṃkhya distinguishes what exists, and Vedic recitation tests memory against an audible measure. This avoids treating the names as proof by accumulation.

**KEEP the follower-as-seeker paragraph.** It gives Chapter 1 the exact plurality-and-choice contrast it needs.

**REPHRASE §0.3's opening.** "The reader already knows more Sanskrit than the reader knows" is clever but self-conscious. Prefer: "Most readers already know some Sanskrit, even if they have never studied the language." The examples can then demonstrate the claim.

**SPLIT the first loanword paragraph after `yoga`.** The paragraph currently carries too many examples and interrupts each with a definition. Two shorter example paragraphs will let the reader retain the pattern.

**EXPLAIN Yāska's action-based account before applying it to contested words.** One ordinary sentence is enough: Sanskrit often treats a name as a clue to what a thing does, rather than as an arbitrary tag. Then introduce the formula and the `deva` example.

**KEEP §0.4's three-field structure and its two figures.** This is a load-bearing orientation section, and the repetition immediately around the figure helps the reader learn unfamiliar terms.

**REPHRASE `percipient selection`.** Retain the term if it is part of the book's vocabulary, but follow it immediately with the ordinary action: communities preserve works they continue to recite, teach, perform, copy, and use; other works can be released or lost. The current phrase "keeping what serves and letting the rest go" is clearer than the term and should come first.

**EXPLAIN why two Sanskrit domains are necessary before the Esperanto forward pointer.** The current question is strong. Add the direct contrast: rules can keep a language orderly, but a calibrant also needs a body of content that remains unchanged. The *vaidika* domain preserves that measure; the *laukika* domain lets speakers apply the architecture to changing circumstances. Chapter 2 can then show what Esperanto lacked.

**REPHRASE the Veda division paragraph in §0.5.** It currently asks the reader to absorb four Vedas, `saṃhitā`, `vistāra`, domains, and modes at once. Keep the statement that Vyāsa divided one Veda into four for preservation, then give the four names. Explain `saṃhitā` and the Brāhmaṇa-Āraṇyaka-Upaniṣad expansion in a following sentence.

**KEEP §0.6's place-value analogy**, but simplify the long paragraph on the transmission of numerals. The main-body action is: Indic mathematicians built the place-value system; Arabic mathematicians received and developed it; Europe received it through Arabic scholarship. Detailed qualification belongs in the note.

**EXPLAIN `dictionary language` before contrasting it with Sanskrit.** The phrase can sound as if English has no generative morphology. State the narrower claim: an English dictionary must list most established lexical items individually, while Sanskrit can derive a far larger share of its vocabulary through an explicit generative system.

**KEEP the `Chandrayāna` example.** It makes generativity visible and prevents the section from remaining numerical.

**REPHRASE the first sentence of §0.7.** "Sanskrit is held by an engineered transmission architecture" is true within the book but abstract as an opener. Suggested form:

> Sanskrit survived because generations of people performed a repeatable set of actions: they listened, recited, corrected, memorized, taught, and checked one lineage against another. Together those actions form an engineered transmission architecture.

**KEEP the mother-to-reciter scale comparison and the hammer** "Scale differs. The duty does not." The preceding paragraph earns it.

**REPHRASE the lineage geography paragraph** so "lineage-independent" does not imply that lineages are irrelevant. Say that many geographically separated lineages preserve comparable forms, allowing each to serve as a check on the others.

**JOIN the three existence hammers** at line 153. "The recordings exist. The lineages exist. The transmission exists" repeats a fact already stated twice. Suggested form: "The recitations continue today in gurukulas, temples, homes, schools, and diaspora communities; recordings make that living transmission audible beyond the lineages themselves."

**REPHRASE §0.8's opening** for a non-specialist:

> The argument ahead does not require prior Sanskrit study. Readers who know the language will meet familiar features in a different setting: the book treats them as parts of an architecture rather than as isolated rules in a grammar lesson.

**KEEP the closing Atri hint.** It withholds the payoff without becoming obscure.

**KEEP and slightly expand the concrete power sequence** at lines 79-85. It successfully explains what happens after a state surveys language: schools and offices reward one form, shame another, and gradually place older memory behind credentialed interpreters.

**REPHRASE §2.2's opening.** "Fastened comparative philology to its governing botanical imagination" is vivid but compressed. **KEEP §2.3 as the fairness paragraph.** It acknowledges where botany works and prevents the critique from sounding like a rejection of all study of natural language change.

**REPHRASE §2.4-§2.6 to remove repeated formulations of the same reversal.** State the sequence once in full: Sanskrit's own order is established bond, living usage, then analytical documentation; the pyramid reverses it into drift, Pāṇini's codification, and a fixed classical language.
