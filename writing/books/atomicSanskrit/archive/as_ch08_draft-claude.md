## 8.1 The Test of Āryatva

During his internment in Ratnagiri from 1924 to 1937, V.D. Savarkar was prohibited by the British from engaging in political activity. CID officers monitored his public utterances for sedition. He developed a rhetorical loophole.

Savarkar would deliver high-energy speeches on history and religion, building the crowd toward a fever pitch. At the height of the moment he would shout the opening of a verse the Maharashtrian tradition had been carrying across many generations:

> बहुत लोक मेळवावे । एक विचारे भरावे । कष्टे करोनी घसरावे । …
>
> *Gather many people. Fill them with one thought. With great effort, fall upon …*

Then he stopped. He did not say the final word. He left it hanging in the air.

The audience knew the verse. They knew what came next. They supplied the word themselves:

> म्लेंच्छांवरी ।
>
> *Upon the* mlecchas.

That was the force of the moment. Savarkar did not need to utter the word; the crowd completed it for him. The quotation became participatory — a remembered line from Marathi political-religious memory turned into a live act of collective declaration, with the British colonial state's monitoring apparatus unable to charge the speaker for a word the speaker had not spoken.[NOTE: savarkar-ratnagiri-mleccha]

The verse was Samarth Ramdas's, originally addressed to Sambhaji Maharaj in the seventeenth-century-Marathi political register against the Mughal invaders.[NOTE: samarth-ramdas-mleccha-verse] The Maharashtrian tradition had been carrying *mleccha* across many generations as a political-emotional category: the population the term named changed across the generations; the criteria for the designation held.

Savarkar used the verse as a political instrument. But were the English actually *mleccha* by Sanskrit's own definition? The English — who called themselves *ārya* through their nineteenth-century philological apparatus, who built the AIT framework that placed the white European at the source of Sanskrit — were they, on the dharmic tradition's own technical reading of the term, themselves the real *mleccha*?

What does *mleccha* mean in Sanskrit anyway?

---

## 8.2 The Mūrdhanya Apparatus

In the tradition's own technical register, *mleccha* names those whose mouths do not produce the engineered Indic sound-system. The root *mlech-* names indistinct utterance — the speech that fails the test the *varṇamālā* codifies. The grid is the test. The mouth that produces the grid enters the sound-field; the mouth that cannot is, by the tradition's own technical category, *mleccha* — *barbarian* in the political register Ramdas and Savarkar carried forward, the same word in its Sanskrit-technical original. The political register and the technical register name the same population through different vocabularies. *Āryatva* आर्यत्व is the achievement of producing the grid; the failure to produce the grid is what the tradition's technical term names.

The engineered apparatus operates in two modes. The **Vedic mode** is recitational-preservational; it preserves accent, meter, recitation, and the full retroflex apparatus including the retroflex lateral ळ that §8.3 treats as load-bearing case study. The **generative-analytical mode** is the *bhāṣāyām* mode Pāṇini's *Aṣṭādhyāyī* codifies; it preserves the rule-set by which sound becomes word and word becomes meaning. Both modes are simultaneously available within the *Aṣṭādhyāyī*'s own framework — *chandasi* rules and *bhāṣāyām* rules operating in parallel, not in historical sequence. The chapter's argument operates across both modes and across the parallel non-codified regional speech-fields that continued outside the *bhāṣāyām* perimeter.

The tongue moves backward in the mouth and curls upward, tip rising toward the hard palate at the middle of its arc. The curl creates a hollow space beneath — a small cavity that did not exist a moment before. Sound passes through. The consonant that emerges is ट. The position the tongue has just found is the structural center of the engineered sound-system the *varṇamālā* codifies, and the achievement of finding it cleanly across the years of a child's training is what the Sanskritic tradition has been calling *āryatva*.

Three consonants live at this position: ट ड ण. The first is unvoiced and unaspirated; the second is voiced; the third is nasal. The triad recurs at every row of the *varṇamālā*'s consonantal grid, anchoring each row with a fixed three-way structural pattern. The retroflex base ट ड ण is shared subcontinent-wide. It recurs, with the same articulatory mechanism, across the Vedic mode of Sanskrit, the post-Pāṇinian *bhāṣāyām* mode, the regional Prakrits, Marathi, the central forest-belt languages, and the southern subcontinental languages. The base set is subcontinent-wide, not Sanskrit-specific.

Sanskrit's *varṇamālā* elaborates the shared base by adding *mahāprāṇa* (aspirated) consonants on top of the *alpaprāṇa* (unaspirated) base: ट → ठ, ड → ढ, with ण without a *mahāprāṇa* doublet in the same way. The *alpaprāṇa* / *mahāprāṇa* contrast is itself an engineering decision — a phonological dimension that Sanskrit's apparatus encodes systematically but that is not universal across the languages of the subcontinent. Tamil's classical phonology, for instance, does not operate the *mahāprāṇa* contrast at this level. The shared subcontinent-wide retroflex base is ट ड ण; the *mahāprāṇa* additions ठ ढ are a further engineering elaboration on top of the base, native to Sanskrit's *varṇamālā* and to the Sanskrit-derived languages that inherit it.

The chapter's argument that retroflexion is a subcontinent-wide native phonetic feature operates on the shared base. The *mahāprāṇa* elaboration is a separate (and load-bearing) engineering decision in Sanskrit's own design, recoverable from the *varṇamālā*'s structural specifications. The point for now is that the test of *āryatva* the chapter develops runs on the shared base. Producing ट ड ण is what the tradition asks; the *mahāprāṇa* additions are Sanskrit-specific elaborations on top of the asking.

Now the engineering geometry. Sanskrit's consonantal grid samples five points along the vocal tract — measured along the midline from the lips to the glottis, with an adult-male reference length of approximately 17 cm:

- ~0 cm — **labial** (*pavarga* — प फ ब भ म), articulated at the lips
- ~3 cm — **dental** (*tavarga* — त थ द ध न), articulated at the back face of the upper teeth
- ~7 cm — **retroflex** (*ṭavarga* — ट ठ ड ढ ण), articulated by curling the tongue tip back to the mid-palate
- ~9 cm — **palatal** (*cavarga* — च छ ज झ ञ), articulated at the hard palate
- ~12 cm — **velar / guttural** (*kavarga* — क ख ग घ ङ), articulated at the soft palate

The intervals between included positions — approximately 3, 4, 2, 3 cm — are not strictly equidistant. The grid is doing something more sophisticated than equal spacing. It samples five well-separated positions across the front-to-back range, with each pair of adjacent positions separated by at least ~2 cm for clean acoustic distinguishability. The retroflex base at ~7 cm sits structurally central — the midpoint anchor of the five-point sampling, with the front cluster (labial, dental) and back cluster (palatal, velar) bracketing it on either side. The design pattern scales with speaker physiology; the adult-female reference of approximately 14–15 cm produces the same sampling pattern at smaller scale.[NOTE: mūrdhanya-articulation-geometry]

The grid was assembled by deliberate choice. The choice is visible in what the grid does not include.

Two structurally distinct kinds of exclusion shape the design. The first is **adjacent-exclusion** — articulation points too close to an already-included position are bounded out. The English phonological apparatus includes a dental fricative at approximately 2 cm, the consonant represented as *th* (θ, ð), produced by placing the tongue tip between or at the front face of the upper teeth. The Indic dental at ~3 cm — back face of the teeth — sits only ~1 cm behind it. Sanskrit's *varṇamālā* includes the dental position and bounds out the interdental. The two articulation points are too close for clean acoustic separation. The grid keeps one and discards the other as a deliberate choice not to crowd the front of the vocal tract with confusable positions.

The same adjacent-exclusion principle governs the entire ~4 cm gap between the Indic dental (~3 cm) and the Indic retroflex (~7 cm). The intervening region houses the alveolar (~4 cm), the post-alveolar (~5–6 cm), and the palato-alveolar (~6 cm) positions. This is the heart of the English consonantal apparatus — *t*, *d*, *n*, *s*, *z*, *l*, *r*, *sh*, *ch*, and *j* all live in this region. Sanskrit's grid crosses this entire range in a single step. The five vargas of the *varṇamālā* include no alveolar varga, no post-alveolar varga, no palato-alveolar varga. The grid was designed to skip this densely-populated region. Where English crowds, Sanskrit separates.

The second exclusion principle is **range-boundary-exclusion** — articulation points beyond the included range are bounded out, regardless of physiological availability. The Arabic phonological apparatus includes a deep-pharyngeal articulation at approximately 15 cm, the consonants represented as ع and ح, produced by constricting the throat well behind the velum. The Indic velar at ~12 cm is the back-end of Sanskrit's included range. Positions deeper than the velum are bounded out. The pharyngeal is physiologically available and operative in fully developed phonological systems — the Arabic apparatus uses it without difficulty. Sanskrit's grid does not extend to it. The grid's back-end is bounded by design, not by physiological limit.

The exclusions are not accidents. The grid was assembled — five well-separated positions across the front-to-back range, with the front-of-mouth crowding-zone (interdental, alveolar, post-alveolar) skipped in a single step and the deep-back zone (pharyngeal) bounded out at the back. The retroflex is not a feature Sanskrit happened to have. It is the central anchor of a bounded selective sampling that the engineering thesis says someone designed — chosen from what the subcontinental sound-field made available, refined into a grid of maximum acoustic separation, and held in that grid through the *varṇamālā*'s formal apparatus.

This is what design looks like.

## 8.3 ळ and the Codification Perimeter

The opening word of the *Ṛgveda* is *agnimīḷe* अग्निमीळे. Its second consonant — placed at the structural front of the foundational text — is the retroflex lateral ळ. The Vedic mode of Sanskrit preserves the consonant as load-bearing; the *Prātiśākhya* and *pāṭha* apparatus that holds the *Saṃhitā* against drift across many generations holds ळ with it.[NOTE: agnimīḷe-rigveda-opening]

Post-Pāṇinian Classical Sanskrit does not have ळ. Western philology reads the absence as evidence that Vedic Sanskrit was an earlier stage from which Classical Sanskrit descended through phonological decay. The reading is an interpretive overlay on what the source texts themselves do not say. Pāṇini's *Aṣṭādhyāyī* names the distinction between Vedic Sanskrit and post-Pāṇinian Sanskrit not as stages in a decay sequence but as two synchronic modes operating in parallel. The *chandasi* (Vedic) rules and the *bhāṣāyām* (generative-analytical) rules constitute parallel functional domains of the same grammatical apparatus. The retroflex lateral ḷ appears in the *chandasi* rules and is bounded out of the *bhāṣāyām* mode's phonological inventory. The two modes are not before-and-after. They are side-by-side.[NOTE: chandasi-bhāṣāyām-aṣṭādhyāyī]

The Sanskrit-internal evidence is dispositive on what Pāṇini did. He did not claim ḷ did not exist in operative Sanskrit. He assigned it to one mode and bounded it out of the other. The codification was a design choice — the *bhāṣāyām* mode optimized for rule-based completeness as the analytical-generative apparatus required, with the retroflex lateral falling outside the scope that optimization required. This is what codification does: it draws a perimeter around a design's intended functional domain.

The implication is more consequential than it appears. The codification did not change the operative subcontinental sound-field. The Vedic mode continued operating with ळ in place, held by the preservation engineering of the *Prātiśākhya*, *Śikṣā*, and *pāṭha* apparatus. The regional speech-fields — the prakritic continuums that fed into Maharashtri and from there into Marathi — continued operating with ळ. The central forest-belt languages continued operating with their native retroflex laterals. The southern subcontinental languages continued operating with their own retroflex-lateral phonemes (Tamil ழ, Malayalam ഴ, Telugu ళ, Kannada ಳ — distinct from one another in articulatory detail, anchored in the same retroflex-lateral category). What the *bhāṣāyām* codification did was draw a perimeter for one specific mode of Sanskrit. Outside the perimeter, the operative sound-field was larger than the codification claimed.

A possible objection at this point: did Pāṇini's codification *drop* ḷ from operative non-Vedic Sanskrit, or did ḷ *already* differentiate to Vedic-mode-only by the codification's moment? The first reading holds that the feature was present in the broader speech-field and the codification chose to bound it out. The second reading holds that the *bhāṣāyām* simply did not include what was no longer operative there. The pre-Pāṇinian non-Vedic prose tradition survives only through post-Pāṇinian transmission that normalized phonology to the *bhāṣāyām* standard, so the question is not settleable from the extant textual record alone. The engineering-thesis argument does not require it to be settled. Either reading converges on the same structural conclusion: the codification draws a perimeter; ळ sat outside the *bhāṣāyām* perimeter; the Vedic mode held ळ in place through the engineered preservation apparatus; the regional speech-fields, the Maharashtri-to-Marathi line, the central forest-belt substrate, and the southern subcontinental sound-field all continued operating with retroflex laterals outside the codification. The operative subcontinental reality was larger than the codified specification of any single mode.

The AIT-aligned reading treats Marathi ळ as a substrate intrusion from "Dravidian" or "Munda" into an "Indo-Aryan"–descended language. The codification-perimeter reading inverts the framing. The retroflex lateral is not an intrusion. It is the continuous subcontinental sound-field that was always there, preserved in the Vedic mode and in every regional speech-field that the *bhāṣāyām* perimeter did not claim to govern. The "Classical Sanskrit without ळ" is not a base reality from which Marathi diverged. It is a bounded codification that the wider Indic linguistic reality lived outside of from the beginning.

ळ lives in the Vedic mode and runs continuously across the subcontinent. The codification did not bring it. The codification calibrated against it. The mouth was here first.

## 8.4 The Test Inverted

The AIT framework imports an *ārya* population from outside the subcontinent. The framework's various reconstructions differ on the population's origin — Pontic-Caspian steppe in some, Central Asian plateau in others — but agree that the population originated outside, arrived inside, and authored Sanskrit upon arrival. The framework requires the imported population to have been the source of the engineered phonetic apparatus the chapter has been developing.

The retroflex test the tradition itself applies returns the verdict. A population whose native sound-field does not include retroflex articulation cannot produce ट ड ण without years of trained tongue-placement work. The Pontic-Caspian and Central Asian sound-fields the AIT framework draws on do not include retroflex articulation. The "invading *ārya*" arrives without the apparatus the source of Sanskrit must possess. The framework's own primary claim — that the imported population authored Sanskrit — collides with the framework's own implicit phonetic geography.

Savarkar was technically accurate. The verse he made his Ratnagiri audience complete named the population it named on two counts simultaneously. The English failed the test of *āryatva* as brutal, oppressive plunderers — the political-emotional count Ramdas had codified and Savarkar carried forward, where the English fit the predecessor classification exactly: their structural relationship to the subcontinent was the same occupation-and-extraction relationship the Mughals had embodied before them. The English also failed the test as those whose mouths could not produce the engineered Indic sound-system — the technical-Sanskrit count §§8.2–8.3 have developed. Their mouths produced alveolars where the retroflex set required; their native sound-field contained no retroflex articulation; they entered the engineered Indic sound-system as the structural outsiders the tradition's own technical category names.

Two counts. Same verdict. The English who built the AIT framework to claim *ārya* for themselves were, on every Indic standard the categories carry, the structural *mleccha*. *Mleccha* in the political register and *mleccha* in the technical register name the same population through different vocabularies.

The dharmic tradition supplies the supporting primary-source authority. The *Assalāyana Sutta* of the *Majjhima Nikāya* preserves a dialogue in which the Buddha, responding to Assalāyana on the question of *varṇa*, observes that the bordering nations of *Yona* and *Kamboja* — Greek and Iranian-frontier regions — operate with only two varnas, *ārya* and *dāsa*, where the dharmic-Indic frame has the full fourfold *varṇa* system.[NOTE: assalayana-sutta] The observation does structural work for the chapter's argument. The *ārya / dāsa* binary that AIT places at the foundation of Indic civilizational prehistory is documented by the dharmic tradition itself as a *foreign-bordering-nations feature* — a category the Yona and Kamboja populations operated within, not a category the dharmic tradition originated or imposed on others. The binary AIT projects backward onto Indic prehistory is, on the tradition's own primary-source observation, an externally-located framework. The full mantra-landing belongs to the Epilogue; here in §8.4 the citation operates as confirmatory evidence for the inversion the chapter performs.

The contemporary popular-culture register confirms the phonetic test at the level of trained-actor caricature. The pattern is consistent across decades of non-native Indian-accent caricature: alveolar consonants replacing the retroflex base, dental consonants where ट ड ण would belong, the tongue's resting placement failing to find the curl. The general audience hears it instantly. The cultural-stereotype apparatus has been operating on the phonetic test for decades — every successful non-native Indian-accent caricature is a public diagnostic of the *mūrdhanya* test passing on the audience the caricature is performed for. The English mouth produces alveolars where the engineered Indic sound-field requires retroflexes. The caricature trades on this and the audience recognizes the trade.

The tradition's test, applied honestly, returns the verdict the tradition has always returned. The "invading *ārya*" of nineteenth-century philology was not the author of Sanskrit. The author of Sanskrit was the population that had been engineering its sound-field for generations before any "invasion" the framework imagines.

## 8.5 The Pedagogical Reading

The retroflex test makes a single structural point above all others: *āryatva* is achievable. The engineered phonetic apparatus the chapter has developed is open to all who undergo the *guru-shishya* training. The trained tongue-placement, the cultivated breath, the disciplined attention to the *varṇamālā*'s structural specifications, the years of practice that turn ट ड ण from impossible articulation to easy articulation — every part of the apparatus is curricular. *Ārya* names the achievement; the *गुरु-शिष्य परम्परा* (*guru-shishya paramparā*) is the mechanism; the *vedāṅga* apparatus is the curriculum. Anyone who undergoes the curriculum becomes *ārya* in the tradition's own technical sense.

There is no permanent exclusion. There is no genetic gate. There is no inherited claim. There is only the test, and the work that the test demands.

The Rigvedic call *कृण्वन्तो विश्वमार्यम्* (*kṛṇvanto viśvam āryam*) — "making the whole world *ārya*" — is incoherent on the racial reading of *ārya* that AIT requires. Race cannot be made. Pedagogical-civilizational achievement can. The mantra refutes AIT on the tradition's own foundational primary-source authority. The Epilogue lands the full mantra; this chapter's contribution to the framing is to establish, through the phonetic-pedagogical reading of *āryatva*, that the mantra is operating on the only reading that makes the call coherent.

The chapter has developed an engineering apparatus. The grid is bounded; the exclusions are deliberate; the retroflex sits at the structural center; the Vedic mode preserves what the *bhāṣāyām* codification bounds out; the substrate languages of the central forest belt and the south continue to operate the same engineered hardware. The apparatus is curricular: it is taught, learned, mastered. The training operates inside the *guru-shishya paramparā*, which is the institutional form *āryatva* has been taking across many generations. Anyone undergoing the training acquires the apparatus. Anyone who has acquired the apparatus is, on the tradition's own classification, *ārya*.

*Āryatva* is achievable. The mouth is the entrance. The work begins there.
