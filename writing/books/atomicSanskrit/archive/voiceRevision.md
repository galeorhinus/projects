1. Lead the chapter with the wordplay, pun, or controlling metaphor — or possibly a historical scene. Consider the payoff. 
2. Wordplay as structural spine, not decoration. When a chapter has a wordplay available that can carry the argumentative spine, the wordplay should run through every section, return at every hammer-close, and land at the chapter's verdict. Decorative wordplay that appears once and disappears is not in voice; structural wordplay that drumbeats across the chapter is.
3. Section titles as verdicts when makes sense, else topics. Section titles can deliver verdicts, sometimes labels. A reader who scans the section titles should absorb the chapter's argument before reading the prose. Intermingle with descriptive labels (The X Apparatus); use verdict statements (X Failed the Y) when needed.
4. Short-line paragraphs as pivots. Isolated short-line paragraphs sit as hinges between accumulation paragraphs at structural pivots (concept introduction, verdict, transition between movements). The technique is sparing — overused, it becomes mannerism. The reader's pause at the white space around the isolated line is part of the rhythm.
5. Name the named especially if the named can be used as a strawman for the system being attacked.  Sellers in The Party, not trained-actor caricature. Extends §7.6's anti-weasel rule from "name the establishment" to "name the named figure where the source is identifiable."
6. Catalogs name concrete instances, not abstract categories if the categories use Western philogical conclusions. Santali, Ho, Mundari, Sora, Korku, Kharia — not "tribal" belt languages. No Dravidian.  No Indo-aryan. Repeat-and-drumbeat construction (each clause closing with the same predicate verb) builds rhythm and frees the verdict-close to land.
7. Conversational asides that don't soften. Em-dash asides can land small jokes mid-paragraph without breaking argumentative momentum. The aside must not soften, qualify, or hedge. Test: removing the aside should leave the argument unchanged; what's lost is voice, not substance.
8. Trust the metaphor. When a working metaphor lands the structural argument, the metaphor carries the chapter; the technical proof goes to the chapter whose territory the proof belongs to, or to an endnote. The metaphor is the argument. Technical analyses that try to "back up" an already-landing metaphor signal authorial uncertainty about the metaphor's strength.
9. Verdict-dismissal beats both-sides survey. When an empirical question is in play, unsettlable, and not load-bearing for the chapter's structural argument: name the question, admit the not-knowing, dismiss it. Question → admission → dismissal as a three-sentence structural unit. Never survey both possibilities to demonstrate scholarly fairness — the survey concedes argumentative force the chapter needs.
10. Enumeration gets bolded leads when the structure is the argument. Bulleted or enumerated leads are out for surveys of considerations; they remain in for cases where the argument's structural form is enumeration. Diagnostic: is the chapter asserting the verdict lands on exactly N counts? If yes, enumerate and bold the leads. If the prose is surveying considerations or examples, run it as prose.
11. Every section closes with a verdict. Sections close. Closes are where the verdict lives. Sections that close with questions, surveys, or hedged conclusions are not in voice. The voice's commitment shows up at the closes; unqualified closes mean a committed writer.

12. Minimize academic formal and use voice driven whenever possible. Verdict driven. No hedging.
