## Feature 1 — Conjunctive participle / absolutive: the field's habit, *decoded and compressed*

The chaining habit is the field's, and so is the constraint that travels with it.
Across the subcontinent a sequence of actions is told by leaving every verb but
the last non-finite, and in Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, and the central-forest-belt
languages alike the chained verbs are **subject-controlled** — they share one
governing subject. That same-subject converb is itself an areal feature, not a
Sanskrit invention. The southern absolutive runs on a sharp **‑i**:

- Tamil: *sāppiṭṭu vandān* — "having eaten, he came."
- Telugu: *nēnu aṅgaḍiki veḷḷ-i, pūlu kon-i, iṇṭiki vaccānu* — "I, to the shop having-gone, flowers having-bought, came home."
- Kannada: *nānu aṅgaḍige hōg-i, haṇṇu tan-du, manege bandenu* — "I, to the shop having-gone, fruit having-brought, came home."
- Mundari: *jom-kedte senojana* — "having eaten, he went" [VERIFY form].
- Sanskrit: खादित्वा गच्छति (*khāditvā gacchati*) — "having eaten, he goes."

**So the differentiator is NOT a "same-subject logic gate."** Tamil and Mundari
do not freely switch subjects there either, and even Sanskrit's coreference has
documented exceptions — under a passive main clause the absolutive corefers with
the *agent* (तेन भुक्त्वा गम्यते, *tena bhuktvā gamyate*, "by-him having-eaten, it
is gone"), and with dative-experiencer subjects (Tikkanen 1987; Lowe, *Raising
and passive in Sanskrit*). "Absolutely / strictly identical / cannot change
subjects" would be a target. Drop it.

**What is engineered is the *form*:**

1. **Rule-governed allomorphy.** The suffix is **‑त्वा (*-tvā*, ktvā)** on a bare
   root — कृत्वा (*kṛtvā*), खादित्वा (*khāditvā*) — and **‑य (*-ya*, lyap)** on a
   prefixed or compounded root — आगम्य (*āgamya*), प्रणम्य (*praṇamya*) — a
   precise, preverb-conditioned alternation Pāṇini documents as a rule. The living
   converbs carry the habit; they do not carry this rule-governed allomorphy.
2. **Maximal compression.** One indeclinable carries a whole prior clause — the
   अल्पाक्षरम् (*alpākṣaram*), few-syllabled, *sūtra* economy (Ch 10).
3. **Decoded, not invented.** The field already had the chaining *and* the
   coreference. Pāṇini decoded and documented the rule; Sanskrit is the
   rule-governed, compressed instantiation of the field's own pattern — not a
   unique constraint, and not a borrowing.

**In the Veda.** The absolutive is already in the encoded corpus: the Vedic corpus
preserves the operation in forms such as **‑त्वी (*-tvī*)** / **‑त्वाय (*-tvāya*)**
(पीत्वी *pītvī* "having drunk," कृत्वी *kṛtvī* "having done") beside *-tvā* / *-ya*
— the same *-tvā/-ya* allomorphy Pāṇini documents (Whitney). The grammatical point
is not chronology but the fact that the operation is already present in the encoded
corpus. [VERIFY a specific Ṛgvedic occurrence of *pītvī* / *kṛtvī*]

The accurate claim: the conjunctive participle is the subcontinent's syntax;
Sanskrit is its engineered, rule-bound, maximally compressed form. (And it is

## Feature 2 — Dative-experiencer subject: the field's psychology, in the *kāraka* matrix

The areal feature (Masica's "dative construction"): the experiencer/recipient
stands in a non-nominative case and the state/stimulus stays nominative.
Underneath the morphology is a worldview — the human is the *locus where a state
happens*, a receiver of hunger, fear, liking, knowing, trust, peace, grace, awe,
safety — even anger — not the master who performs them.

**English makes the human the agent; the Veda makes the human the receiver.**
English says *I believe him*, *I forgive him*, *we have peace* — the human is the
subject acting on a direct object, or the owner possessing a thing. The
subcontinental stance refuses this: the state *flows to* a receiver in the dative.
Two grammatical faces carry it — the **recipient** (faith, peace, grace flow *to*
a person) and the **experiencer** (hunger, liking, knowing happen *to* a person).
The living languages realize both by different means:

- **Tamil** — with the dative case: எனக்கு பசி இருக்கு (*enakku pasi irukku*)
  "to-me hunger is" = "I'm hungry"; எனக்கு அவரை தெரியும் (*enakku avarai teriyum*)
  "I know him."
- **Mundari** — by *incorporating the experiencer into the verb* as a pronominal
  object: रेंगेज्-इञ्-अ (*rengej-iñ-a*) "hunger-me-is" = "I'm hungry." Different
  morphology, identical psychology: the human is undergone-upon, not agent.
  [VERIFY form — Osada]
- **Telugu** — dative *‑ku/ki*: *nāku ākaḷigā undi* "to-me hungrily it-is" = "I'm
  hungry"; *nāku kōpaṁ vaccindi* "to-me anger came"; *nāku telusu* "I know."
- **Kannada** — dative *‑ge/kke*: *nanage haśivāgide* "to-me hunger has-happened" =
  "I'm hungry"; *nanage siṭṭu bantu* "to-me anger came"; *nanage gottu* "I know."
- **Sanskrit** — with the dative: मह्यं रोचते (*mahyaṃ rocate*) "it pleases to me";
  मह्यं क्षुधा अस्ति (*mahyaṃ kṣudhā asti*) "to me hunger is" [VERIFY idiom —
  Sanskrit may prefer the genitive मम].

**The engineered fingerprint — the कारक (*kāraka*) matrix.** This is where the
dative strand gets its own engineering signature, parallel to *ktvā/lyap* and
*abhyāsa*. The receiver is not left to idiom: the engineered grammar carries it as
a defined semantic role. The **kāraka** system — Sanskrit's engineered grammar of
semantic roles (agent, patient, instrument, recipient, source, locus) — gives the
receiver the **सम्प्रदान (*sampradāna*)** role, and Pāṇini decoded the rule in two
moves. The general rule, **1.4.32 — कर्मणा यमभिप्रैति स सम्प्रदानम्** (*karmaṇā yam
abhipraiti sa sampradānam*, "the one whom, through the action, one intends to
reach is the *sampradāna*"), defines the receiver of a flowing action — exactly
the *śrad asmai dhatta* / *śaṃ naḥ* / *mṛḷa mahyam* psychology of the mantras. The
extension, **1.4.33** (*rucyarthānāṃ prīyamāṇaḥ*), records that with verbs of
pleasing the one who is pleased is also *sampradāna* — the experiencer folded into

## Feature 3 — Reduplication: the engineering fingerprint (strongest)

Reduplication is the case's best evidence, because it is the feature that, on its
face, should not be in a concision-engineered language at all.

It is the deepest and least concise of the three, and the field runs it as a full
engine. Tamil repeats whole words for sensory intensity (*pala-pala*, flashing)
and distribution (*veedu-veedu*, "every house"), and forms echo words by swapping
the onset (*puli-kili*, "tigers and such") [VERIFY echo formant — standardly *ki-*].
Telugu and Kannada run the identical engine: sensory expressives (Telugu
*gama-gama*, "wafting fragrance," *thala-thala*, "glittering"; Kannada
*gama-gam-ane*, "smelling wonderful," *thaḷa-thaḷ-ane*, "sparkling") and
dummy-consonant echo words (Telugu *gī-*: *pustakam-gistakam*, "books and such";
Kannada *gi-*: *haṇa-giṇa*, "money and such").
In the central forest belt the engine is largest: Mundari and Santali pour **more
than a tenth of their whole lexicons** into mimetic reduplication — expressives
doubling a syllable, word, or echo to paint sound, motion, texture, taste,
feeling (Phillips & Harrison 2017); Mundari builds vivid CVC-CVC echoes
(*oṛa-moṛa*, "houses and such") [VERIFY — Osada], and Santali even *counts* by
reduplication (gɛl "ten" → gɛ-gɛl "by tens"). The device works by repeating
material; repetition is the opposite of compression.

Sanskrit is built on compression — the *sūtra* standard is अल्पाक्षरम्
(*alpākṣaram*). A language engineered for brevity has no obvious use for a device
whose method is to say the thing twice. So reduplication was not imported raw —
it was disciplined:

- **Reduced + regularized the copy** into a minimal, rule-bound अभ्यास
  (*abhyāsa*): one onset consonant + a short vowel, with fixed substitutions the
  *sūtras* document — clusters drop to a single consonant, long vowels shorten,
  aspirates deaspirate, and **velars surface as palatals** (Pāṇini *kuhoś cuḥ*).
  Seen in action: कृ (*kṛ*, "do") → perfect चकार (*ca-kāra*) — the root's velar
  *k* re-emerges as palatal *c* in the reduplicant. (State this as the rule and
  its *effect* — a compact, fixed reduplicant — not as a claimed motive like
  "to save effort.") [VERIFY rule statement vs Whitney §588 ff.]
- **Assigned it exact grammatical jobs** instead of open expressive range: the
  perfect (चकार, *cakāra*), the reduplicating present class जुहोत्यादि
  (*juhotyādi*: जुहोति, *juhoti*; ददाति, *dadāti*), the desiderative — desire
  encoded straight into the verb — ज्ञा (*jñā*, "know") → जिज्ञासते (*jijñāsate*,
  "desires to know"); पिपासति, *pipāsati*), and the intensive (बोभूयते, *bobhūyate*).
- **Tied it to the phonetics.** The reduplicating class is built from
  acoustically robust atoms: *juhotyādi* enriches the voiced-aspirate column (C4)
  far above any other *gaṇa*, because the repeated onset has to carry across the
  syllable boundary (Ch 11 §11.6; by-the-numbers App). Accidental contact does
  not select its members for acoustic robustness; an engineered subsystem does.

**In the Veda (the strongest attestation).** Reduplication runs all through the
Ṛgveda — the perfect (चकार *cakāra*, बभूव *babhūva*, ददर्श *dadarśa*), the
reduplicating present (जुहोति *juhoti*, ददाति *dadāti*, दधाति *dadhāti*), and above
all the **intensives** (जोहवीति *johavīti* "calls again and again," रोरवीति
*roravīti* "roars," नोनवीति *nonavīti*) — a largely *chandas*-domain operation,
abundant in the encoded corpus. This is the decisive point: the field's expressive,
iterative doubling is not outside Sanskrit waiting to be borrowed — it is *in the
encoded corpus itself*, carried by the engineered grammar as the disciplined
*abhyāsa*. [VERIFY intensive forms vs Whitney ch. XIV]
