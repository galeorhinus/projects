## 1. Working Thesis

The chapter isolates the **मूर्धन्य (*mūrdhanya*) — the retroflex set — as the operational test of *āryatva*** in the Indic phonetic-pedagogical framework. *Ārya* in the tradition's own register names not race or lineage but a phonetic-pedagogical achievement: mastery of *saṃskṛtam*'s engineered sound-system, including the precise tongue-placement required for the retroflex consonants ट ड ण (the shared subcontinent-wide retroflex base) and the retroflex lateral ळ (which the Vedic mode preserves and the *bhāṣāyām* codification bounds out). On the tradition's own classification, a population entering the subcontinent from a sound-field that lacked retroflex articulation would have failed the test the tradition itself applies — and would have been classified, matter-of-factly, as *mleccha*, speakers outside the refined sound-system.

The chapter develops the test along four trails:

1. **The Maharashtrian *mleccha*-against-invader tradition** supplies the chapter's opening — Samarth Ramdas's strategic-advice verse to Sambhaji Maharaj; Savarkar's Ratnagiri-period rhetorical-loophole anecdote, in which the audience completes the verse with the suppressed *म्लेंच्छांवरी*. The political-emotional register Ramdas codified and Savarkar carried forward is the chapter's setup; the §9.1 hammer-question pivots to the Sanskrit-technical question that §§9.2–8.3 answer.
2. **The *mūrdhanya* apparatus as engineered phonetic structure** — the tongue-curl mechanism placing ट ड ण at approximately 7 cm into the vocal tract (adult-male reference ~17 cm), structural midpoint of a bounded selective sampling grid (labial ~0 cm / dental ~3 cm / retroflex ~7 cm / palatal ~9 cm / velar ~12 cm) the *varṇamālā* deploys. ट ड ण constitute the shared subcontinent-wide retroflex base; Sanskrit's *mahāprāṇa* additions (ठ ढ) are a further engineering elaboration on top of the base, not part of it. Two structurally distinct kinds of exclusion shape the design: *adjacent-exclusion* of the English dental fricative *th* at ~2 cm and the entire alveolar / post-alveolar region between ~4–6 cm where English crowds three positions; *range-boundary-exclusion* of the Arabic deep-pharyngeal at ~15 cm. The retroflex is not a feature Sanskrit happened to have but the central anchor of a sampling someone designed.
3. **The retroflex lateral ळ as load-bearing case study** — the Vedic mode preserves ळ as load-bearing through the *Prātiśākhya* and *pāṭha* apparatus; the *bhāṣāyām* codification bounds it out. The "loss" of ळ in Classical Sanskrit is a codification choice, not phonetic decay. Operative reality outside the *bhāṣāyām* perimeter (Vedic mode, regional Prakrits, Maharashtri-to-Marathi line, central forest-belt substrate, southern subcontinental languages) continues running with ळ in place. The *Aṣṭādhyāyī*'s own synchronic distinction between *chandasi* (Vedic mode) and *bhāṣāyām* (generative-analytical mode) treats this as a mode-distinction, not a stage-distinction. §9.3 does the subcontinental-anchor work for ळ specifically; the broader substrate-plus-calibrant model belongs to the companion paper.
4. **The test inverted** — AIT's "invading *ārya*" fails the phonetic-pedagogical test the tradition itself applies; the English who built the framework to claim *ārya* for themselves were, on every Indic standard the categories carry, the structural *mleccha*. Buddha-Assalāyana citation as supporting primary-source authority; Hollywood-caricature material as modern-popular-culture confirmation. The §9.4 hammer-closer: *Savarkar was technically accurate*; the English failed the test of *āryatva* on two counts simultaneously — as brutal, oppressive plunderers (political-emotional) and as mouths that could not produce the engineered Indic sound-system (technical-Sanskrit).

The chapter closes with the **pedagogical reading**: *āryatva* is achievement, not inheritance. The engineered phonetic apparatus is open to all who undergo the *guru-shishya* training. The Rigvedic call *kṛṇvanto viśvam āryam* makes sense only on this reading — race cannot be made; pedagogical-civilizational mastery can. Forward-pointer to the Epilogue mantra-landing.

## 2. Detailed Outline

### 2.1 §9.1 — The Test of Āryatva

**Opening: the Maharashtrian *mleccha*-against-occupier tradition.**

Open the chapter with the seventeenth-century Marathi political register. **Samarth Ramdas's strategic-advice verse to Sambhaji Maharaj** uses *mleccha* (म्लेच्छ) matter-of-factly to designate the occupying forces — the term is Sanskrit-technical in origin and seventeenth-century-Marathi-political in operative deployment. Pivot to **Savarkar's Ratnagiri internment writing (1924–1937)** redeploying the same *mleccha*-vocabulary against the contemporary colonial occupier — twentieth-century Marathi political register, continuous with the seventeenth-century usage, still anchored in the Sanskrit-technical foundation.

The **triple convergence**: the British uniquely fit the *mleccha* designation in three senses simultaneously:

- **Sanskrit technical** — they cannot produce the *mūrdhanya* set; the phonetic-pedagogical test the tradition itself applies returns them as outside the refined sound-system.
- **Seventeenth-century Marathi political** — Samarth Ramdas's *mleccha* designation for the contemporary occupying force, used as a strategic-political category.
- **Twentieth-century Marathi political** — Savarkar's continued use of the same vocabulary for the same structural relationship, the colonial successor in a different formal register.

The three readings converge on the same population by independent paths. The classification holds without any need to import racial-genealogical categories — the test the tradition applies is phonetic-pedagogical, and the test the British fail is the same test the tradition has been applying for many generations.

**Two-modes framework introduced.**

Brief introduction of the **two modes** of Sanskrit operative throughout the chapter: the **Vedic mode** (recitational-preservational; preserves accent, meter, recitation, and the full retroflex apparatus including the retroflex lateral ळ that §9.3 treats as load-bearing case study) and the **generative-analytical mode** (Pāṇinian *bhāṣāyām*; preserves the rules by which sound becomes word). Both modes are synchronically available within the *Aṣṭādhyāyī*'s own framework — *chandasi* rules and *bhāṣāyām* rules operating in parallel, not in historical sequence. The retroflex consonants ट ड ण are engineered in both modes and constitute the shared subcontinent-wide retroflex base; the chapter's argument operates across both modes and across the parallel non-codified regional speech-fields that continued outside the *bhāṣāyām* perimeter.

**Hammer close for §9.1.**

The test of *āryatva* is the test of being able to enter the engineered Indic sound-field. On that test, the AIT-imagined "invading *ārya*" fails — and the tradition itself, on its own categories, would have called them by the term that fits.

### 2.2 §9.2 — The Mūrdhanya Apparatus

**The shared subcontinent-wide retroflex base.**

The core retroflex consonants ट ड ण constitute the shared subcontinent-wide retroflex hardware. The set is anchored in a specific tongue-placement: the tongue tip curls backward toward the hard palate at roughly the midpoint of the vocal tract. The three consonants ट ड ण recur — with the same articulatory mechanism — across the Vedic mode of Sanskrit, the post-Pāṇinian *bhāṣāyām* mode, the regional Prakrits, Marathi, the central forest-belt languages (Korku, Mundari, Santali, Ho, Sora), and the southern subcontinental languages. The base set is subcontinent-wide, not Sanskrit-specific.

**The *mahāprāṇa* additions as Sanskrit's engineering elaboration.**

Sanskrit's *varṇamālā* elaborates the shared base by adding *mahāprāṇa* (aspirated) consonants to the *alpaprāṇa* (unaspirated) base: ट → ठ, ड → ढ, with ण without a *mahāprāṇa* doublet in the same way. The *alpaprāṇa* / *mahāprāṇa* contrast is itself an engineering decision — a phonological dimension that Sanskrit's apparatus encodes systematically but that is not universal across the languages of the subcontinent. Tamil's classical phonology, for instance, does not operate the *mahāprāṇa* contrast at this level. The shared subcontinent-wide retroflex base is ट ड ण; the *mahāprāṇa* additions ठ ढ are a further engineering elaboration on top of the base, native to Sanskrit's *varṇamālā* and to the Sanskrit-derived languages that inherit it.

The chapter's argument that retroflexion is a subcontinent-wide native phonetic feature operates on the base set. The *mahāprāṇa* elaboration is a separate (and load-bearing) engineering decision in Sanskrit's own design, recoverable from the *varṇamālā*'s structural specifications. The empirical question of how Sanskrit's calibrant register inflects regional substrate languages — what the *mahāprāṇa* additions and the broader engineered apparatus contribute when meeting the central-forest-belt substrate, the western-coastal substrate, the southern substrate — belongs to *The Subcontinental Calibrant* companion paper.

**The geometry of the engineered articulation grid.**

The retroflex base sits at a specific structural position in the engineered apparatus the *varṇamālā* deploys. Sanskrit's consonantal grid samples five points along the vocal tract — measured along the midline from the lips to the glottis, with an adult-male reference length of approximately 17 cm:

- ~0 cm — **labial** (*pavarga* — प फ ब भ म), articulated at the lips
- ~3 cm — **dental** (*tavarga* — त थ द ध न), articulated at the back face of the upper teeth
- ~7 cm — **retroflex** (*ṭavarga* — ट ठ ड ढ ण), articulated by curling the tongue tip back to the mid-palate
- ~9 cm — **palatal** (*cavarga* — च छ ज झ ञ), articulated at the hard palate
- ~12 cm — **velar / guttural** (*kavarga* — क ख ग घ ङ), articulated at the soft palate

The intervals between included positions — approximately 3, 4, 2, 3 cm — are not strictly equidistant. The grid is doing something more sophisticated than equal spacing: it samples five well-separated positions across the front-to-back range, with each pair of adjacent positions separated by at least ~2 cm for clean acoustic distinguishability. The retroflex base at ~7 cm sits structurally central — the midpoint anchor of the five-point sampling, with the front cluster (labial, dental) and back cluster (palatal, velar) bracketing it on either side. The design pattern scales with speaker physiology; the adult-female reference of approximately 14–15 cm produces the same sampling pattern at smaller scale.

Chapter 7 develops the full *varṇamālā* grid as engineered design; the placement of the retroflex at the center of the bounded sampling is one specific case of the engineering Chapter 7 demonstrates across the whole grid.

**What the apparatus deliberately excludes.**

The five-point sampling is not the maximum possible inventory of articulation points along the vocal tract. Two structurally distinct kinds of exclusion shape the design.

**Adjacent-exclusion.** The English phonological apparatus includes a dental fricative articulation at approximately 2 cm — the consonant represented as *th* (θ, ð), produced by placing the tongue tip between or at the front face of the upper teeth. The Indic dental at ~3 cm (back face of the teeth) sits only ~1 cm behind it. Sanskrit's *varṇamālā* includes the dental position and bounds out the interdental — a deliberate choice not to distinguish two articulation points that sit too close for clean acoustic separation.

The same pattern of adjacent-exclusion governs the entire ~4 cm gap between the Indic dental (~3 cm) and the Indic retroflex (~7 cm). The intervening region houses the alveolar (~4 cm), the post-alveolar (~5–6 cm), and the palato-alveolar (~6 cm) positions — the heart of the English consonantal apparatus, where *t*, *d*, *n*, *s*, *z*, *l*, *r*, *sh*, *ch*, and *j* all live. Sanskrit's grid crosses this entire region in a single step. The five vargas of the *varṇamālā* include no alveolar or post-alveolar varga. The grid was designed to skip this densely-populated region.

**Range-boundary-exclusion.** The Arabic phonological apparatus includes a deep-pharyngeal articulation at approximately 15 cm — the consonants represented as ع and ح, produced by constricting the throat well behind the velum. The Indic velar at ~12 cm is the back-end of Sanskrit's included range; positions deeper than the velum are bounded out. The pharyngeal is physiologically available and operative in fully developed phonological systems; Sanskrit's grid does not extend to it.

The exclusions are not accidents. The grid was assembled by deliberate choice — five well-separated positions across the front-to-back range, with intermediate positions skipped (adjacent-exclusion: the interdental, the alveolar / post-alveolar region) and deep-back positions bounded out (range-boundary-exclusion: the pharyngeal). The retroflex is not a feature Sanskrit happened to have. It is the central anchor of a bounded selective sampling that the engineering thesis says someone designed — chosen from what the subcontinental sound-field made available, refined into a grid of maximum acoustic separation, and held in that grid through the *varṇamālā*'s formal apparatus.

**Hammer close for §9.2.**

*This is what design looks like.*

### 2.3 §9.3 — ळ and the Codification Perimeter

**The retroflex lateral as load-bearing case study.**

The retroflex consonants ट ड ण constitute the shared subcontinent-wide base §9.2 establishes. The retroflex lateral ळ does specific structural work the base set does not — and gets its own treatment here because the codification-perimeter argument the engineering thesis turns on lands cleanest on ळ.

The Vedic mode preserves ळ as load-bearing. **The opening word of the Rigveda — *अग्निमीळे पुरोहितम्* (*agnimīḷe purohitam*) — places the retroflex lateral as the second consonant of the foundational text**.[NOTE: agnimīḷe-rigveda-opening] The *Prātiśākhya* apparatus and the *pāṭha* hierarchy lock ळ in place across the recension; the engineered preservation system that holds the *Saṃhitā* against drift holds the retroflex lateral with it.

**The codification-perimeter argument.**

The *Aṣṭādhyāyī* treats the retroflex lateral differently across its two modes. The *chandasi* rules — governing the Vedic mode — fully include ḷ. The *bhāṣāyām* rules — governing the generative-analytical mode — do not include ḷ in the phonological inventory. Pāṇini's grammar is explicit that this is a mode distinction, not a stage distinction: the *Aṣṭādhyāyī* is itself a synchronic specification of both modes operating in parallel.[NOTE: chandasi-bhāṣāyām-aṣṭādhyāyī]

The Sanskrit-internal evidence for the codification-perimeter reading: Pāṇini did not claim ḷ did not exist in operative Sanskrit; he assigned it to one mode and bounded it out of the other. The codification was a design choice — the *bhāṣāyām* mode optimized for rule-based completeness as the analytical-generative apparatus required, and the retroflex lateral sat outside the scope that optimization required.

**What the codification did and did not do.**

The codification did not change the operative subcontinental sound-field. The Vedic mode continued operating with ळ in place — preservation engineering of the *Prātiśākhya* / *Śikṣā* / *pāṭha* apparatus held it. The regional speech-fields — the prakritic continuums that fed into Maharashtri and from there into Marathi — continued operating with ळ. The central forest-belt languages continued operating with their native retroflex laterals. The southern subcontinental languages continued operating with their own retroflex-lateral phonemes (Tamil ழ, Malayalam ഴ, Telugu ళ, Kannada ಳ — distinct from each other in articulatory detail, anchored in the same retroflex-lateral category). What the *bhāṣāyām* codification did was draw a perimeter for one specific mode of Sanskrit; outside the perimeter, the operative sound-field was larger than the codification claimed.

**The drop-vs-did-not-include question set aside.**

Whether Pāṇini *dropped* ळ from non-Vedic pre-Pāṇinian Sanskrit (the feature was present in the operative speech-field and the codification chose to bound it out) or whether ळ had *already* differentiated to Vedic-mode-only by the codification's moment (and Pāṇini's *bhāṣāyām* simply did not include it because it was no longer operative there) — both readings are consistent with the surviving evidence. The pre-Pāṇinian non-Vedic Sanskrit prose tradition survives only through post-Pāṇinian transmission that normalized phonology to the *bhāṣāyām* standard, so the question is not settleable from the extant textual record.

The engineering-thesis argument does not require the question to be settled. Either way:

- The codification was a design choice with a phonological perimeter.
- ळ sat outside the *bhāṣāyām* perimeter.
- The Vedic mode held ळ in place through the engineered preservation apparatus.
- The regional speech-fields, the Maharashtri-to-Marathi line, the central forest-belt substrate, and the southern subcontinental sound-field continued operating with retroflex laterals.
- The operative subcontinental reality outside the codification was larger than the codification claimed.

The specific empirical question — drop vs already-differentiated — is flagged for the *Subcontinental Calibrant* companion paper's empirical investigation (Brāhmaṇa-prose phonological survey, Pāṇinian *vārttikā*-and-*bhāṣya* commentary mining for retained ḷ-references, Maharashtri Prakrit textual archaeology).

**The implication for AIT.**

The standard AIT-aligned framing reads Marathi ळ as a Dravidian or Munda substrate intrusion into an "Indo-Aryan"–descended language. The codification-perimeter reading inverts the framing: ळ is not an intrusion. It is the continuous subcontinental sound-field that was always there, surviving alongside the bounded Pāṇinian codification, preserved in the Vedic mode and in every regional speech-field that the *bhāṣāyām* perimeter did not claim to govern. The "Classical Sanskrit without ळ" is not a base reality from which Marathi diverged. It is a bounded codification that the wider Indic linguistic reality lived outside of from the beginning.

**Hammer close for §9.3.**

ळ lives in the Vedic mode and runs continuously across the subcontinent. The codification did not bring it; the codification calibrated against it. The mouth was here first.

### 2.4 §9.4 — The Test Inverted

**The AIT framework tested against its own *ārya*.**

The AIT framework imports an *ārya* population from outside the subcontinent — from the Pontic-Caspian steppe in some reconstructions, from Central Asia in others. The framework requires this population to have authored Sanskrit. The retroflex test the tradition itself applies returns the verdict: a population whose native sound-field does not include the retroflex set cannot produce ट ड ण without years of trained tongue-placement work. The retroflex base is the engineered output of an apparatus that anchored in the subcontinental sound-field; arriving from outside means arriving without the apparatus.

On the tradition's own three-fold classification of *mleccha* — Sanskrit technical (phonetic-pedagogical failure), Marathi political (occupying force), Marathi continued-political (colonial successor in different formal register) — the AIT-imagined "invading *ārya*" is the structural *mleccha* in every sense.

**The Buddha-Assalāyana citation as primary-source authority.**

The *Assalāyana Sutta* (Majjhima Nikāya 93) preserves a primary-source observation that bears directly on the *ārya / dāsa* binary AIT requires. The Buddha, in dialogue with Assalāyana on the question of varna, notes that the bordering nations of *Yona* and *Kamboja* — Greek and Iranian-frontier regions — have only two varnas, *ārya* and *dāsa*, where the dharmic-Indic frame has the full fourfold *varṇa* system.[NOTE: assalayana-sutta] The observation does specific structural work: the *ārya / dāsa* binary, which AIT places at the foundation of Indic civilizational history, is documented by the dharmic tradition itself as a **foreign-bordering-nations feature** — a category the Yona / Kamboja populations operated within, not a category the dharmic tradition originated from or imposed on others. The binary AIT projects backward onto Indic prehistory is, on the tradition's own primary-source observation, an externally-located framework.

The Buddha-Assalāyana citation lands here as supporting primary-source authority. The primary land for the citation is the Epilogue's mantra-landing; here in Ch9 it operates as confirmatory evidence for the inversion the chapter performs.

**The Hollywood-caricature material as modern-popular-culture confirmation.**

The contemporary popular-culture register confirms the phonetic test at the level of trained-actor caricature. Peter Sellers in *The Party*; Hank Azaria's Apu in *The Simpsons*; the long catalog of non-native Indian-accent caricature deploys consistent substitutions — alveolar consonants replacing the retroflex base, dental consonants replacing ट ड ण, the tongue's resting placement failing to find the curl. The caricature is the test passing its own diagnostic: every time a non-native attempts the Indic sound-field, the failure to produce the retroflex base surfaces immediately. The general audience hears it. The cultural-stereotype apparatus has been operating on the phonetic test for decades without naming it as such.

This material is supporting evidence for the structural argument, not the structural argument itself. If on revision the §9.4 prose runs too long, the Hollywood material can move to an endnote.

**Hammer close for §9.4.**

The tradition's test, applied honestly, returns the verdict the tradition has always returned. The "invading *ārya*" of nineteenth-century philology was not the author of Sanskrit. The author of Sanskrit was the population that had been engineering its sound-field for generations before any "invasion" the framework imagines.

### 2.5 §9.5 — The Pedagogical Reading

***Āryatva* as achievement, not inheritance.**

The retroflex test the chapter has developed makes one structural point above all others: *āryatva* is **achievable**. The engineered phonetic apparatus is open to all who undergo the *guru-shishya* training — the trained tongue-placement, the cultivated breath, the disciplined attention to the *varṇamālā*'s structural specifications, the years of practice that turn the *mūrdhanya* set from impossible articulation to easy articulation.

The phonetic-pedagogical reading is internally consistent with the tradition's own classification. *Ārya* names the achievement; the *guru-shishya* paramparā is the mechanism; the *Vedāṅga* apparatus is the curriculum. Anyone who undergoes the curriculum becomes *ārya* in the tradition's own technical sense. There is no permanent exclusion. There is no genetic gate. There is no inherited claim. There is only the test, and the work that the test demands.

**The Rigvedic call makes sense only on this reading.**

The Rigvedic mantra *कृण्वन्तो विश्वमार्यम्* (*kṛṇvanto viśvam āryam*) — "making the whole world *ārya*" — is incoherent on the racial reading of *ārya*. Race cannot be made. Pedagogical-civilizational achievement can. The mantra refutes AIT on the tradition's own foundational primary-source authority. The Epilogue lands the full mantra; Ch9's contribution to the framing is to establish, through the phonetic-pedagogical reading of *āryatva*, that the mantra is operating on the only reading that makes the call coherent.

**Forward-pointer to the Epilogue.**

The Epilogue carries the mantra-landing in full. Ch9's work is to prepare the reading the Epilogue will land — to establish that *āryatva* is a phonetic-pedagogical achievement, anchored in the engineered Indic sound-field, available to all who would do the work the engineering thesis points toward. The reader who arrives at the Epilogue carrying the Ch9 reading carries the apparatus the Epilogue's call requires.
