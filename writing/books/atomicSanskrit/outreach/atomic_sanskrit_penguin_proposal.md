## What Kind of Order Does Freedom Protect?

After *Operation Red Lotus*, I wanted to understand what our ancestors had fought to protect. Freedom would allow Indians to establish स्वराज्य (*svarājya*), self-rule. But what would that mean? A less oppressive version of British or Islamic rule, with Indians occupying the same positions of authority? Or a fundamentally different relationship between government and society? This inquiry led me beyond who governs to how people create order together.

Most explanations of public order begin with government and the *constraints* it imposes. Someone makes the rules, institutions enforce them, and people live beneath their authority. Citizens may choose their government and limit its powers, but the arrangement still places the state above the society it governs. This book calls that arrangement the **pyramid**. Fear and punishment are its currency of order: people comply because disobedience carries a penalty.

Most people want to live peacefully, care for those around them, and pursue their lives without harming others. Some will deceive, exploit, or use violence. Must the conduct of that minority determine the order under which everyone else lives? *Pyramidal order treats everyone as an exception waiting to happen.*

This book argues that Sanātan offers a fundamentally different way of creating order. Its starting point is the restraint people learn to exercise themselves, rather than the constraints imposed upon them from above. Much of the knowledge behind that approach is no longer explained as a connected whole. Yet people continue to teach it through stories, recitations, and practices, often without connecting what they do with a larger conception of order.

Could these familiar practices help us understand how society can create order without depending on commands from an apex? Sanskrit became my starting point because its construction and the disciplines that keep it exact can still be examined directly. Sanskrit remains alive because of the partial victory we gained in the Anglo-Indian War of 1857. 

Readers need no prior knowledge of Sanskrit: the book explains its examples through familiar words, translations, and diagrams. *Atomic Sanskrit* examines how people keep a language exact through shared knowledge, teaching, and correction, without a central authority commanding them. Could those same principles recur at the scale of society, allowing people to create order through their own understanding and responsibility? This suggests an approach based on recursion: applying the same recurring principles or patterns within successively larger groups. The larger inquiry, *Second Shanti*, will develop that possibility from first principles, asking how it could help people across the world build a better future together.

## Has This Alternative Actually Existed?

Yes. The language system that *Atomic Sanskrit* examines already exists in full. Teachers, learners, and recitation lineages share the knowledge needed to recognize and correct errors. This book demonstrates how that system has kept Sanskrit unchanged for thousands of years without a central authority controlling its form.

As a child reciting the *Bhagavad Gītā*, I misplaced a sound. My mother caught the mistake because it changed the number of syllables in the line. The verse followed a prescribed pattern, and my recitation no longer fitted it. Restoring the sound also restored the pattern. She could therefore show me why the correction was necessary, rather than simply ask me to copy her pronunciation.

The check did not depend on my mother's personal preference, regional accent, or the way our family happened to pronounce the words. The verse itself provided the pattern against which any of us could check our recitation. Another trained listener elsewhere could identify the same error by examining the same pattern. I could learn to perform that check myself and eventually teach someone else. The verse carried a means of checking its own recitation, while people across different households and regions maintained the knowledge needed to use it. This introduces what the book calls a **calibrant**: a *shared reference* against which people can recognize and correct departures.

Meter, the pattern of syllable counts and durations in a verse, is one such check. The book examines how several checking methods reinforce one another, allowing communities separated by distance and generations to maintain the same precise reference. Reciters learn sounds and sequences; grammar explains how words are formed. The Vedas are the shared reference, or calibrant, that keeps the language exact. Different communities teach people how to use that reference and check their recitations against it. No single teacher or institution owns the standard.

## How Does Preserving a Language Relate to Order in Society?

Maintaining Sanskrit requires people to do more than remember words. They learn a standard they cannot change at will, exercise judgment in applying it, accept correction, and teach others to recognize the same errors. A teacher's correction can be examined against the shared reference. Responsibility therefore includes both knowing the form and being able to explain it.

The book argues that these relationships express Sanātan's conception of order. People acquire the knowledge to govern their own conduct and help others do the same. A shared standard makes their judgments accountable without becoming the property of a commanding authority. Sanskrit allows readers to examine how people sustain those relationships within a language system, across places and generations.

This is the connection the series will develop. *Atomic Sanskrit* examines the language system in detail. Later volumes will consider how discernment, restraint, and shared responsibility can shape government, economic life, and other relationships among living beings.

## Is This a Description of India Today or a Proposal for Its Future?

Present-day India does not fully embody the order this series investigates. Much of the knowledge through which Hindu society organized its public life has been damaged, interrupted, or displaced. Parts remain alive in teaching, stories, and everyday practice. Sanskrit is the first demonstration because its construction and transmission can still be examined as a connected system.

The larger task is to develop a polity from first principles. The inquiry begins with the needs and responsibilities of living beings and asks how they can create order together. What must government protect? How should responsibilities be shared? How should society respond to deception, exploitation, and violence without treating everyone as a potential offender? What prevents those entrusted with protection from taking ownership of the standards they protect?

Later volumes will develop those arguments. Their method will be to reason through what such an order requires, using Sanskrit's existing system as a demonstration of principles that people have already sustained in practice. The aim is to establish how an order can function, rather than reconstruct a past government from historical fragments or treat translations of old prescriptions as a ready-made polity.

## Why Does This Inquiry Matter Now?

In conversations about the book, I have found that the prospect of an inside-out order draws interest beyond Sanskrit learners. I see the growing Hindu assertiveness since 2014 creating a readership that wants more than an affirmation of national pride. These readers sense that India has a distinctive conception of civilization, yet the political explanations available to them often remain those of Western nationalism: who holds power, whose interests the state advances, and how strong the nation becomes. They want to understand what makes India's worldview different and what that difference could mean for public life.

Families, teachers, and regional-language traditions have kept knowledge alive through recitations, stories, and practices. Many English-educated Indians recognize that inheritance but lack an explanation connecting it with public life. English-language education has often taught them to regard those practices as ritual, mythology, or an obsolete past. Living an inheritance and being able to explain what it accomplishes are different things.

*Atomic Sanskrit* addresses that gap. It examines what familiar practices do, how people have maintained them, and why they matter beyond worship or the classroom. Its appeal is the possibility that knowledge dismissed as belonging to the past can help people think differently about how they live together now and what kind of future they want to create.

## What Will This Book Show, and Must Readers Know Sanskrit?

Readers will examine practices they may have inherited without knowing what those practices accomplish. Repeating a Vedic passage in different arrangements checks its words and sequence. A verse's meter can expose a missing syllable. These disciplines allow people to detect mistakes and protect knowledge long after a particular teacher has died or an institution has disappeared.

The book also explains how an unchanged language can name something new, why writing down a grammar cannot by itself stop a language from changing, and why an exact written record remains vulnerable to decay, destruction, and control. Familiar words and recitations become examples readers can examine for themselves.

Readers do not need to know Sanskrit. The language is the *system* being examined; knowledege is not a condition for understanding the explanation. Sanskrit appears in Devanagari and in Roman letters using IAST, the International Alphabet of Sanskrit Transliteration, with English explanations. Diagrams locate sounds in the mouth, show how words are assembled, and make the checks used in recitation visible. Readers can follow the examples before learning the technical names for what they have seen.

## What Does It Mean to Call Sanskrit Engineered?

The explanation begins with the speaking body. Sanskrit selects sounds and assigns them positions according to where and how the mouth produces them. Sounds combine into धातवः (*dhātavaḥ*), small units that carry meanings and that this book calls atoms. Speakers form words from these atoms, add endings to establish relationships, and combine words into sentences. Readers follow the construction through diagrams and worked examples.

English has changed over the centuries, accumulating several hundred thousand words as its speakers adapted to a changing world. An adult native English speaker may recognize [roughly forty to fifty thousand words](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01116/full). Sanskrit's architecture handles change differently. The book demonstrates how its design can generate more than twelve million word-meanings, with scope for further expansion. Although that architecture is thousands of years old, it allows speakers to name new discoveries, technologies, and experiences. Sanskrit can remain unchanged and yet remain useful to an ever-changing world.

Readers can see this capacity in familiar words. From ⟪कृ⟫ (*kṛ*, to do or make), Sanskrit forms कर्म (*karma*, action), कार्य (*kārya*, what is to be done), and कर्तृ (*kartṛ*, doer or maker). The same architecture also accommodates new purposes. चन्द्रयान (*Candrayāna*), formed from words for Moon and vehicle, names a modern spacecraft through procedures that long predate space travel.

## How Has Sanskrit Kept That Architecture Unchanged?

Creating new words is one engineering achievement. Keeping the system that creates them unchanged across thousands of years requires another: systems engineering, the design of interacting parts and procedures that keep the whole functioning. People can build a language and then change it through ordinary use. They can document its grammar without stopping its drift. The book compares Sanskrit with Tamil, Esperanto, Latin, and Arabic to distinguish grammatical documentation, institutional enforcement, and distributed calibration.

Sanskrit's system must resist two enemies. The first is entropy: unintended change that accumulates as people speak, remember, and teach. The second is deliberate attack. The book uses **asuric** for conduct directed toward containment, concealment, and control. Those who attack knowledge in this way destroy its carriers, capture institutions, or teach later generations to misunderstand what survived. A written record alone cannot protect against both enemies. It can decay, burn, be altered, or fall under the control of those who authorize its circulation.

The Vedas function as Sanskrit's calibrant. Exact recitation protects their sound, wording, sequence, pitch, and meter. Different recitation procedures check different features, while teachers in geographically separated lineages pass on the ability to make those checks. Capturing one institution cannot give an attacker ownership of the complete standard.

The book explains two domains within Sanskrit. The वैदिक (*vaidika*) domain protects the exact forms used in Vedic recitation. The लौकिक (*laukika*) domain allows new poetry, mathematics, medicine, philosophy, trade, and everyday expression through the same language. One responsibility keeps the reference unchanged; the other keeps the language useful in a changing world. The book treats these as complementary domains, not successive stages of a language's evolution.

## If the Architecture Already Existed, What Did Pāṇini Do?

Pāṇini, the Sanskrit grammarian, decoded and documented it with exceptional precision. The Vedas already used the sounds, forms, and grammatical procedures he explained. Earlier analysts had examined the same language. Pāṇini continued their inquiry and described its grammatical procedures through an extraordinarily compact and systematic set of rules.

The book rejects the codification story because it assigns him a different role: an authority who imposed order on drifting speech. That account credits one celebrated individual with keeping Sanskrit unchanged, while overlooking the communities that maintained it across generations. Pāṇini's achievement becomes clearer when his documentation is placed within the system that preceded him and continued after him.

## What Makes This Architecture Distributed, Radiant, Calibrant, and Fractal?

**Distributed.** A teacher in one region can recognize a mistake that another teacher elsewhere would also recognize. The knowledge needed to make those judgments belongs to households, teachers, and recitation lineages across the Indian subcontinent, north and south, east and west. Different communities maintain different responsibilities without giving any one region or institution ownership of the shared standard. The book also argues for Sanskrit's subcontinental home by comparing its sounds with those of Indian languages that Western academics assign to separate families.

**Radiant.** Teachers, travelers, and communities carried Sanskritic words and methods beyond India, where other speakers adopted and reshaped what they learned. The book calls this outward spread radiance. Its Radiance Thesis argues that similarities between Sanskrit, Greek, Latin, and other languages record that influence, which Western accounts conceal by assigning those languages to an unrecorded common ancestor.

**Calibrant.** The Vedic reference remains available to those who learn it. A listener can compare a recitation with that reference and explain a correction, as my mother did. No teacher or institution needs to own the reference before someone else can use it. Its constancy allows people in different places and generations to check their speech against the same standard.

**Fractal.** A fractal has a recognizable pattern that recurs at different scales. Recursion helps explain how such a pattern can arise: the same organizing process or pattern is applied again within the structure it creates. The result is a recurring pattern in both smaller parts and larger wholes. The book examines this organization within Sanskrit, from sounds to words and sentences. The opening asked whether those principles could recur at the scale of society. *Second Shanti* will explore that possibility through relationships among living beings.

## Why Is the Series Called Second Shanti?

Hindus often say शान्तिः (*śāntiḥ*, quietude) three times. The three invocations concern three domains. The first concerns disturbances within the person. The second concerns relationships with other people and living beings. The third concerns natural and cosmic forces beyond direct human control that nevertheless affect life.

Hindu society has kept alive practices through which a person can seek quietude in the **first domain**. Yoga and meditation remain familiar examples. Much of the knowledge through which it organized the second was damaged under centuries of Abrahamic pyramidal rule. Institutions were destroyed, memory was interrupted, and public life was reorganized around commands from above. *Second Shanti* will reason from first principles about how living beings can create order in this second domain.

Every volume concerns relationships among living beings in the second domain. I interpret the three śāntis as a *fractal* map of the universe, extending from the person through relationships among beings to the wider natural and cosmic order. The final volume will develop that interpretation in full.

## Why Does the Book Confront Western Philology and the Indian Academic Establishment?

Philology studies language and its history through written records. The book argues that Western philology's classifications conceal the architecture the reader has just examined. It describes Sanskrit's atoms as botanical roots, its sound-grid as an alphabet, its two domains as historical stages, and its Vedic calibrant as early religious literature. Indian academic institutions continue to teach those categories as the accepted explanation of their own inheritance.

I call the order in which these institutions claim the right to define and certify knowledge the **asuric pyramid**. Authorized interpreters decide what Sanskrit and the Vedas represent, while the people who have taught and transmitted them are expected to accept that verdict. The book examines the methods and institutions that sustain this hierarchy, including what it calls the fourth Abrahamic religion: progressivism, with its assumption that the present must stand above the past.

Proto-Indo-European, or PIE, is the reconstructed language proposed as the common ancestor of Sanskrit, Greek, Latin, and other languages. It is central to this dispute. The pyramid places that reconstructed ancestor above Sanskrit and explains the recorded language through it. *Atomic Sanskrit* begins by analyzing Sanskrit's recorded sounds, words, and grammar. It then examines how other languages adopted Sanskritic words and methods and changed them through their own usage.

The book calls the foreign-origin account the **Racial Arya Thesis**, or RAT. It challenges the attribution of Sanskrit's authorship to incoming populations: evidence that people moved does not establish that they brought a language rather than learned it. Its argument for Sanskrit's subcontinental home examines the language's sounds, its grammatical procedures, and the communities that have maintained its connection with the Vedas.

The dispute reaches beyond linguistic ancestry. Concealing Sanskrit's architecture also conceals a working alternative to the claim that lasting order requires a commanding apex. The book names the institutions and methods responsible because recovering the architecture requires readers to recognize how it was hidden.

## How Does the Book Take the Reader Through the Argument?

The book follows the Vedic account of an eclipse: Svarbhānu covers the Sun, and the Atris, a lineage of seekers, find the radiance behind the darkness. Sanskrit is the Sun in the book's visual narrative. The pyramid's classifications form the blocks that conceal it. As the chapters examine and challenge those claims, successive illustrations show the eclipse clearing.

Two opening chapters introduce the seekers and the opposing apex. Parts I and II examine the classifications imposed on Sanskrit and explain how its own analysts understood formation and change. Parts III and IV show how the mouth produces sounds and how Sanskrit assembles them into atoms, words, and sentences. Part V examines the greater system that keeps the language calibrated. Parts VI and VII challenge the foreign-origin account and examine how people carried Sanskritic knowledge beyond India. The epilogue connects the language's distributed correction to responsibility, discernment, and order among living beings.

The figures remain part of the explanation throughout. Readers can inspect how a form is assembled or a recitation is checked while following the larger argument. Appendices and fuller references contain the denser technical detail without interrupting the main narrative.

## Who Is the Book For?

Although Sanskrit is in the title, the book's primary audience consists of English-speaking Hindu readers keen to understand a distinctly Indian worldview. Many care deeply about India and defend its civilization, but want an explanation of what makes it different beyond national identity or pride in its achievements. The book asks them to consider whether स्वराज्य (*svarājya*) means strengthening the state they inherited or developing a different relationship between government and society. Readers of *Operation Red Lotus* will recognize the continuing inquiry into what freedom protects.

Engineers, software developers, scientists, and other technically minded readers may be drawn to a different part of the argument: how a system can remain precise for thousands of years while people continue to use it in new ways. They will recognize the use of reusable components, repeated error checks, and knowledge maintained in more than one place. The worked examples explain how Sanskrit's construction and Vedic transmission use these methods without requiring prior knowledge of the language.

Sanskrit learners and teachers will find an account that connects pronunciation, word formation, grammar, and Vedic recitation. It explains how these disciplines reinforce one another and why their connection matters beyond the classroom.

Readers in the Indian diaspora face the additional task of explaining that inheritance to children growing up elsewhere. The book gives them concrete examples to discuss. Its account includes southern and central Indian languages alongside northern ones, allowing readers from different regions to examine the argument for Sanskrit's subcontinental home.

## How Does It Differ from Other Books?

The book connects two subjects usually discussed separately: how Sanskrit works and how people create order together. Its distinctive claim is that the language and the people who maintain it demonstrate principles from which a wider polity can be developed. The following titles indicate related readerships and the differences in approach:

- **Peggy Mohan's *Wanderers, Kings, Merchants* (India Viking, 2021)** connects language and Indian history through migration and contact. *Atomic Sanskrit* begins instead with the language's construction and calibration, separating those from the movement of its speakers. [Publisher](https://www.penguin.co.in/book/wanderers-kings-merchants/)
- **Mohan's *Father Tongue, Motherland* (India Hamish Hamilton, 2025)** examines language mixture and the relationship between incoming vocabulary and local grammar. *Atomic Sanskrit* challenges that explanatory starting point through the combined architecture of sounds, grammar, and preservation. [Publisher](https://www.penguin.co.in/book/father-tongue-motherland/)
- **Rajiv Malhotra's *The Battle for Sanskrit* (HarperCollins)** addresses the struggle over who interprets Sanskrit and Indian civilization. *Atomic Sanskrit* develops its alternative through worked demonstrations of language engineering and distributed calibration. [Author](https://rajivmalhotra.com/product/battle-for-sanskrit/)
- **William Dalrymple's *The Golden Road* (Bloomsbury, 2024)** addresses India's transmission of ideas abroad. *Atomic Sanskrit* advances a different argument about the source and direction of that influence and connects it to the system that sustains knowledge within India. [Publisher](https://www.bloomsbury.com/uk/golden-road-9781408864449/)

## How Can the Book Reach Its Readers?

The project already includes explanatory diagrams, essays, and a website. These can support illustrated talks, interviews, excerpts, and reading groups. A talk can begin with a recitation mistake that listeners learn to recognize, or with a familiar Sanskrit word whose construction they can follow. The discussion can then explain why people have continued to teach those disciplines and what that continuity reveals about the civilization that sustains them.

Proposed outreach includes readers of *Operation Red Lotus*, Sanskrit teaching communities, Indian-history and civilizational forums, and engineering audiences interested in distributed systems. Regional-language conversations would help reach readers who know Indian concepts well but do not routinely read academic English. These are proposed routes to readers, not commitments from named institutions.
