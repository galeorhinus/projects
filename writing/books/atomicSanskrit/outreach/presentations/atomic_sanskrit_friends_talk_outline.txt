PURPOSE AND AUDIENCE

A 40-minute introduction for interested friends, with no Sanskrit or engineering
knowledge assumed. Allow a separate 10-15 minutes for discussion if the host can.
The presentation begins and ends with civilizational order. Sanskrit provides
the system through which the book examines that order; this is not primarily
a lesson in grammar or a chronological summary of all twenty-one chapters.

Opening promise: "You do not need to know Sanskrit. I want to show you why its
construction and preservation changed how I understood Sanatan."

OPENING ARGUMENT: FROM FREEDOM TO THE ARCHITECTURE OF SANATAN

Follow the author's introduction in AS-friends-presentation-intro.md:
Operation Red Lotus and three freedoms -> what is Swarajya for? -> Second
Shanti and the three domains -> constraint versus restraint -> what was lost,
what survived, and why first principles -> Sanskrit as the surviving system.

The audience should understand why this is a book about civilizational order
before encountering the sound surveys. The surveys then explain the Indian
resources from which the book argues Sanskrit was engineered. They are not
a separate introductory talk about pronunciation.

Keep three distinctions clear in the spoken explanation:
- The three freedoms of Operation Red Lotus are political, economic, and
  personal. They are not the three domains addressed by the Shantis.
- Much of the social and political order was destroyed; not every practice
  disappeared. Stories, recitations, and other traditions still carry knowledge.
- "Preserved exactly" refers to the language architecture and its protected
  reference, not a claim that every manuscript, lineage, or Sanskrit utterance
  escaped loss or error. Explain what survived and later show how it is checked.

belong to the author's account in Operation Red Lotus. Before slide production,
attach its exact passages and proclamation locator to the speaking notes.

connection; this planning pass does not independently verify the other details.
Explain "India remained a Hindu nation" as civilizational continuity despite

Use simple vocabulary without softening the book's challenge to the pyramid.

SLIDE 11 | Small-Small Things: An Indian Habit in English Too
Time: 1:30 | Elapsed: 18:45-20:15

On screen, use three successive reveals rather than one crowded table:
1. Across Indian languages: Hindi छोटे-छोटे बच्चे, little children;
   Tamil பளபள (paḷa-paḷa; पळ-पळ), sparkling;
   Korku DoDo-DoDo (डोडो-डोडो), seeing-seeing.
2. In Indian English: "small-small things"; "hot-hot coffee".
3. In Sanskrit: गृहे-गृहे (gṛhe-gṛhe), in every house.

Say: Begin with the familiar English phrases. These are recognizable Indian
ways of speaking English, not random duplication. Then show the regional
examples: repetition carries meaning across languages assigned to different
families. It can distribute something across people or places, emphasize a
quality, or express continuing activity. Return to Sanskrit's गृहे-गृहे and
explain that Sanskrit gives repetition defined grammatical uses.

Key line: "We carry this way of expressing meaning even when we speak English."

Meaning notes: "Small-small things" can refer to a number of little things;
it does not always mean "very small." "Hot-hot coffee" emphasizes heat.
Context matters, and the regional examples do not all perform the same operation.
Use "across Indian languages," not "every Indian language uses every pattern."
Reduplication also occurs outside India; the book's argument concerns the
regional cluster and Sanskrit's integration of it, not exclusive Indian ownership.
