# Studying Sanskrit. Governing Its Future.

## One Chart for the Friends' Talk

Draft chart content and source notes, 15 October 2026. An editable version is now Slide 2 of the six-slide presentation pilot. Its full-talk position is 05A, after the personal correction story and before the subcontinental sound surveys. The eighteenth-century opening establishes the background to the nineteenth-century developments.

The chart puts study, political power, missionary purpose, and language construction in the same historical frame. It does not turn their coexistence into evidence that everyone followed one coordinated plan.

| When | Who did what? | What the audience should notice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1784 / 1786 | William Jones helped establish the Asiatic Society, then proposed a common source for Sanskrit, Greek, and Latin. [1] | Admiration for Sanskrit accompanied an explanation that placed another language before it. |
| 1816 | Franz Bopp used Sanskrit conjugation in his comparison of Sanskrit, Greek, Latin, Persian, and Germanic forms. [2] | Sanskrit was central to the development of European comparative grammar. |
| 1821 | The Bombay Presidency government founded Pune's Hindoo College, later Deccan College, using the Dakshina grant. [3] | An existing source of support for Indian learning came under a British-administered college. |
| 1832 | Oxford filled the Boden Chair of Sanskrit, endowed to assist the Christian conversion of Indians. [4] | Here, studying Sanskrit explicitly served conversion. |
| 1835 | Macaulay called for stopping government printing of Sanskrit and Arabic books, closing the Sanskrit College at Calcutta, and directing resources toward English education. [5] | While Europeans studied Sanskrit, an influential British official sought to weaken its educational support in India. |
| 1857 | The new University of Calcutta adopted the London model; its Senate organized examinations and university rules. [6] | The state-backed university acquired authority to examine and certify learning. |
| 1861 | Schleicher published a language-family tree in his comparative grammar, developing the reconstruction of the ancestor now called Proto-Indo-European, or PIE. [7] | A hypothetical ancestor became the starting point for explaining Sanskrit's ancestry. |
| 1887 | Zamenhof published Esperanto, a deliberately constructed international language. [8] | European language design belongs in this period too. It is a comparison with Sanskrit's generativity, not evidence of an anti-Sanskrit mission. |

**The book's challenge:** Europe learned from Sanskrit while claiming the authority to explain its ancestry and, through imperial institutions, reshape the education available to Indians. Why should that history be treated as detached from power?

## How to Show It

The pilot uses one widescreen chart with three vertical tracks, with dates progressing within each track. This leaves room for readable event labels:

1. **Study Sanskrit:** Jones, Bopp.
2. **Control institutions and education:** Pune, Boden, Macaulay, Calcutta.
3. **Construct languages and language histories:** Schleicher; Esperanto as a separate, explicitly labelled comparison.

Use short event labels on screen. The table's fuller wording belongs in presenter notes and the accompanying handout. Reveal one lane at a time; keep earlier lanes visible. Dates are milestones, not equally spaced intervals. Avoid arrows from the missionary chair or Macaulay to Esperanto: those would imply a causal connection the sources do not establish.

Keep the book's authority icon beside the education lane. Use the existing PIE icon only after checking its appearance at projection size. Use Sanskrit's established atom imagery for the transition into the sound surveys, not as decoration around every historical event.

Allow 90 seconds. The revised outline gives this chart its own 05A slot and shortens the opening and closing by 15 seconds each, for a total of 40 minutes. Keep abugida on its later sonomer/audiograph slide: it is not a nineteenth-century event for this chart.

## Suggested Spoken Thread

"Look at these events together. Europeans were studying Sanskrit closely. Bopp used its grammar to compare languages. In India, the British government organized colleges and universities. At Oxford, the Boden endowment explicitly connected Sanskrit study with Christian conversion. Macaulay wanted public support redirected from Sanskrit and Arabic to English.

"Meanwhile, comparative philologists developed the hypothetical ancestor now called PIE. My book challenges the authority given to that reconstruction over the language we can actually examine. Esperanto belongs beside this history as a separate comparison: Europe was also deliberately designing a language.

"These were different people and projects. What I want us to examine is how admiration for Sanskrit coexisted with efforts to weaken its standing and control the account of its origins. Let us now look at the sounds themselves."

## Evidence and Wording

- **Conversion:** the Boden endowment supplies direct evidence of an institutional purpose. It does not establish the founding purpose of every Indian university.
- **Educational attack:** Macaulay's Minute supplies explicit proposals. Say he *called for* closure and reduced support; do not report all his proposals as accomplished facts. His call to stop printing concerned government-funded printing, not a universal ban on Sanskrit books.
- **Pune:** the college's foundation documents British administration and use of the Dakshina grant. That alone does not prove it was founded to destroy Sanskrit. The fuller argument about its subsequent role belongs to Appendix Part 1.
- **Universities:** Calcutta is the sourced example here. Colleges, Oxford professorships, learned societies, and Indian universities are different kinds of institution; do not label all of them universities or attribute their foundation to philologists.
- **PIE:** introduce it as an unattested, reconstructed common ancestor, then explain the book's disagreement. Jones's proposal predates the nineteenth century; Schleicher did not invent the entire hypothesis in 1861. His earlier tree dates to 1853. The 1861 entry identifies a specific publication, not the first-ever family tree.
- **Esperanto:** the chronology establishes a later constructed language, not Sanskrit borrowing or an intention to suppress Sanskrit. Zamenhof's stated international communication purpose differs from the Boden conversion mandate. Any proposed direct intellectual connection requires its own evidence.
- **Central argument:** the book's interpretation should be stated forcefully as its interpretation. Documented hostile purposes are stronger examples than unsupported claims that all European language projects shared those purposes.
