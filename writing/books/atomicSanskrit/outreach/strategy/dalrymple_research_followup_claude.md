## Verdict

**The strongest version of the Dalrymple criticism is now sourced, and it is not the version the brief currently proposes.** Two new findings carry it, both from the authorized preview and both previously unrecorded:

1. **Printed p. 4 makes Greece and India parallel, independent founts and partitions their spheres.** "Like ancient Greece, ancient India came up with a set of profound answers to the big questions… What Greece was first to Rome, then to the rest of the Mediterranean and European world, so at this period India was to South-east and Central Asia and even to China." India's influence on Europe is then routed through Baghdad in the eighth century CE. This is a demonstrable **effect of a frame**, in his own words: a structure in which India cannot have contributed to Greece's formative period, because Greece is the template against which India is measured. It requires no allegation of intent and no claim about the "Greek miracle" as a doctrine he defends.

2. **Pythagoras appears once in the inspected preview, and runs the opposite way** — Indian gurus at Taxila in 326 BCE questioning Alexander's Greeks "about Socrates, Pythagoras and Diogenes" (p. 33). The one place the name occurs, Greeks are the subject of Indian curiosity, not Indians a source for Greek thought.

**A direct Dalrymple statement on Aryan origins now exists, verified.** On 19 June 2017 he posted an article headlined "The Aryan Invasion Theory Is Finally Proven Right: Science - 1, Hindu Right-Wing - 0," reproducing the scoreline. Both earlier audits recorded this as not found. It establishes his position and his political framing of the opposition. It does **not** establish a racial thesis, and the scoreline is the article's wording, not his coinage.

**Three cautions against the draft as it stands.** The "DNA does not speak" passage will fail as written: Narasimhan et al. do not read language off DNA, and say so — their language conclusion rests on the Satem and Ruki isoglosses, with the genetics supplying a possible carrier. Pāṇini is named exactly once in the preview, in a subordinate clause about Taxila's former residents, so the Müller/Macdonell praise-then-erase rhythm has no Dalrymple counterpart on Pāṇini. And the full book remains uninspected: the only complete copy located is an unauthorized upload, which falls outside the prompt's lawful-access instruction and was not used.

## C01. The Parallel-Founts Frame — the Strongest Sourced Version of the Criticism

**Claim tested:** that Dalrymple protects Greek priority. **Result: supports**, in a precise and defensible form.

**Source:** William Dalrymple, *The Golden Road: How Ancient India Transformed the World*, Bloomsbury; authorized publisher preview, ePDF ISBN 9781526686619. Introduction, **printed p. 4** (PDF p. 19; printed p. 1 = PDF p. 16). Local copy: [`dalrymple_sources/golden_road_authorized_preview.pdf`](dalrymple_sources/golden_road_authorized_preview.pdf), archived 21 September 2026.

**Exact quotation (59 words):**

> Indian learning, Indian religious insights and Indian ideas are among the crucial foundations of our world. Like ancient Greece, ancient India came up with a set of profound answers to the big questions about what the world is, how it operates, why we are here and how we should live our lives. What Greece was first to Rome, then to the rest of the Mediterranean and European world, so at this period India was to South-east and Central Asia and even to China

**Context:** The sentence continues that India radiated "not by conquest but instead by sheer cultural allure and sophistication," and then: "In matters of science, astronomy and mathematics, India was to be a teacher of the Arab world, and hence Mediterranean Europe too." The Baghdad mechanism is spelled out at printed p. 11: Indian mathematics "first passed from India to Abbasid Baghdad in the late eighth century," where "the knowledge of ancient India was cross-fertilised with that of ancient Greece."

**What I inspected:** The archived authorized preview, text-extracted with `pdftotext -layout` and the printed page confirmed against the running head and the InDesign slug on the facing pages.

**What this establishes:** Three things, all from his own words. Greece is the **comparator**, India the term compared to it. The two are presented as **independent** originators — "Like ancient Greece, ancient India came up with" — which forecloses contribution in either direction at the formative stage. And the spheres are **geographically partitioned**: Greece westward to Rome and Europe, India eastward to Asia, with India's westward reach arriving later and via the Arabs.

**What remains inference:** That this is deliberate, or that Dalrymple would deny an earlier Indian contribution if asked. Neither follows. The argument available is about the structure of the frame and what it makes unsayable, which is exactly the distinction the brief asks to be preserved. Note also that the passage is *praise* — it is the strongest praise in the introduction — which is what makes it usable for the article's rhythm without overstatement.

## C02. Pythagoras Appears Once, and the Direction Is Reversed

**Claim tested:** whether the book engages possible Indian contributions to early Greek thought. **Result: contradicts** the premise that he engages it at all in the inspected text.

**Source:** Same preview, **printed p. 33** (running head "A Gale of Stillness 33").

**Exact quotation (18 words):**

> So the Greeks kept their clothes on, and the senior guru questioned them about Socrates, Pythagoras and Diogenes.

**Context:** Alexander's men meet the naked ascetics — "gymnosophists" — two miles outside Taxila in 326 BCE. The gurus question the Greeks about Greek philosophers. The Pyrrho discussion follows immediately on the same page and continues to p. 34.

**Term counts in the preview** (51 pages, text-extracted, case-insensitive): Pythagoras 1, Pythagorean 0, Veda 3, Vedic 2, Indus 7, Sanskrit 20, Greek 20, Greece 5, Pyrrho 2, "Aryan" 0, Harappa 0, steppe 0, migration 0, "miracle" 0, Sulba 0. The three "Arya" hits are all *Aryabhata*.

**What this establishes:** In the inspected portion, the single occurrence of Pythagoras places Indians as *questioners about* Greek philosophy rather than as a source for it. That is a usable observation about the book's directionality, and a much narrower claim than "he protects the Greek miracle" — which, as both prior audits found, is not established and is answered by the subtitle.

**Limits, stated plainly:** This is 51 pages of a 496-page book. Absence in a preview is not absence from the book. The counts above are evidence about the preview and nothing more.

## C04. Dalrymple's Own Words on Aryan Origins — Verified

**Claim tested:** that he accepts an external origin and rejects Out of India. **Result: supports**, with a political framing; both prior audits recorded this as not found.

**Source:** William Dalrymple (@DalrympleWill), X, **19 June 2017**. Canonical URL: https://x.com/DalrympleWill/status/876695416041091072

**Verification method:** X's own oEmbed endpoint returned the post text, author identity, and date — the same method the Codex audit used for the 2022 recommendation (S11). The endpoint 301-redirects; follow redirects or it returns an empty body. Local copy: [`dalrymple_sources/dalrymple_x_post_2017_06_19_oembed.json`](dalrymple_sources/dalrymple_x_post_2017_06_19_oembed.json), SHA-256 `9a831a17a384fabe2e7db80e0e9395db9c2de647edb729166ec797e572ba1563`, retrieved 23 September 2026.

**Exact post text (16 words):**

> Has the Aryan Invasion Theory been Finally Proven Right? Science - 1, Hindu Right-Wing - 0

**The link resolves to:** Ganesh Prasad, "The Aryan Invasion Theory Is Finally Proven Right: Science - 1, Hindu Right-Wing - 0," *RAIOT*, 19 June 2017. https://raiot.in/the-aryan-invasion-theory-is-finally-proven-right-science-1-hindu-right-wing-0/ — local copy `dalrymple_sources/raiot_aryan_invasion_2017_06_19.html`, SHA-256 `a02af0455685de751194c1fba85b741c56a30869cea58f5bb70e6d8a8dbb9aff`. The article argues from Moorjani et al. (2013) and Richards et al. (2017) Y-DNA that the infusion was male-dominated and therefore a military **invasion** rather than migration. It does not discuss Tony Joseph, Reich, or Rakhigarhi.

**Three distinctions that must be preserved, or this finding will be turned around:**

- **The scoreline is the article's headline, not his coinage.** He reproduced it. He also converted the declarative title into a question — a small hedge that a hostile reader will press.
- **Sharing is not line-by-line endorsement.** The prompt's own rule applies here as much as to the Joseph recommendation.
- **This is "invasion," and it sits awkwardly with his 2005 position.** In the 2005 NYRB piece he described "a slow seepage of pastoralists speaking Indo-Aryan languages" and expressly distinguished that from invasion by a single Aryan people (Codex S02). Promoting a 2017 article headlined "Aryan Invasion Theory… Proven Right" is in tension with his own earlier formulation. That tension is a legitimate thing to point out. Asserting that he holds a settled invasion position across two decades is not supported.

**What this establishes:** A dated, first-person, public endorsement of the external-origin account, and a characterization of the opposing position as "Hindu Right-Wing" rather than as a scholarly alternative. That second half is the more useful half for the article: it is the political dismissal, in his own words, that the 2005 article framed more discursively.

**What it does not establish:** Anything racial. Ancestry and invasion are not the racial Arya thesis. The four-way distinction in the first Claude audit — migration, language, ancestry, race — still holds and still has to be honored.

## C05. Vedic Religion as a Superseded Stage

**Claim tested:** that Buddhism functions as a foil and Vedic practice as the primitive thing left behind. **Result: supports.**

**Source:** Same preview, **printed p. 26**.

**Exact quotation (44 words):**

> The fire sacrifices that had been the staple of the early Hinduism of the Vedas, composed a thousand years or more before the birth of the Buddha, were designed for a nomadic and tribal society; but in the flux of change as India urbanised these had begun to give way

**Context:** The paragraph continues that ceremonies became "increasingly elaborate and expensive… sometimes involving the mass slaughter of large numbers of animals," and that "many seem to have yearned for a move away from blood sacrifices and towards religious practice more focused on good conduct and ethics." At p. 29: Buddhism "was open to all and required no priestly intervention nor any rituals or sacrifices."

**Two-way reading, both worth recording:**

- **For the article:** the Vedic corpus is characterized by what it was *for* — a "nomadic and tribal society" — and as the stage people "yearned" to move away from. The developmental sequence is explicit. This is the most direct preview support for the claim that Vedic India is framed as the superseded predecessor rather than the foundation.
- **Against a blanket version:** he dates the Vedas "a thousand years or more before the birth of the Buddha," which is an early dating freely given, and he asserts no external origin anywhere in the preview.

**Manuscript note (not an edit, a flag):** "nomadic and tribal society" uses vocabulary CLAUDE.md bans in the book's own prose. If quoted in the article, it must be quoted as his wording and attributed, not absorbed.

## C06. Contrary Evidence — Sanskrit's Antiquity Conceded

**Claim tested:** whether the 250 BCE start implies a denial of earlier Sanskrit. **Result: contradicts.**

**Source:** Same preview, **printed p. 3**.

**Exact quotation (34 words):**

> Sanskrit had been a profoundly sacred tongue for at least a millennium before the Common Era, but at some point between the first century bce and the first century ce, Sanskrit was reinvented as a literary and political language

**Context:** Immediately after the scope statement, "from about 250 bce to 1200 CE." Sheldon Pollock is invoked by name in the next sentence.

**What this establishes:** He concedes Sanskrit's antiquity explicitly. "Reinvented as a literary and political language" is a claim about changed *use*, not about the invention of the grammar — the Codex audit's reading (S01) is confirmed against the text. An article asserting that he dates Sanskrit from 250 BCE will be refuted from p. 3. The Pollock dependency is the more promising target, and the manuscript already engages Pollock.

## C07. Śulba Sūtra Priority — Real, but Priority Is Not Transmission

**Claim tested:** a concrete pre-500 BCE Indian mathematical achievement bearing on Greek foundations. **Result: supports priority; not found for transmission.**

**Indian source:** The Baudhāyana Śulba Sūtra contains the earliest surviving verbal statement of the theorem later named for Pythagoras. **Greek source:** Pythagoras, c. 570–495 BCE.

**Dating, stated honestly:** conventionally c. 800 BCE, but the range in the literature runs from the 8th to the 2nd century BCE, and there is no direct internal evidence fixing it. Any use in the article must carry that range, not the single favorable figure.

**What I inspected:** The MacTutor-hosted student project page, https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Projects/Pearce/chapter-5/ — which gives "The Sulba-sutras have been dated from around 800–200 BC" and the formulation "The rope stretched along the length of the diagonal of a rectangle makes an area which the vertical and horizontal sides make together." **This is a University of St Andrews *Projects* page by Ian Pearce, not a peer-reviewed article**, and should be cited as such or replaced. Kim Plofker, *Mathematics in India* (Princeton, 2009), is the appropriate authority; I did not obtain her pages this session. The Springer reference-work entry on the Śulbasūtras is paywalled (303 to an auth redirect).

**The finding that disciplines the argument:** the same page argues *against* transmission — "none of the methods of Greek geometry are discernible in Vedic geometry, but this merely serves to support arguments that it is independently developed." That cuts both ways and the article should say so. **Priority is not transmission.** A pre-Pythagorean Indian statement of the theorem establishes that the result was known in India first. It does not establish that Greece received it from India, and no evidence of transmission was located.

## C08. Aristoxenus — A Fourth-Century BCE Greek Witness to Indian–Greek Contact

**Claim tested:** pre-Alexander Indian–Greek philosophical contact. **Result: supports**, and this is a materially better source than Clement.

**Source:** Aristoxenus of Tarentum (pupil of Aristotle, late 4th c. BCE), fr. 53, preserved in Eusebius of Caesarea, *Praeparatio Evangelica* **Book XI, chapter III**, trans. E. H. Gifford (1903). https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/eusebius_pe_11_book11.htm

**Exact quotation (48 words):**

> a certain man of that nation fell in with Socrates at Athens, and presently asked him, what he was doing in philosophy: and when he said, that he was studying human life, the Indian laughed at him, and said that no one could comprehend things human, if he were ignorant of things divine.

Introduced by: "Now Aristoxenus the Musician says that this argument comes from the Indians."

**Why this outranks Clement (F08).** Clement of Alexandria writes c. 200 CE and is expressly arguing for non-Greek priority — an interested witness, six centuries after Pythagoras. Aristoxenus writes within a century of Socrates, is a pupil of Aristotle, and is reporting about Athens, not about a remote traveller's itinerary. The transmission is still indirect — the fragment survives only because Eusebius quotes it in the early 4th century CE, and Eusebius has his own apologetic purpose.

**What this establishes:** That a fourth-century BCE Greek writer believed an Indian was in Athens in Socrates' lifetime and that a specific philosophical argument "comes from the Indians." That is genuine testimony to contact and to a Greek belief in Indian priority for one argument.

**What it does not establish:** Anything about the period *before* the fifth century BCE. Socrates died in 399 BCE; the encounter, if it happened, is fifth-century. It therefore **does not answer the brief's narrow chronological question**, which concerns contributions before 500 BCE. It does establish that Indian–Greek philosophical contact is not a Hellenistic invention, which weakens the assumption that Alexander opened the channel. Use it for that, and say what it does not reach.

## C09. Risley — Anthropometry to Social Rank, Verified

**Claim tested:** an original example linking body measurement to social and racial classification. **Result: supports.** The brief's locator is confirmed.

**Source:** H. H. Risley, *The People of India* (1908), **printed p. 28** — confirmed by the running head "28 PEOPLE OF INDIA," immediately preceding the passage. Method description is on p. 27. Internet Archive item `peopleindia00rislgoog`. Local text: `dalrymple_sources/risley_people_of_india_1908.txt`, SHA-256 `6adf978ee4d857e9e3162a5c7401a332e8b0cdfcccd15fcafdd836f4d0566811`, retrieved 23 September 2026.

**Exact quotation (45 words):**

> Thus, for those parts of India where there is an appreciable strain of Dravidian blood it is scarcely a paradox to lay down, as a law of the caste organization, that the social status of the members of a particular group varies in inverse ratio to the mean relative width of their noses.

**Context, and the detail worth using:** immediately before, Risley benchmarks Indian noses against Europeans — the "Indo-Aryan group can fairly bear comparison with the noses of sixty-eight Parisians, measured by Topinard." The European measurement is the standard against which the Indian is scored. That is the same structural move as C01 at a different scale, and the pairing is available to the article if it wants it.

**Retrieval note:** the `_djvu.txt` download returns zero bytes for this item; the text is reachable through `https://archive.org/stream/<id>/<id>_djvu.txt` and extracting the `<pre>` block.

**Limits, restated from F07:** this does not establish a lineage from Risley to Dalrymple, and the brief's instruction not to invent one stands.

## C10. The Modern Inference — Where "DNA Does Not Speak" Fails as Written

**Claim tested:** that modern genetics reads language off DNA. **Result: contradicts.** This finding requires the author to revise the passage.

**Source:** Vagheesh M. Narasimhan et al., "The formation of human populations in South and Central Asia," *Science* 365 (2019), eaat7487. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6822619/ — local copy `dalrymple_sources/narasimhan_2019_science_pmc.html`, SHA-256 `be25134e84620a4608635c86d383b78e1a3c3978b511d98ac3599f152e689bda`.

**The three steps of the actual inference, quoted verbatim:**

1. **The genetic observation, hedged.** "The Steppe ancestry in South Asia has the same profile as that in Bronze Age Eastern Europe, tracking a movement of people that affected both regions and that **likely** spread the unique shared features shared between Indo-Iranian and Balto-Slavic languages."

2. **The linguistic evidence doing the real work.** "…it provides a plausible genetic explanation for the linguistic similarities between the Balto-Slavic and Indo-Iranian sub-families of Indo-European, which despite their vast geographic separation, **share the Satem innovation and Ruki sound laws**."

3. **A present-day social correlation offered as corroboration.** "Groups that view themselves as being of traditionally priestly status, including traditional custodians of liturgical texts in the early Indo-European language Sanskrit, tend (with exceptions) to have more Steppe ancestry than expected based on ANI-ASI mixture, **providing an independent line of evidence** for a Steppe origin for South Asia's Indo-European languages."

**And a difficulty the authors raise against themselves:** "it is striking that there are so few material culture similarities between the central Steppe and South Asia in the Middle to Late Bronze Age."

**What this establishes.** The paper does not claim DNA identifies language. The *linguistic* fact — the Satem and Ruki isoglosses shared by Balto-Slavic and Indo-Iranian — is the load-bearing evidence; the genetics supplies a **carrier** for a correspondence linguists had already identified. A sentence ending "DNA does not speak" aimed at this paper attacks a claim the paper explicitly does not make, and the quoted hedges are available to any respondent.

**The two defensible criticisms that remain, and they are better ones:**

- **Step 3 projects backwards from the present.** The corroboration is a correlation between *contemporary* self-identified priestly status and ancestry, used to support a claim about the second millennium BCE. Calling it "an independent line of evidence" is the strongest wording in the passage and the most exposed. Whether present-day caste standing is a reliable proxy for ancient linguistic custody is a real methodological question, and it is the question the manuscript's own transmission argument is best placed to press.
- **The authors concede the missing material culture.** That concession is in their text and can be quoted without dispute.

**Recommendation:** keep "DNA does not speak" only if the surrounding prose concedes that the geneticists agree, and then presses step 3. As a standalone refutation it will be answered from the paper's own abstract.
