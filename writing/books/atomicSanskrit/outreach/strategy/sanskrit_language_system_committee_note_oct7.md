# Sanskrit as a Language System
## A Proposal for Joint Research, Teaching, and Sanskrit Patronage

**For discussion at Deccan College on 7 October 2026**  
**Proposed by Parag Tope, author of *Atomic Sanskrit: The Architecture of Sanātan***  
**5 October 2026 · Draft for review before circulation**

## Purpose

I propose a joint programme between Deccan College and Central Sanskrit University to study Sanskrit as an engineered language system designed to remain unchanged, and to strengthen society's ability to maintain and use it. The programme would examine how universities can support the distributed architecture through which society has maintained Sanskrit.

**Guardianship should remain with society. Universities should support that guardianship, not replace it.** Teachers, learners, recitation lineages, and knowledgeable listeners carry the language and participate in its correction. Universities can contribute research, resources, training, and opportunities for collaboration without making themselves the sole authorities over the shared reference.

Sanskrit allows speakers to create new words and compositions through established rules. Alongside this freedom to create, Vedic teaching maintains disciplines for transmitting particular sounds, words, and sequences with great precision. How do these two activities support each other? How can a language meet new needs while maintaining the architecture through which it creates new expression?

*Atomic Sanskrit* argues that Sanskrit was engineered as a complete language system **designed to remain invariant while enabling new expression**. Invariance means that its underlying sound distinctions and rules of formation remain unchanged, even as speakers create new words and compositions. Its two complementary domains separate what must remain unchanged from what speakers can continually create. Distributed teaching, recitation, and attentive listening sustain that design across places and generations.

The book also explains why invariance was required: knowledge carried across generations needs a dependable language through which people can understand what they inherit, examine it, and pass it on. Keeping that language's foundations unchanged protects the continuity of civilizational memory while allowing each generation to address new circumstances.

This programme would examine that engineering through Sanskrit sources, traditional teaching practices, comparative language study, and computational analysis. It would give researchers concrete examples to investigate and help teachers, learners, and listeners use the findings within and beyond university campuses.

The practical question is: **how can universities help more people learn, use, check, and transmit Sanskrit while keeping that ability distributed across society?**

The first step could be a twelve-month pilot within existing institutional structures, supported by research fellowships, participation by traditional teachers, and a workshop. I can contribute the research materials developed for the book and help approach prospective donors, including families and trusts with a history of supporting Sanskrit learning.

## What Is a Language System?

A language needs more than rules for forming words and sentences. It also needs ways to teach those rules, recognize departures, correct errors, and pass that knowledge to future generations. Writing a grammar addresses one part of this task. Maintaining the knowledge needed to understand and use it addresses another.

For Sanskrit, the book identifies **invariance** as a defining feature of this system: its underlying sound distinctions and rules of formation remain unchanged for thousands of years. Speakers can still create new words and compositions. The design preserves the foundations through which new expression becomes possible, rather than limiting speakers to what has already been said.

The proposed programme would examine how Sanskrit's two domains work together to maintain this invariance:

| Domain | Responsibility examined in this programme | What researchers would study |
|---|---|---|
| वैदिक (*vaidika*) | Maintain a fixed reference through prescribed compositions and disciplined transmission. | The forms of particular passages, their sounds and grammatical relationships, and the methods used to teach and check them. |
| लौकिक (*laukika*) | Enable new expression through the shared language architecture. | How speakers form words and sentences, extend meanings, and describe circumstances that earlier compositions did not anticipate. |

The वैदिक domain maintains the reference; the लौकिक domain enables new expression through the shared architecture. In the book's account, these complementary responsibilities were designed to keep Sanskrit invariant, rather than leaving its continuity to chance.

Contrary to Western philology's account of an evolving Vedic language followed by a fixed Classical Sanskrit, the book argues that Sanskrit as a language system has always been invariant. It therefore treats grammatical differences between the वैदिक and लौकिक domains as features of one engineered architecture, not as stages in the imaginary evolutionary sequence constructed by Western philologists. The research would examine why a form or operation is permitted in one domain but restricted in the other, what that difference contributes to the system, and how teaching, listening, and distributed correction sustain it.

The Vedas serve as a shared reference for checking Sanskrit's sounds, word formations, and grammatical relationships. I call this reference a **calibrant**: something people can use to recognize departures and explain corrections. The book argues that the Vedas' continuing role as this calibrant has helped Sanskrit remain invariant for thousands of years, while the लौकिक domain allows new words and compositions to meet changing needs.

For example, a teacher may explain a recitation correction through सन्धि (*sandhi*), how sounds combine when words meet; व्याकरण (*vyākaraṇa*), which explains the grammatical form; and छन्दस् (*chandas*), the verse's metrical pattern. The learner can then understand the correction and learn to perform the check. The research would identify exactly what each check establishes and how the different checks reinforce one another.

**The listener also helps maintain accuracy.** This responsibility extends beyond formal teaching and recitation lineages. People who learn to recognize the sounds and patterns through repeated listening can notice a departure in a household, a gathering, or a public recitation. The audience is therefore more than a recipient: knowledgeable listeners can question a pronunciation and prompt a correction. The proposed programme would examine how this wider ability to listen and check contributes to the accuracy of Sanskrit's sound system.

### Resisting Unintended Change

Every natural language changes over time. Pronunciation shifts, distinctions are lost or replaced, and grammatical usage changes. The book uses **entropy** to describe the accumulation of such changes; departures from established Sanskrit forms are discussed here as अपभ्रंश (*apabhraṃśa*).

The book argues that **Sanskrit was engineered as a language system designed to withstand entropy**. Its two complementary domains, shared Vedic reference, and distributed practices of teaching, recitation, and attentive listening keep its foundations unchanged while allowing new expression. The research would examine how these parts work together to resist change over thousands of years.

The proposed relationship between the two domains is therefore important. New expression need not mean changing the reference. Maintaining a reference need not mean prohibiting new expression. The programme would investigate how these responsibilities operate together, including where their rules and permissions differ.

### Resisting Capture and Destruction

Preservation faces deliberate threats as well as accidental change. A library can be destroyed, a teaching institution can lose its patronage, or a central authority can try to control access to knowledge.

Distributed preservation means that the reference and the knowledge needed to maintain it are carried by teachers, learners, recitation lineages, and knowledgeable listeners in different places. If one centre is lost, the whole system need not disappear with it. If one institution claims exclusive authority, other participants may still possess the knowledge needed to check its claim.

This does not make every lineage immune to loss. It provides a concrete research question: **how does knowledge distributed across people and regions help the larger system survive the failure or destruction of individual centres?** The programme would study the relationships and practices through which that resilience is maintained.

## Four Research and Teaching Projects

The four projects connect research with society's ability to maintain Sanskrit. They examine the complementary design of the two domains, Sanskrit's subcontinental foundations, the rules through which an app can build and explain words, and society's participation through attentive listening. Together, they investigate how the language remains unchanged while people continue to create new expressions and pass on the knowledge needed to check them.

### 1. How Do the Two Domains Keep Sanskrit Unchanged?

**Research question:** How do the वैदिक and लौकिक domains complement each other, maintaining a fixed reference while allowing new expression through the same language architecture?

The project would examine the book's proposition that the two domains were engineered together to resist entropy. The Vedas maintain the calibrant; लौकिक expression allows new words and compositions without changing the language's foundations. Researchers would investigate the grammatical permissions of each domain and ask why a form is available in one but restricted in the other. The differences would be studied in relation to the purposes they serve within the shared architecture.

Selected Vedic passages would be compared with लौकिक formations to identify shared sound distinctions and formation rules, alongside permissions specific to each domain. The comparison of रुद्रैः and रुद्रेभिः in the explanatory examples offers one starting point: researchers can examine how grammatical alternatives meet metrical requirements. Teaching, recitation, and listening would then show how people maintain the reference and distinguish an error from a permitted difference.

A proposed pilot would examine approximately thirty passages with two or three participating teaching traditions. Traditional teachers would help select the material, explain the practices, and review the descriptions. Researchers would document pronunciation as taught through शिक्षा (*śikṣā*), together with सन्धि, व्याकरण, and छन्दस्. They would also examine स्वर (*svara*), the prescribed Vedic pitch or accent where relevant, and the पाठ (*pāṭha*) methods through which words are recited in specified sequences. This would show how the different disciplines help maintain each passage.

The project would follow an actual correction from detection to explanation. What alerted the teacher? Which feature confirmed the correction? Could another trained participant perform the same check? It would also document how participants learn these skills and maintain contact with other teachers or lineages.

The study would also include listeners outside the participating lineages. What can a person recognize through regular exposure to recitation? When does a listener notice that something sounds wrong, and when can that person explain why? Following how such observations reach a reciter or teacher would show how the wider audience participates in maintaining accuracy.

**Results:** worked comparisons explaining the shared architecture and the purposes of different grammatical permissions; recordings where participants agree; a report on how distributed checking maintains that architecture; and teaching demonstrations for use by participating teachers, local learning groups, and other institutions. Practitioners would help interpret the findings and review the account of their methods. Agreed recordings and materials would be returned to participants in forms they can use, rather than remain available only through a university archive. Present-day demonstrations would establish how the mechanisms operate, while claims about their history would be investigated through separate source records.

**What a donor could fund:** a research fellowship, traditional-teacher participation, field visits, recording and transcription, and specialist review. Teachers should be supported for preparation and sustained collaboration, not only for a public lecture.

### 2. Sanskrit and the Subcontinent's Languages

**Research question:** What can the sounds and grammatical features Sanskrit shares with Tamil, Toda, Kurukh, Korku, Mundari, and Ho tell us about its subcontinental foundations? How can those relationships help people learn Sanskrit while maintaining its sound distinctions and rules?

The book's language surveys provide starting material for this study. Sanskrit specialists, researchers in the participating languages, and speech scientists would work together to review the comparisons, collect recordings, and examine selected grammatical features. The purpose is both to investigate Sanskrit's relationship with the subcontinent's languages and to develop teaching that builds on what learners already know.

The first task would be to agree a common comparison method. Researchers would compare स्थान (*sthāna*), where a sound is produced, and प्रयत्न (*prayatna*), how it is produced, rather than simply compare symbols in different scripts. They would distinguish a sound that separates words in a language from a pronunciation that occurs only in a particular setting. Recordings and source descriptions would identify the variety being examined.

The grammatical comparisons could begin with reduplication: repeating a word or part of it to contribute to meaning. Researchers would compare actual examples, explain how repetition functions in each language, and identify similarities and differences. These comparisons would become teaching examples through which students can relate Sanskrit formations to familiar patterns in their own languages.

The pronunciation study would similarly identify what learners already recognize and what requires particular attention. Participating Sanskrit teachers and learners would demonstrate how they recognize and correct the influence of regional pronunciation while keeping permitted recitation differences distinct from errors. This connects the survey with the practical task of maintaining a shared reference across geography.

A first phase could review the existing comparisons and plan a manageable recording sample. Later phases could extend the coverage across northern, southern, eastern, and western India and include specified external comparisons. Historical explanations would then be assessed alongside the sound evidence; present-day similarity alone does not establish a date or direction of transmission.

**Results:** a reviewed comparison method, a source-linked sound inventory, selected recordings, worked grammatical comparisons, and lessons that use familiar features of a learner's language to explain Sanskrit. Diagrams and listening exercises would make pronunciation differences clear. Local language collaborators would review descriptions of their speech, receive the agreed materials, and help make the explanations useful to teachers in their communities.

**What a donor could fund:** a comparative-language fellowship or a defined student project, local language collaborators, speech-science participation, field recording, sound and grammatical analysis, and preparation of teaching materials.

### 3. A Sanskrit Word and Meaning Builder

**Research question:** Can an app help users build Sanskrit words through permitted rules, analyse existing words, and understand their possible meanings in Sanskrit?

The app would let users start with धातवः (*dhātavaḥ*), the language's meaning-bearing atoms, and build word-molecules through permitted combinations. Users could also enter an existing word to examine how it is formed. In both cases, the app would explain possible meanings in Sanskrit and show the elements and rules behind each formation. This makes the capacity for new expression something users can explore for themselves.

Patañjali recounts that बृहस्पति (*Bṛhaspati*) taught इन्द्र (*Indra*) words one by one for a thousand divine years without reaching the end. The story shows why we should learn how words are formed, rather than try to learn every word one by one. The app would put that principle into practice by letting users follow and test the steps through which words are created.

The first version would offer two ways to begin: choose a धातु (*dhātu*) and build a word, or enter a word and examine its possible formations. Within the reviewed pilot sample, it would:

1. Offer permitted next steps when building a word, or identify possible formation paths for an entered word.
2. Check each step against the relevant rules and their वैदिक or लौकिक permissions.
3. Show the धातु and any उपसर्ग (*upasarga*), prefix; प्रत्यय (*pratyaya*), suffix; or समास (*samāsa*), compound formation involved.
4. Explain the possible meanings in Sanskrit, using the contributions of those elements and the rules connecting them.
5. Show recorded examples of use where available, so users can compare the explanation with actual usage.

For example, कर्ता (*kartā*) could be explained as यः करोति सः कर्ता, “the one who does,” alongside its grammatical derivation. Sanskrit explanations would be the foundation of the tool, rather than English translations. Where several analyses or meanings are possible, the builder would show the alternatives and explain what further context is needed.

The book's computational analysis already follows operations that generate millions of possible word-meanings. A proposed pilot would draw on those records and select approximately one hundred word families for specialist review. It would include familiar words, less familiar formations, and unresolved cases. For each family, researchers would identify the rules that remain unchanged as new forms are created.

The project would keep three questions distinct: Is the formation permitted? Is it recorded in use? What meaning can be established or reasonably proposed? Absence from a dictionary would not by itself invalidate a formation, and a generated form would not by itself establish a settled meaning. A case the system cannot resolve would remain open for review rather than receive an automatic rejection.

The existing Encyclopaedic Dictionary of Sanskrit could contribute quotations and recorded meanings against which the builder's explanations can be tested. The builder would add derivations, possible new formations, and explanations that users can examine. A reviewed pilot could demonstrate how these methods might enrich the existing dictionary, with access and reuse agreed through the participating institutions.

**Results:** a working app that builds and analyses words within the reviewed sample, with rule references, Sanskrit explanations, and a record of corrections and unresolved cases. Teachers and users outside the universities would be invited to examine the reasoning and contribute corrections. A later version could accept a meaning and suggest words that express it, showing the formation path for each candidate.

**What a donor could fund:** a Sanskrit research fellow and a software developer working together, supported by specialist grammatical review and testing with teachers and learners. The developer would build the app's word-building, rule-checking, word-analysis, and Sanskrit explanation functions, drawing on the existing computational research. The first deliverable would be a working app for the reviewed word families.

*Source for the Bṛhaspati-Indra account:* Patañjali, महाभाष्य (*Mahābhāṣya*), पस्पशाह्निक (*Paspaśāhnika*), Kielhorn edition, vol. I, p. 5, line 24 onward. [Sanskrit passage](https://vishvasa.github.io/sanskrit/vyAkaraNam/pANinIyam/mUlAni/mahA-bhAShyam/sarva-prastutiH/01_paspashAhnikam/03_nirmANa-rItiH/).

### 4. श्रुति Needs श्रोता

**Purpose:** Strengthen society's participation in maintaining Sanskrit's invariance through a listening-first course. Students would learn to hear distinctions, identify errors, and understand corrections without first having to master chanting themselves.

There is renewed interest in Sanskrit chanting, particularly भगवद्गीता (*Bhagavad Gītā*) chanting. Attentive listening serves an equally important purpose. As *Atomic Sanskrit* explains, Sanskrit's invariance depends on society's active participation: people hear its sounds, discern differences, identify errors, and help correct them. The course would develop this ability as a contribution in its own right, with chanting optional rather than a condition of participation.

श्रुति (*śruti*), that which is heard, needs श्रोता (*śrotā*), the listener. A person can learn to notice an omitted sound or a shortened vowel without being able to recite the whole passage accurately. Knowledgeable listeners in households, gatherings, and public recitations can therefore help maintain the shared reference alongside teachers and reciters. Universities can strengthen that ability across society.

**Activities:** Traditional teachers and speech-science educators would prepare listening exercises covering sound distinctions, vowel duration, सन्धि, and familiar verse patterns. Initial exercises could use short Gītā passages that participants already recognize. Students would hear a teacher-approved reference, compare it with a clearly identified practice example containing one change, and point out what differs. Examples could involve a missing sound, a shortened vowel, or a disrupted सन्धि. The teacher would then explain why the change matters and let students hear the corrected form.

As students progress, they would listen without a side-by-side prompt and try to locate and describe an error. Exercises would also include correct recitations and permitted variations, so students learn to discern rather than assume every unfamiliar rendering is wrong. They would practise asking informed questions and recognize when further guidance is needed. Any later Vedic exercises would follow the requirements of the particular recitation tradition; Gītā examples would not be treated as demonstrations of Vedic स्वर.

The short course would first be tested with a local learning group, then revised using participants' and teachers' feedback. No prior command of technical grammar or ability to chant would be required. Comparable listening exercises before and after the course would show whether participants can identify and locate errors, explain what they heard, and avoid mistaking permitted variation for error. Assessment would focus on listening and discernment, not on a chanting performance.

**Connection to Project 1:** Project 1 investigates how the two-domain architecture maintains invariance and how people check it in practice. Project 4 uses its reviewed examples of listening and correction to help more people participate. The projects would share practitioner expertise while producing distinct results: a research account of the architecture and a tested listening curriculum.

**Results:** a short course, reviewed audio examples, a teacher's guide, and listening exercises with an account of what participants learned. Local teachers and participants would receive the agreed materials in forms they can continue using beyond the pilot. This gives universities a direct way to strengthen society's guardianship without becoming the sole source of correction.

**What a donor could fund:** curriculum development, traditional-teacher and speech-science educator participation, audio recording, a programme coordinator, and community sessions. The first deliverable would be a tested listening course and the materials needed for another local group to use it.

## Teaching Through the Research

The programme should produce teaching material as it produces research. An initial workshop could bring Sanskrit teachers, recitation practitioners, linguists, students, and engineers together around the same worked examples.

An introductory session would require no knowledge of the book's vocabulary. Participants would hear a sound distinction, follow a word formation, and see how a correction can be explained. More advanced sessions would examine the Sanskrit sources and rules underlying those demonstrations.

Teaching should extend beyond the campuses. Local teachers and learning groups could host demonstrations and use the materials in their own settings. The universities could support preparation, travel, and teacher-to-teacher exchanges. A learner's ability to explain a correction should rest on the sounds, rules, and reference being examined, not on holding a university certificate.

Once the examples and teaching approach have been tested, the institutions could develop a short course of approximately forty to sixty hours. Its subjects could include:

- How the वर्णमाला (*varṇamālā*), Sanskrit's organized sound inventory, relates sounds to their स्थान and प्रयत्न.
- How धातवः, उपसर्ग, प्रत्यय, and समास contribute to words and their meanings.
- How sounds and grammatical patterns familiar from other Indian languages can help students learn Sanskrit, and where the differences require attention.
- How the वैदिक and लौकिक domains share an architecture while using different permissions.
- How teachers use शिक्षा, सन्धि, व्याकरण, छन्दस्, and पाठ methods to explain corrections.
- How knowledge maintained across different people and places contributes to preservation.

Marathi, Hindi, and English explanations could be used alongside Sanskrit according to the audience and available teachers. Donors could support course preparation, visiting teachers, student assistance, and reusable materials. Any certificate or credit-bearing course would follow the institutions' academic approval procedures.

## A Practical First Year

The programme need not begin with a new department or permanent chair. I suggest starting with **Projects 1 and 3 as the research core**, with a small **Project 4 listening-course pilot** developed alongside Project 1. This connects preservation, new expression, and society's participation in maintaining accuracy. Project 2 could begin with a faculty-led review and a recording plan, then expand when suitable collaborators and funding are available.

The proposed first-year package is two research fellowships, software-development support for Project 3, traditional-teacher visits, a joint workshop, and curriculum development and coordination for the Project 4 community pilot. Shared sessions and examples can support both research and teaching, with their costs accounted for once. If the available supervision or funding supports only one project initially, the programme can begin at that scale.

| Stage | Proposed activity |
|---|---|
| Before launch | Nominate academic leads, select examples, agree the institutional host and approval route, prepare budgets, and confirm funding and access. |
| Months 1–3 after launch | Select researchers through institutional procedures; establish the source records and research method; review an initial sample. |
| Months 4–8 | Develop the examples, recordings, and analyses; hold working sessions with traditional teachers and specialist reviewers; prepare the listening exercises. |
| Months 9–12 | Complete the reviewed pilot collections and word-and-meaning app, hold the workshop, test and revise the listening course with a local group, prepare the broader course outline, and recommend the next stage. |

The institutions would carry responsibility for the programme's funding, appointments, academic supervision, and assessment. Participating teachers and practitioners would help shape the research questions, interpret examples, and review teaching materials. The book's propositions would be examined through evidence, including findings that require correction or a different explanation.

### How Would We Judge Success?

Publications and university enrolments would be only part of the assessment. The pilot should also report:

- Whether participating learners and listeners can explain and perform selected checks for themselves.
- Whether the app lets users build and analyse words in the reviewed sample, understand the rules and possible meanings, and identify cases that need further review.
- Whether listening-course participants improve in recognizing sound distinctions, explaining what they heard, and seeking guidance when needed, using comparable exercises before and after the course.
- Whether local teachers and groups use the materials to teach others outside the campuses.
- Whether participants receive usable copies of the agreed materials and can continue using them without depending on a single institutional repository.
- Whether teachers and listeners across different places establish continuing ways to exchange examples, discuss departures, and contribute corrections.

These measures ask whether the programme strengthens society's ability to maintain Sanskrit, rather than simply increasing the university's collection of records.

## Funding and Institutional Arrangements

Fellowships, student research grants, visiting-teacher programmes, workshop support, and course-development grants give donors specific activities to support. A family or trust could support one project without underwriting the whole programme. A sustained programme could later justify a funded chair with defined research and teaching responsibilities.

Deccan College's published rules provide routes for fellowships, courses, visiting scholars, and expenditure from external contributions. Its published scholarship list and donor-supported Jain Archaeology chair report provide precedents for combining patronage with academic activity.[1][2][3]

Projects using the Centre of Indian Knowledge System and Encyclopaedic Dictionary of Sanskrit, known as the **IKS-EDS Centre**, would need to follow its CSU governance arrangements. Under CSU Ordinance 48, the Centre's Governing Committee approves programmes and budgets, while additional funding sources require CSU Executive Council approval.[4]

Before donor commitments are sought, the institutions should identify the grant recipient, academic host, appointment route, eligible costs, and reporting arrangements. New posts and some payments require additional approvals; donor funding should not be assumed to remove those requirements. Access, recording consent, and publication rights should also be agreed at the outset. Those agreements should enable participants to retain and use their knowledge and agreed materials; participation should not require transferring exclusive custody of their teaching resources to the university.

I can help prepare donor-facing descriptions of the selected projects and approach prospective patrons. Budgets and any naming or recognition arrangements would be developed with the institutions. No donor commitment is being represented in this proposal.

## Explanatory Examples

These examples illustrate the proposed research. They do not require readers to know the book's terminology.

### A. A Difference in Form Can Serve a Precise Purpose

Two adjacent passages in ऋग्वेद (*Ṛgveda*) 3.32 use रुद्रैः (*rudraiḥ*) and रुद्रेभिः (*rudrebhiḥ*). Both mean “with the Rudras” and express तृतीया बहुवचनम् (*tṛtīyā bahuvacanam*), the instrumental plural. The first has two अक्षराणि (*akṣarāṇi*), syllables; the second has three.

Each complete पाद (*pāda*), or verse line, has the eleven अक्षराणि of त्रिष्टुभ् (*triṣṭubh*). Replacing the three-syllable form with the two-syllable form in the second line would leave only ten. A researcher can therefore examine the grammatical alternatives and छन्दस् together, rather than treat every difference as an unexplained change in the language.

This is a useful pilot exercise: identify the allowed forms, show their precise use, and explain which checks would reveal an alteration. It also raises a question about the two domains: what is lost if a narrower rule for new composition is imposed on a Vedic passage?

*Source:* Ṛgveda 3.32.2d and 3.32.3d; worked analysis in *Atomic Sanskrit*, Chapter 16, “Pitch, Meter, and Recitation.” The proposed project would have specialists review the source readings and analysis.

### B. New Circumstances Do Not Always Require New Rules

चन्द्र (*candra*), Moon, and यान (*yāna*), vehicle, combine through समास in चन्द्रयान (*Candrayāna*). Existing words and an established method of combination can name a modern undertaking.

The research would extend this simple demonstration to more demanding cases. For each proposed word, it would show the starting material, the rules used, and the basis for the meaning. This would help students understand both what the language permits and where interpretation still needs judgment.

*Source:* *Atomic Sanskrit*, Chapter 12, discussion of word formation and compounding.

### C. A Sound Comparison Must Compare Sounds

त (*ta*) is दन्त्य (*dantya*), made at the teeth; ट (*ṭa*) is मूर्धन्य (*mūrdhanya*), made with the tongue curled back. They differ in स्थान, the place of articulation. Writing both approximately as “t” can conceal that difference. Conversely, two different scripts may represent comparable sounds with very different symbols.

A sound survey therefore compares स्थान and प्रयत्न, asks whether a distinction changes words in that language, and identifies the speech variety being described. Shared recording tasks and clear comparison rules make the results easier to examine and repeat.

*Source:* *Atomic Sanskrit*, Chapters 8–9 and Appendix Part 4. These provide the initial comparison framework, to be reviewed by the proposed research team.
