# Sanskrit as a Language System
## A Note on Joint Research, Teaching, and Patronage

**For discussion at Deccan College on 7 October 2026**  
Parag Tope, author of *Atomic Sanskrit: The Architecture of Sanātan*  
5 October 2026 · Draft for review before circulation

## The Proposal

I propose a joint programme between Deccan College and Central Sanskrit University to study Sanskrit as a complete language system and help society maintain and use it. The programme would connect research with teaching, practical tools, and donor-funded projects.

*Atomic Sanskrit* argues that Sanskrit was engineered as a language system designed to remain unchanged, or invariant, while allowing new expression. **Invariance** means that its underlying sound distinctions and rules of word formation remain unchanged for thousands of years, while speakers can create new words and compositions. This protects the continuity of knowledge without restricting the language to the needs of the past.

A language system includes more than grammar. It includes the reference against which people check usage, the methods through which they learn and correct it, and the people who keep those methods alive. The book explains Sanskrit through two complementary domains:

| Domain | Role within the language system |
|---|---|
| वैदिक (*vaidika*) | Maintains the fixed reference through the Vedas and disciplined transmission. |
| लौकिक (*laukika*) | Allows new words and compositions through the shared language architecture. |

The Vedas serve as what I describe as a **calibrant**: a shared reference that people can use to recognize departures and explain corrections. The book argues that this relationship has kept Sanskrit invariant. It challenges Western philology's account of successive stages of an evolving language, treating differences between the two domains as features of one engineered system.

The book uses **entropy** for the gradual accumulation of changes in pronunciation and grammatical usage. The proposed research would examine how Sanskrit resists such change, and how preservation across many people and places helps it survive the loss or destruction of individual centres.

**Guardianship should remain with society; universities should support it.** Teachers, recitation lineages, learners, and knowledgeable listeners all participate in maintaining accuracy. Universities can strengthen their knowledge and connections without becoming the sole authority over Sanskrit.

## Four Possible Projects

### 1. How Do the Two Domains Keep Sanskrit Unchanged?

Study how the वैदिक (*vaidika*) and लौकिक (*laukika*) domains complement each other: the Vedas maintain the calibrant, while लौकिक expression allows new words and compositions without changing the language's foundations. The project would examine the book's proposition that these domains were engineered together to resist entropy. Why do they permit different grammatical forms, and how do those differences serve their respective purposes? Researchers would compare selected Vedic passages with लौकिक formations, then document how teachers, reciters, and listeners use sound, सन्धि (*sandhi*), grammar, and metre to recognize and correct departures. The results would include worked examples explaining the shared architecture and teaching materials that participants can continue using.

**Donor support:** a research fellowship, traditional-teacher participation, field visits, and recording.

### 2. Sanskrit and the Subcontinent's Languages

Investigate Sanskrit's subcontinental foundations by identifying the sounds and grammatical features it shares with Tamil, Toda, Kurukh, Korku, Mundari, and Ho. Building on the book's language surveys, Sanskrit specialists, local-language researchers, and speech scientists would compare how sounds are produced and how features such as reduplication, repeating a word or part of it, contribute to meaning. The findings would also support teaching: familiar features of a learner's own language can help explain Sanskrit, while differences show where pronunciation needs particular attention. The study could expand to include more languages across the subcontinent.

**Donor support:** a comparative-language fellowship or student project, local collaborators, speech analysis, and teaching materials.

### 3. A Sanskrit Word and Meaning Builder

Develop an app that lets users build Sanskrit words from धातवः (*dhātavaḥ*), the language's meaning-bearing atoms, combining them into word-molecules through permitted rules. Users could also enter an existing word to examine how it is formed. In both cases, the app would explain possible meanings in Sanskrit and show the elements and rules behind each formation. A later version could accept a meaning and suggest words that express it.

Patañjali recounts that बृहस्पति (*Bṛhaspati*) taught इन्द्र (*Indra*) words one by one for a thousand divine years without reaching the end. The story shows why we should learn how words are formed rather than try to learn every word. A Sanskrit researcher and software developer could begin with one hundred word families, drawing on the book's computational research and, through agreed collaboration, the existing Encyclopaedic Dictionary. Unresolved cases would remain open for review.

**Donor support:** a Sanskrit fellowship, a software developer, grammatical review, and user testing.

### 4. श्रुति Needs श्रोता

श्रुति (*śruti*), that which is heard, needs श्रोता (*śrotā*), the listener. There is renewed interest in Sanskrit chanting, particularly गीता (*Gītā*) chanting. Attentive listening serves an equally important purpose. As *Atomic Sanskrit* explains, Sanskrit's invariance depends on society's active participation: people hear its sounds, discern differences, identify errors, and help correct them. A listening-first course would strengthen this ability without requiring students to master chanting. They would learn to recognize missing sounds, altered vowel lengths, and disrupted सन्धि, while distinguishing errors from permitted variations.

**Donor support:** course preparation, teacher participation, audio examples, and a local pilot.

## A Practical First Year

Begin with a twelve-month pilot rather than a new department or permanent chair. Projects 1 and 3 could form the research core, with a small listening course developed alongside Project 1. Project 2 could start with a faculty-led review and expand as collaborators and funding become available.

An initial package could include two research fellowships, software-development support, traditional-teacher visits, a joint workshop, and preparation of the listening course. A donor could support one defined activity without funding the whole programme. Student grants, visiting-teacher programmes, and course development offer further opportunities; a sustained programme could later justify a funded chair.

The institutions would settle supervision, approvals, budgets, and access arrangements before funding commitments are sought. Traditional teachers would help select examples and review findings. Participants should receive usable materials, and local groups should be able to continue teaching without depending on a single university repository.

Success would mean reviewed research and practical results: a working word-and-meaning app, a tested listening course, and teachers and learners able to explain and perform checks themselves. I can contribute the book's research materials and help approach prospective donors, including families and trusts with a history of supporting Sanskrit learning.
