# Must Order Always Come from Above?

*By Parag Tope*

## What would Indian self-rule actually mean?

After writing *Operation Red Lotus*, I wanted to understand what our ancestors had fought to protect. Would स्वराज्य (*svarājya*), self-rule, mean Indians occupying the positions previously held by British or Islamic rulers? Or could it mean a fundamentally different relationship between government and society?

That inquiry led to *Atomic Sanskrit: The Architecture of Sanātan*. The book argues that Sanātan creates order from the inside out, through knowledge, restraint, and shared responsibility. Sanskrit is the language system through which we can still examine how that order functions.

## How does that differ from the order we know?

Governments make rules, institutions enforce them, and people live beneath their authority. Citizens may elect their government and limit its powers, but the state remains above society. I call this arrangement the pyramid. Fear and punishment are its currency of order: disobedience carries a penalty.

Most people want to live peacefully and care for those around them. Some will deceive, exploit, or attack others. Must their conduct determine the arrangement under which everyone else lives?

**The pyramid treats everyone as an exception waiting to happen.**

Sanātan begins with restraint rather than imposed constraint. People learn to judge their conduct, accept correction, and take responsibility for others. Government protects those relationships and acts against harmful conduct without becoming the source of every obligation.

## What could a language demonstrate about this?

As a child reciting the *Bhagavad Gītā*, I misplaced a sound. My mother caught the mistake because it changed the number of syllables in the line. Restoring the sound restored the prescribed pattern. She could show me why the correction was necessary.

Her judgment did not depend on her accent, personal preference, or authority as my mother. Another trained listener elsewhere could identify the same departure. I could learn to make that check myself and eventually teach someone else.

The verse provided a shared reference; people maintained the knowledge needed to use it. This is what I mean by a **calibrant**: a reference against which people can recognize an error and explain its correction.

The person correcting me did not own the standard. I could use that same standard to recognize a mistake she made.

## Can such a system endure without someone controlling it?

Vedic transmission combines several checks. Students learn pronunciation, vowel duration, pitch, and sequence. They recite passages continuously, separate them into words, and repeat those words in prescribed arrangements. Each procedure allows the listener to examine something another procedure may conceal.[^recitation]

For three words, A, B, and C, paired recitation proceeds A-B, then B-C. The repeated B connects the pairs. A missing word or an altered junction can be checked against the separated and continuous versions.

The Vedas have carried this reference across thousands of years. Their custodians changed, kingdoms fell, and the languages spoken around them continued changing. The reference remained available because its protection depended on many people learning, checking, and teaching it, rather than one office continuing to command them.

Teachers and lineages across India's north, south, east, and west maintain these disciplines. A teacher can err; another can identify the error. Capturing one institution cannot give an attacker the power to rewrite every independent transmission.

The greater engineering achievement is therefore the system that protects the language. It must withstand both unintended change and deliberate attack upon its teachers, knowledge, and memory. Writing a grammar cannot accomplish that by itself.

## How can an unchanged language describe a changing world?

Sanskrit divides these responsibilities between two domains. The वैदिक (*vaidika*) domain keeps the Vedic reference exact. The लौकिक (*laukika*) domain permits new composition through the same language. Speakers can describe new circumstances without rewriting the reference against which they check their Sanskrit.

Consider चन्द्रयान (*Candrayāna*): Moon and vehicle combine to name a spacecraft through procedures that existed long before space travel. The book follows those procedures from sounds through meaning-bearing atoms to words and sentences.

Its argument concerns an engineered language system capable of remaining unchanged while people continue creating through it.

## Why is this architecture rarely discussed?

Western philology teaches a different account: Sanskrit changed naturally until Pāṇini imposed order upon it. The Vedas become early religious literature, and their role in keeping Sanskrit calibrated disappears. Indian academic institutions continue teaching that account.[^curricula]

The pyramid credits a celebrated authority with creating the order that people maintained through distributed knowledge. I call this **heroic erasure**. Praise for Pāṇini conceals the architecture he analyzed.

Pāṇini decoded and documented an existing language. His grammar distinguishes the circumstances in which particular forms apply; the book examines those distinctions as complementary domains rather than successive historical stages.[^domains] The difference matters beyond grammar: one account makes lasting order depend on an authority; the other asks how people maintain it together.

## What would this mean for society today?

Hindus invoke शान्तिः (*śāntiḥ*), quietude, three times. The three invocations concern disturbances within oneself, relationships with other living beings, and natural or cosmic forces beyond direct human control.

*Second Shanti*, the larger inquiry of which this book is the first volume, concerns the second domain. Much of its institutional expression was destroyed under centuries of Abrahamic pyramidal rule. Yoga and meditation remain familiar; the knowledge behind society's self-government needs renewed examination.

Sanskrit demonstrates how people learn a shared standard, exercise judgment, and keep correction available without surrendering ownership to an apex. Could those principles recur in economic life and government? This recurrence across scales is the book's fractal proposition.

Later volumes will develop that polity from first principles. The aim is to help people build a better future, not simply admire an inheritance. Freedom should allow us to ask what kind of order we want, rather than merely who should command it.
