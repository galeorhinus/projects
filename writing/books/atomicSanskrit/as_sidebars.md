**Voice-calibration alert (author direction, May 9, 2026):** *Shruti* texts (the Vedas) and *smriti* texts (Mahābhārata, Bhagavad Gītā, Purāṇas, etc.) are NEVER to be presented as "two registers" or "different registers." They are two different styles engineered for two fundamentally different design purposes: the Vedas have tone and style designed for self-encryption as *shruti* (oral preservation through redundancy and structural constraint, every phoneme load-bearing); *smriti* texts are engineered for narrative-philosophical-didactic transmission with different design constraints. Use *styles*, *design purposes*, *engineering targets* — never *registers* — when contrasting the two. The phrase "two registers" is to be avoided categorically when comparing Indic texts or text-classes; use it only for non-Indic-textual contexts where it has a settled meaning (e.g., describing the linguistic register a Wave 2 outer recalibrant established in a target language like Chinese Buddhism, or describing the engineering register of the book's own analytical voice).

## Sidebar 1 — The mūrdhanya / ārya / mleccha axis as phonetic refutation of AIT

*Captured: Session 3 (continued), May 9, 2026.*

**Author's framing (verbatim):**

> The so-called "आर्य (ārya) invasion or migration" story becomes almost comic when tested against the sound-system of संस्कृतम् (saṃskṛtam). If आर्य (ārya) denotes refinement, discipline, and precision, then the real marker of आर्यत्व (āryatva) was not race, lineage, or skull shape, but speech: the ability to enter the exact phonetic architecture of संस्कृतम् (saṃskṛtam), including its मूर्धन्य (mūrdhanya) sounds — ट, ठ, ड, ढ, ण, ष (ṭa, ṭha, ḍa, ḍha, ṇa, ṣa). These are not casual sounds; they require a precise placement of the tongue in the middle of the mouth, a placement deeply native to the Indian sound-field. If Sanskrit-speaking आर्याः (āryāḥ) had encountered Central Asian or European peoples unable to produce this disciplined phonetic grid, they would not have recognized them as linguistic superiors arriving with Sanskrit. They would have called them म्लेच्छाः (mlecchāḥ) matter-of-factly: people whose speech fell outside the ordered field of intelligible, refined sound. On the evidence of the mouth itself, the "invading आर्य (ārya)" looks less like the author of संस्कृतम् (saṃskṛtam) and more like precisely the sort of untrained speaker Sanskrit tradition would have classified as म्लेच्छ (mleccha).

**Argumentative core.** *Ārya* in the Indic frame names a phonetic-pedagogical achievement (mastery of *saṃskṛtam*'s engineered sound-system), not a racial-migratory category. The retroflex set (*mūrdhanya*) is the operational marker of *āryatva*. AIT's "invading *ārya*" is incoherent on the evidence of the mouth: a population entering India from the Central Asian or European sound-fields would have lacked the retroflex articulation native to the Indian sound-field; on the tradition's own classification, such a population would have been the *mlecchas*, not the *āryas*. The reductio is internal to the Indic frame and requires no external evidence to land.

**Recommended chapter destinations.**

- **Chapter 7 (the retroflex / mūrdhanya chapter)** — *primary*. The disposition (`as_atomic_draft_disposition.md`, Section III² disposition note) flags Ch7 as needing new material on "the retroflex as the acoustic signature of the subcontinent." This sidebar supplies the polemical hammer Ch7 has been waiting for: the retroflex set is not just acoustically distinctive but is the operational marker of *āryatva*, and AIT's racial *ārya* fails the phonetic test the tradition itself would have applied. Ch7 currently has no chapter notes file; this sidebar can seed `as_1_07_adivadya_notes.md` when that file is created.
- **Chapter 2 §2.2 (the racial pillar)** — *secondary*. Ch2 §2.2 already treats AIT as the racial pillar of the orthodoxy that protects the botanical metaphor. This sidebar provides a phonetic-empirical refutation that strengthens the structural argument: AIT is not just racially motivated, it is empirically incoherent against the Indic phonetic framework. Could be added as a one-paragraph reductio inside §2.2.
- **Chapter 15 / 15 (precursor model, PIE prosecution)** — *tertiary*. The "invading *ārya* looks like a *mleccha*" reductio is a strong rhetorical move for the closing prosecutorial chapters. Ch7 develops it architecturally; Chs 14–15 can deploy it as a reductio.

**Cross-references.**

- Ch1 ¶7+¶8 establishes *saṃskṛtam* / *prākṛtāni* as the engineered/natural language pair. The *ārya* / *mleccha* phonetic distinction is a parallel pair operating at the speaker level: *ārya* names the master of *saṃskṛtam*'s sound-system; *mleccha* names the speaker outside that system.

## Sidebar 2 — The recalibrant hypothesis: expert-to-expert transmission, not population-to-population

*Captured: Session 3 (continued), May 9, 2026.*

**Author's framing (verbatim):**

> For sanskrit to have influenced languages in other parts of the world did not require invasions or migrations. it required perhaps even a single arya person from India, an expert in vyakarana, a guru, to have influenced linguists in their own respective cultures.
>
> SO the recalibrant theory doesn't need lots of people with a calibrant language to lots of people with the calibrated language. it requires the credibility of having the mastery over something that is evidently capable of improving communication and speech. it was the aryas who were doing their job of educating the mlecchas (and not in a pejorative way)

**Argumentative core.** Standard models of linguistic influence (PIE migration, AIT, Sprachbund, contact-induced-change, metatypy) assume population-level transmission — a community speaking language X migrates and brings X with them; X then influences the languages spoken in the destination. The author's refinement: Sanskrit's influence on other linguistic traditions does not require population transfer at all. A single Sanskrit-trained *ārya* — expert in *vyākaraṇam*, functioning as a guru — influencing the linguists of another culture is sufficient. The transmission unit is the expert, not the population. The mechanism is pedagogical credibility, not demographic pressure. This is structurally orthogonal to the standard frameworks.

The *āryas* / *mlecchas* framing here is non-pejorative and faithful to the technical force of the terms: *āryas* are masters of the refined phonetic-grammatical system; *mlecchas* are speakers outside it. The educational relationship is pedagogical, not hierarchical-in-the-modern-sense. *Āryas* educating *mlecchas* in *vyākaraṇam* is the standard guru-shishya structure exported across linguistic communities.

**Recommended chapter destinations.**

- **Chapter 16 (PIE in the Sky / Wave 1 / Wave 2)** — *primary*. The calibrant-language framework Ch16 develops (per `as_16_chapter_notes.md` §2.8) is fundamentally about how Sanskrit's influence reached other languages. This sidebar refines the transmission mechanism: expert-to-expert (a guru influencing the linguists of another culture), not population-to-population. The Wave 1 / Wave 2 structure can absorb this directly.
- **Chapter 15 (Failure of Precursor Model)** — *secondary*. The PIE model assumes population transfer (a Proto-Indo-European-speaking community migrating). The expert-to-expert transmission model is structurally orthogonal — it is not a precursor model at all but a pedagogical-consultancy model. Ch15 can use this sidebar to argue that the precursor model fails not just because the data doesn't fit, but because the transmission mechanism it assumes is the wrong kind of mechanism.
- **Companion paper *The Subcontinental Calibrant*** — *tertiary*. The Indian-language transmission story (Marathi, Munda, Korku, Tamil, Dravidian and Tibeto-Burman per `as_16_chapter_notes.md` §5.8) may benefit from the same expert-transmission framing applied to subcontinental rather than transcontinental linguistic influence.

**Cross-references.**

- Sidebar 1 (the *ārya*/*mleccha* phonetic axis) provides the phonetic-pedagogical reading of *āryatva* that this sidebar's transmission model assumes. The two sidebars are paired.
- Ch13 (engineered preservation) — the *guru-shishya* transmission within India is the calibration matrix. This sidebar extends the same mechanism externally: *guru-shishya* across cultures is the calibrant-transmission to the calibrated languages.
- Ch16 §2.8 (the *calibrant language* coinage per `as_16_chapter_notes.md`) — this sidebar specifies the *agent* of calibrant transmission. The calibrant language doesn't transmit itself; *āryas* expert in *vyākaraṇam* transmit it.

## Sidebar 3 — Recalibrant-traveler exemplars across two waves: pre-Pāṇini (Wave 1) and post-Pāṇini (Wave 2)

*Captured: Session 3 (continued), May 9, 2026. Revised same day per author direction to flag the waves as pre-Pāṇini / post-Pāṇini rather than inner-wave / outer-wave, and to add the other Saptaṛṣis as the first outer wave.*

**Author's direction.** The waves should be flagged as pre-Pāṇini (Wave 1) and post-Pāṇini (Wave 2). Agastya was a Vedic ṛṣi: his recalibrant transmission did not carry the formal *Aṣṭādhyāyī* — it carried the *Vedas* and the implicit (and possibly explicit) grammatical apparatus that informed Vedic recitation and preservation. The other Saptaṛṣis can serve as the first outer wave — the original carriers of Vedic-tradition knowledge across geographies.

**The wave structure refined.**

The recalibrant-traveler framework operates in two historical phases keyed to Pāṇini:

- **Wave 1 (pre-Pāṇini).** Vedic-trained experts transmit the *Vedas* and the *Vedāṅga* apparatus — the six auxiliary disciplines that include grammar (*Vyākaraṇa*), phonetics (*Śikṣā*), prosody (*Chandas*), etymology (*Nirukta*), ritual (*Kalpa*), and astronomy (*Jyotiṣa*). The grammatical apparatus is partly implicit (embedded in the recitation conventions and in the *Prātiśākhya* tradition that develops the phonetic analysis of each Vedic recension) and partly explicit (the *Vedāṅgas* themselves, the *Śikṣā* tradition, the *padapāṭha* tradition, the recitation-preservation systems). What Wave 1 transmits is not the *Aṣṭādhyāyī* — which has not yet been formalized in the Indic frame — but the apparatus from which the *Aṣṭādhyāyī* will eventually be condensed. (Sidebar 6 documents the explicit analytical layer of this pre-Pāṇinian apparatus through the Śākalya *padapāṭha* and the named earlier grammarians whom Pāṇini cites by name in the *Aṣṭādhyāyī* — converting the "possibly explicit" hedge into documentary evidence.)
- **Wave 2 (post-Pāṇini).** Sanskrit-trained experts transmit the formalized grammatical apparatus of the *Aṣṭādhyāyī* and its commentarial tradition (Kātyāyana's *Vārttikāni*, Patañjali's *Mahābhāṣya* — the *Trimuni Vyākaraṇam*). What Wave 2 transmits is the engineered language with its codified formal grammar.

Each wave has both **inner** (within the subcontinent) and **outer** (transcontinental) reach. The same structural pattern operates in both phases — a single expert recalibrating a new linguistic-cultural area through pedagogical transmission — but the apparatus carried differs by phase.

This distinction matters for one specific reason. When Indic tradition records pre-Pāṇinian transmission events, critics sometimes object that the *Aṣṭādhyāyī* didn't yet exist. The objection misses the structural point. Wave 1 doesn't transmit the *Aṣṭādhyāyī*; it transmits the apparatus from which the *Aṣṭādhyāyī* will eventually be condensed. The recalibrant-transmission framework is the same pattern at two historical layers, not a single pattern that requires the *Aṣṭādhyāyī* to be already formalized.

### Wave 1 inner: Agastya अगस्त्य

Agastya is the canonical Wave 1 inner figure. He is one of the *Saptaṛṣis* on the Jaiminīya Brāhmaṇa list (the earliest formal enumeration of the seven sages) and a co-author with Lopāmudrā of Rigvedic hymns 1.165–1.191. He travels south, crosses the Vindhyas, and establishes a Vedic-tradition foothold in the Tamil country. The Velvikkudi and Chinnamanoor inscriptions record him as priest, Tamil teacher, and coronation-performer for the Pandya dynasty. Tamil tradition credits him with composing the *Agattiyam* (अगत्तियम्), the first Tamil grammar — non-extant today, but referenced in fragments by medieval commentators.

Agastya's recalibrant transmission was Vedic, not post-Pāṇinian. He carried what a Vedic ṛṣi carried: the *Vedas*, the *Vedāṅga* apparatus, the implicit grammatical machinery embedded in Vedic recitation, and the explicit phonetic-grammatical analysis of the *Prātiśākhya* and *Śikṣā* traditions that served the Vedic preservation system. When he composed the *Agattiyam*, he was applying this pre-Pāṇinian apparatus to the analysis of a southern subcontinental language outside the Sanskritic register. The structural move is the same as Wave 2 (a single expert recalibrating a new linguistic area through pedagogical transmission); the apparatus carried is different (Vedic + *Vedāṅga*, not *Aṣṭādhyāyī* + *Trimuni*).

Both northern and southern traditions agree on Agastya's north-to-south travel and on his teaching role. Northern legends emphasize his role in spreading Vedic tradition and Sanskrit; southern traditions emphasize his role in spreading agriculture, irrigation, and Tamil grammar. The recalibrant transmission did not require erasure of the destination tradition — it required the destination tradition to absorb a new analytical apparatus carried by an expert. Tamil tradition records the absorption gracefully: Agastya is "the father of the Tamil language," not its replacer.

### Wave 1 outer: the other Saptaṛṣis and the Mitanni evidence

The Saptaṛṣis are the *gotra*-originating ṛṣis whose lineages structure the entire Vedic-tradition transmission system. The exact list varies across traditions — the Jaiminīya Brāhmaṇa names Agastya, Atri, Bharadvāja, Gautama, Jamadagni, Vasiṣṭha, Viśvāmitra; the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad substitutes Kaśyapa for Agastya; the Mahābhārata's *Śānti Parva* names a different set entirely (Marīci, Atri, Aṅgiras, Pulastya, Pulaha, Kratu, Vasiṣṭha) as *Citra-śikhaṇḍins*. The constellation list (the seven stars of the Big Dipper) is a third set: Marīci, Atri, Aṅgiras, Pulastya, Pulaha, Kratu, Vasiṣṭha, with Arundhatī (Vasiṣṭha's wife) as Alcor. For the recalibrant-traveler framework, what matters is not which list is canonical but that the Saptaṛṣi tradition names a roster of pre-Pāṇinian Vedic experts whose lineages extend geographically.

Several Saptaṛṣis have geographic-extension narratives that fit the Wave 1 outer pattern at transcontinental scale:

- **Kaśyapa कश्यप** — the strongest named candidate for Wave 1 outer. Kashmir is etymologized in Indic tradition as *Kāśyapa-mīra*, "the lake of Kaśyapa." The *Nīlamatapurāṇa* tradition centers his role in the region's establishment. The Kāśyapa lineage is one of the largest *gotras*, with reach across Kashmir and into the Central Asian frontier. Kashmir was historically the major transmission node toward Central Asia, the Iranian plateau, and the Silk Road corridor. Kaśyapa is also the principal hymn-composer of Rigvedic Maṇḍala IX — the *Soma Pavamāna* hymns. The combination is structurally suggestive: a Vedic ṛṣi whose lineage is associated with the northwestern frontier and whose hymn-corpus structures one of the foundational Vedic books.
- **Bharadvāja भरद्वाज** — explicitly described in tradition as scholar, **grammarian**, economist, and physician, in addition to ṛṣi. Composer of the sixth Maṇḍala of the Rigveda. His lineage extends through Droṇācārya into the *Mahābhārata* narrative. The "grammarian" attribution is direct: Bharadvāja is recorded as a teacher of the grammatical apparatus that pre-Pāṇinian tradition transmitted. As a Wave 1 figure, Bharadvāja is precisely the Vedic-grammarian-traveler the recalibrant framework predicts.
- **Bhṛgu भृगु and the Bhārgava lineage** — extensive geographic reach. Paraśurāma (in the Bhārgava lineage) has the western-coast creation tradition (Konkan / Kerala — Wave 1 inner, west-coast). The wider Bhārgava lineage has speculative connections to the Iranian zone — some scholars have proposed parallels between Bhṛgu and Avestan lineage names, though these parallels are contested. The Bhārgava lineage's geographic spread within and beyond the subcontinent makes Bhṛgu a possible Wave 1 outer candidate, with the contested-parallel caveat.
- **Aṅgiras अङ्गिरस्** — vast lineage; the Atharvavedic tradition is associated with him. Some scholars have proposed Avestan-Iranian etymological parallels (Aṅgiras ↔ Avestan *aṅra*), again contested. The Āṅgirasa lineage is one of the foundational *gotra*-lineages; its geographic reach extends across the Vedic-tradition transmission network.
- **Pulastya पुलस्त्य** — son Viśravas, grandson Kubera, great-grandson Rāvaṇa. The Pulastya lineage extends to Lanka via Rāvaṇa. Lanka is geographically subcontinental but culturally distinct in the tradition; Pulastya's reach is inner-edge or outer-edge depending on framing.
- **Atri अत्रि** — composer of the fifth Maṇḍala of the Rigveda; some traditions associate him with southern travel alongside Agastya. Less specific outer-wave attribution but a major lineage figure.

**The Mitanni evidence as the historical-empirical anchor.** The strongest non-mythological documentation of pre-Pāṇinian Vedic-tradition transmission outside the subcontinent is the Mitanni record from northern Mesopotamia:

- The Hittite-Mitanni treaty (between Suppiluliuma I and Shattiwaza, dated through external Hittite chronology) — preserved in the Bogazköy archive — invokes *Mitra*, *Varuṇa*, *Indra*, and *Nāsatya* (the Aśvins) as treaty-witness deities. Four Vedic gods named explicitly in a non-Indic diplomatic document, in Sanskritic form (Mitanni Sanskritic *Aruna* for *Varuṇa*, etc.).
- The Kikkuli horse-training treatise — a 184-day, 1080-line manual on four cuneiform tablets — uses Sanskritic numerical terms: *aika* (cf. Sanskrit *eka*, one), *tera* (*tri*, three), *panza* (*pañca*, five), *satta* (*sapta*, seven), *na* (*nava*, nine), *vartana* (turn). The form *aika* is structurally pre-Vedic-Sanskritic (Vedic Sanskrit has *eka*, with the contraction *ai* > *e*), placing the Mitanni Sanskritic layer in a phonologically pre-Vedic-Sanskritic position.
- Mitanni rulers bore Sanskritic throne names: Tushratta = *Tveṣaratha*; Shattiwaza = *Sātivāja*; Indaruda = *Indrota*; Artashumara = *Artasmara*. The Mitanni warriors were called *marya* — the Sanskrit term for "(young) warrior."

The Mitanni Sanskritic layer is widely accepted in the philological literature as evidence of pre-Vedic-Sanskritic linguistic transmission to northern Mesopotamia. For the recalibrant-traveler framework, the Mitanni evidence is structural confirmation: pre-Pāṇinian Vedic-tradition apparatus reached non-Indic linguistic-cultural areas, presumably through Vedic-trained experts who served the Mitanni court (the precise transmission agents are not named in the Mitanni record, but the linguistic artifacts they produced — treaty oath, horse manual, throne names — require their presence). The Saptaṛṣi tradition supplies the structural roster of pre-Pāṇinian Vedic experts; the Mitanni evidence supplies the empirical confirmation that such experts and their lineages reached transcontinental destinations.

### Wave 2 inner: post-Pāṇinian recalibrants within the subcontinent

Once the *Aṣṭādhyāyī* is formalized and the *Mahābhāṣya* is canonical, the recalibrant-traveler framework gets a second-phase apparatus to transmit. Post-Pāṇinian Wave 2 inner figures include:

- **Hemacandra हेमचन्द्र** — Jain polymath of the western flowering; composed grammars in Sanskrit, Prakrit, and Apabhraṃśa, treating each through the analytical apparatus of *vyākaraṇam*. The *Siddha-Hema-Śabdānuśāsana* covers multiple Indic languages of the Gujarat-Rajasthan region in a single *vyākaraṇam* idiom.
- **Vararuci वररुचि** (also identified with one of the Kātyāyanas in tradition) — author of the *Prākṛta-prakāśa* (प्राकृतप्रकाश), the foundational grammar of the Prakrits. Brought Sanskrit grammatical apparatus to the systematic analysis of Prakrit speech-systems.
- **Tolkāppiyar தொல்காப்பியர்** — author of the *Tolkāppiyam*, the earliest extant Tamil grammar. Tradition makes him a student of Agastya (which would place him at the boundary of Wave 1 and Wave 2). The *Tolkāppiyam* shows Sanskrit-style analytical apparatus — the *eḻuttu* / *col* / *poruḷ* trichotomy parallels Sanskrit's *varṇa* / *pada* / *artha*. His placement in Wave 1 vs Wave 2 depends on dating-and-sequencing questions the chronology rule keeps open; structurally, the *Tolkāppiyam* shows post-Pāṇinian-influenced apparatus.
- **Adi Shankaracharya आदि शङ्कराचार्य** — established four mathas at the corners of the subcontinent (Sringeri, Dvaraka, Puri, Jyotirmath); a continent-wide network of Sanskrit-trained Vedanta. Theological-analytical recalibration in the post-Pāṇinian frame.
- **Various Buddhist and Jain missionary scholars within India** — Mahinda (carrying Pali Buddhist learning to Sri Lanka via India), and the network of scholars between Nalanda, Vikramashila, Odantapurī, and the southern monastic centers.

### Wave 2 outer: post-Pāṇinian recalibrants outside the subcontinent

Same Wave 2 apparatus (post-Pāṇinian *Aṣṭādhyāyī* + *Trimuni Vyākaraṇam*) carried transcontinentally:

- **Kumārajīva कुमारजीव** — the Kashmiri-Kuchean scholar who became the major Sanskrit-to-Chinese translator. Created the enduring Chinese Buddhist vocabulary that subsequent Chinese Buddhist tradition used as standard. A single expert recalibrant whose work shaped the linguistic register of Chinese Buddhism for over a millennium.
- **Padmasambhava पद्मसम्भव** (Guru Rinpoche) — recalibrant figure for Tibet; brought Vajrayana Buddhism; the Tibetan Buddhist linguistic register absorbs the Sanskrit-Buddhist apparatus through his transmission lineage.
- **Atiśa Dīpaṅkara Śrījñāna अतीश दीपङ्कर श्रीज्ञान** — went from Vikramashila to Tibet as the later-period recalibrant; reformed and re-systematized what Padmasambhava had begun. The pair (Padmasambhava, then Atiśa) is itself the Wave 1 / Wave 2 pattern applied within the Tibetan destination — Padmasambhava establishing the initial apparatus, Atiśa reforming and consolidating it.
- **Śāntarakṣita शान्तरक्षित** — Indian Buddhist scholar who went to Tibet; helped establish Samye monastery; debated the Bon tradition. The recalibrant work is institutional and pedagogical.
- **Bodhidharma बोधिधर्म** — traditionally Kanchipuram-born; carried Indic Buddhist meditative tradition to China; the founding figure of Chan (later Zen). The recalibrant work is meditative-pedagogical rather than narrowly grammatical, but the structural pattern is the same.

### The pattern across the waves

Same structural move at both historical phases. A single expert, trained in the apparatus of their phase (Wave 1: *Vedas* + *Vedāṅga*; Wave 2: *Aṣṭādhyāyī* + *Trimuni*), travels to a linguistic-cultural area outside their training tradition, and recalibrates the local intellectual system through teaching. No population transfer at either phase. The transmission unit is the expert; the credibility is pedagogical mastery; the result is the calibrated language or tradition.

## Sidebar 6 — The Śākalya *padapāṭha* and Pāṇini's pre-Pāṇinian sources: documented explicit grammatical analysis older than the *Aṣṭādhyāyī*

*Captured: Session 3 (continued), May 9, 2026.*

**Author's framing (verbatim):**

> Just as later grammarians analyzed the Gītā's vocabulary back to its 480+ verbal roots, the Ṛgveda had already undergone a much earlier layer of grammatical analysis in the padapāṭha, especially the Śākalya padapāṭha, which separates the continuous saṃhitā text into its constituent padas. This analytical tradition is demonstrably pre-Pāṇinian or at least already authoritative by Pāṇini's time, since Pāṇini himself cites Śākalya in the Aṣṭādhyāyī.

**Argumentative core.**

Three structural moves:

1. **The *padapāṭha* is itself a grammatical-analytical project, not just a recitation aid.** The *saṃhitā-pāṭha* (continuous-text recitation) preserves the Rigveda in its sandhi-joined sound; the *pada-pāṭha* (word-by-word recitation) decomposes the same text into its constituent *padas* (word-units). The decomposition is non-trivial. It requires the analyst to know where compound boundaries fall, where sandhi has joined separate words, where morphological complexity hides structural simplicity. The Śākalya *padapāṭha* — credited in tradition to **Vidagdha Śākalya** विदग्ध शाकल्य (son of the sage Sakala, student of the teacher Satyasri, of Paila's scholarly lineage) — is the canonical version preserved by the tradition. It separates the continuous *saṃhitā* into individual words and identifies elements within compound expressions. This is documented analytical work, not just transmission.
2. **Pāṇini cites Śākalya by name in the *Aṣṭādhyāyī*.** Multiple rules invoke Śākalya as authority — among them 1.1.16 (*sambuddhau śākalyasyetāv anārṣe*), 6.1.127 (the *iko 'savarṇe* context), 8.3.18 (*vyor laghuprayatnataraḥ śākalyasya*), and 8.4.51 (*sarvatra śākalyasya*), with several other references across the text. The citations treat Śākalya's analytical decisions as authoritative input to Pāṇini's own rule-making — sometimes adopted, sometimes overruled, sometimes preserved as alternatives. The *Prātiśākhya* tradition independently cites Śākalya as well. By the time the *Aṣṭādhyāyī* was composed, Śākalya's grammatical-analytical work was already settled tradition.
3. **The Wave 1 apparatus included documented explicit grammatical analysis with named authoritative practitioners.** Sidebar 3's hedged claim about Wave 1 carrying "the implicit (*and possibly explicit*) grammatical apparatus" can now be unhedged. The pre-Pāṇinian apparatus included not just recitation conventions and the *Prātiśākhya* tradition's phonetic analysis. It included the *padapāṭha* tradition's word-by-word grammatical decomposition of the foundational text, with named authoritative practitioners. Wave 1 transmitted analytical work, not just recitation.

**The named pre-Pāṇinian grammarians.**

Śākalya is one of approximately ten named earlier grammarians whom Pāṇini cites in the *Aṣṭādhyāyī*. The standard list:

- **Āpiśali** आपिशलि
- **Kāśyapa** काश्यप

- **Gālava** गालव
- **Cākravarmaṇa** चाक्रवर्मण

- **Saunaga** सौनाग
- **Senaka** सेनक
- **Sphoṭāyana** स्फोटायन
- **Śākalya** शाकल्य

Different *Aṣṭādhyāyī* rules take different positions on each predecessor's analytical decisions. The pattern itself is structurally significant: Pāṇini does not present the *Aṣṭādhyāyī* as sui generis revelation. He presents it as a refined analytical apparatus that engages with prior named authorities and adjudicates among their decisions. *Bhāradvāja* on this list is the same Bharadvāja flagged in Sidebar 3's Wave 1 outer roster as the explicitly-grammarian Saptaṛṣi figure — the connection between the Saptaṛṣi tradition and the named pre-Pāṇinian grammarian tradition is direct.
