## Purpose

The guide should introduce more than the book's central argument. Each chapter contains smaller discoveries that may attract a reader who would not otherwise open a technical or historical chapter. Oṃ is one example, not the only subject that deserves this treatment.

Each candidate below gives the reader a concrete reason to continue: a familiar word, a sound to try, a story, a surprising comparison, or a problem the chapter examines. The accompanying explanation is editorial guidance, not additional booklet copy.

These are destinations in the book's argument, not newly verified historical conclusions. In particular, the author's reconstruction of Sanskrit's origin remains identified as speculation, and the radiance prompts lead into his case rather than claiming that this guide has independently established every proposed transmission route.

## How to Use the Bank

- Give most booklet topics one selected prompt; add a second only when it opens a genuinely different subject.
- Put the chapter and section immediately after the prompt. Use section numbers rather than book page numbers, which vary by edition.
- Mix questions with statements. Avoid turning every ending into the same rhetorical pattern.
- Prefer an example omitted from the summary over a question that simply repeats the paragraph above it.
- Use Devanagari alongside IAST for Sanskrit examples. Explain unfamiliar terms within the sentence rather than making readers consult the glossary first.
- Do not introduce unexplained vocabulary such as “semantic,” “morphology,” or “phonotactics” in a prompt.
- Where a subject is essential to understanding the guide, explain it in the body. A reading pointer cannot replace that explanation.
- Use the full bank across both parts, not as 101 page footers. Prompts that crowd an explanation belong in the exploration guide.
- Let the page count grow when an explanation needs space. Do not shrink type or compress prose to retain the first draft's 24 pages.

Suggested printed treatment:

> **Explore in the book**
>
> Why does Sanskrit have no short ए (*e*) or ओ (*o*)? Chapter 9 §9.7.

For a statement:

> **Explore in the book**
>
> Counting short and long syllables produces the sequence 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13. Chapter 14 §14.4 shows how.

## Subjects That May Need More Than a Footer

1. **Oṃ as a sounded example.** Add a short passage near the speaking-body explanation. Let the reader follow the sound physically before pointing to Chapter 10's account of the fourth. Do not put the entire Upaniṣadic discussion into a footer.
2. **The Vedic ending that completes the meter.** Show a compact pair or retain enough explanation of रुद्रैः (*rudraiḥ*) and रुद्रेभिः (*rudrebhiḥ*) to make the two-domain distinction tangible. Direct the reader to Chapter 16 for the full counterexample.
3. **A practice of calibrant order.** दर्शन (*darśana*) or भिक्षा (*bhikṣā*) can give the inside-out conclusion a human setting. Choose one for the body; point to the other. Do not allow the social argument to survive only as a list of future-volume promises.
4. **The page changing the tongue.** One sentence about “often” or “fault” may explain the risk more clearly than a general statement about writing. Keep the remaining examples as a Chapter 13 pointer.

The other candidates can remain invitations unless a later draft reveals a gap in the explanation. A prompt is valuable because it offers a further discovery, not because every topic must be summarized twice.
